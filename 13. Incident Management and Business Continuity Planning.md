# Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Incident management and business continuity planning (BCP) are the mechanisms by which an organisation detects, responds to, and recovers from disruptive events — whether a ransomware attack, a data centre outage, a supply chain failure, or a natural disaster. Together they span the full disruption lifecycle: preparing before events occur, responding during them, and restoring and improving afterward.

These disciplines are related but structurally distinct:

- **Incident management** governs the start-to-finish handling of a specific event — detection, triage, containment, eradication, investigation, and documentation.
- **Incident response** is the operational subset of incident management: the actions taken once an incident is declared.
- **Business continuity planning (BCP)** is the broader organisational programme that maintains critical business functions during and after any type of disruption. Cyber incidents are one cause; so are pandemics, natural disasters, and utility failures.
- **Disaster recovery planning (DRP)** sits within BCP as the IT-focused subset: restoring systems, data, and infrastructure to operational state.
- **Crisis management** is the strategic-level discipline — executive and board-level decisions, communication, and leadership when an event threatens the organisation's strategic position, reputation, or survival. It sits above incident response and BCP, not inside them.

In the broader security landscape, these capabilities sit at the intersection of risk management, security operations, governance, and legal/regulatory obligations. Without them, all other security investment is undermined: controls that fail to recover from incidents leave an organisation unable to demonstrate the resilience that regulators, customers, and boards increasingly demand.

---

## Why It Matters

Poor incident management and continuity planning produce failures that are measurable, public, and increasingly the subject of regulatory enforcement.

**Ransomware and double extortion** — attackers exfiltrate data before encrypting it, so a slow or absent response simultaneously creates an operational outage and a notifiable data breach. The 2021 Colonial Pipeline incident demonstrated that inadequate incident response planning can cause cascading economic and public safety consequences well beyond the directly affected organisation. The 2022 Medibank and Optus breaches in Australia triggered regulatory investigations, class actions, and legislative reform that continues to reshape Australian compliance obligations.

Specific failure modes include:

- **No declared incident:** Teams misclassify events or defer decisions without clear escalation criteria, delaying containment while attackers persist and spread laterally.
- **Plans that have never been tested:** BCP and IRP documents written for audit purposes that bear no relation to actual organisational capability.
- **RTOs and RPOs set without business input:** Recovery time objectives technically achievable by IT but that do not reflect what the business actually needs — or business demands that are impossible with current backup architectures.
- **No communication plan:** Incidents that escalate into reputational disasters because internal and external communication is improvised, contradictory, or untimely.
- **Evidence destroyed during response:** First responders rebooting or wiping systems before preserving volatile evidence, making forensic investigation and legal proceedings impossible.
- **Regulatory notification missed:** Mandatory reporting deadlines breached because obligations were not built into the IRP before the incident occurred.

## Core Concepts

**Event vs. Incident**
Not everything that triggers an alert is an incident. An event is any observable occurrence on a system or network — including routine logins. An incident is an event assessed as having, or likely having, an adverse effect on confidentiality, integrity, or availability. Mislabelling wastes resources and desensitises responders; failure to declare an incident delays the containment clock and activates notification obligations.

**Incident Management vs. Incident Response**
Incident management is the overarching programme — governance, policy, team structure, escalation paths, communication, and continuous improvement. Incident response is the operational subset activated once an incident is declared. Both are necessary: tactics without governance produce inconsistent response; governance without operational capability is performative.

**Crisis vs. Incident**
A crisis is an event that exceeds normal operational procedures and threatens the strategic position, reputation, or survival of the organisation — requiring board- and executive-level decision-making. An incident is operationally managed. Many significant cyber events begin as incidents and escalate into crises when financial, regulatory, or reputational thresholds are crossed. The escalation path must be pre-defined, with clear triggers for moving from incident management into crisis management.

**The BIA as the Analytical Foundation**
The Business Impact Analysis (BIA) identifies which business functions are critical, what the financial and operational consequence of their loss is over time, and what recovery resources those functions require. BIA outputs drive BCP strategy — RTOs, RPOs, recovery site decisions, and resource allocation cannot be meaningfully set without them. A BIA is not the same as a risk assessment: it quantifies impact from disruption regardless of cause; a risk assessment evaluates threat likelihood and cause. Both are necessary and complementary.

**Recovery Objectives**
These are business commitments, not technical targets. All must be set by the business, not by IT in isolation:

```
                      DISASTER
                          |
[Last Backup]<----RPO---->|<-----------RTO----------->|<--MTD-->|
                                                       ^
                                               Business Resumption
                                               (at SDO level)

RPO   = maximum data loss tolerable (drives backup frequency)
RTO   = maximum time systems can be unavailable (drives recovery technology)
MTD   = absolute outer time limit; RTO must always be less than MTD
SDO   = minimum service level at recovery site before full restoration
AIW   = maximum interruption before financial survival is threatened
RCO   = maximum acceptable data inconsistency across distributed systems (%)
RCapO = minimum processing capacity at recovery site as % of normal
```

**Digital Forensics and Forensic Readiness**
Digital forensics is the scientific identification, collection, examination, and analysis of data from systems in a manner that preserves integrity and supports legal proceedings. Forensic readiness is the preparation discipline: having the logging, tooling, procedures, and legal authorities in place before an incident occurs so that evidence can be collected properly when needed. Organisations without forensic readiness routinely destroy usable evidence during initial response.

The order of volatility governs evidence collection sequence — most volatile data must be collected first:

```
Most volatile (collect first)
  1. CPU registers, cache
  2. RAM, running processes, open network connections, encryption keys
  3. Swap space, temporary files
  4. Hard disk / SSD data (modifiable)
  5. Remote logging and monitoring data
  6. Physical configuration, network topology
  7. Archival media (cold backups, tapes)
Least volatile (collect last)
```

Rebooting or powering down a system before RAM capture destroys encryption keys, active C2 connections, running attacker tooling, and process lists held only in memory.

**Chain of Custody**
Chain of custody is the documented record of every person who has handled evidence — what was collected, when, by whom, where stored, and who accessed it. For digital evidence, integrity is established via cryptographic hashing (SHA-256 preferred): the hash of a forensic image must match the hash of the original at every verification point. Never conduct analysis on the original media; always work from verified forensic copies. Under the Australian Government Investigations Standards (AGIS) 2022, government entities must conduct security incident investigations in accordance with documented procedures that preserve admissibility, apply principles of procedural fairness, and support potential criminal referral.

**BCP vs. DRP**
BCP is proactive and continuous — it encompasses prevention, mitigation, and recovery across all dimensions of the business. DRP is reactive and IT-specific — it activates upon an invocation decision and focuses on restoring systems and data. The DRP is a component of the BCP, not its equivalent. Both sit within the broader Business Continuity Management System (BCMS).

## Australian Compliance Requirements

This section consolidates the specific Australian legislative and regulatory obligations that incident management and BCP programmes must satisfy. Compliance is not optional in the Australian context — most obligations carry civil penalties, and regulatory enforcement is active.

### Protective Security Policy Framework (PSPF Release 2025)

The PSPF is the mandatory security framework for all non-corporate Commonwealth entities (NCEs) — covering most Australian Government departments and agencies — and extends in practice to contractors and service providers handling Commonwealth data. PSPF Release 2026 took effect 1 July 2026 and is now the current release; Release 2025 was published 24 July 2025 and superseded Release 2024. Verify current requirements against the annual release page. The PSPF is now structured across six security domains: governance, risk, information, technology, personnel, and physical.

**Who is accountable under the PSPF:**

- The **Accountable Authority** (Secretary or equivalent) is responsible for overall protective security and must approve the entity's annual protective security report.
- The **Chief Security Officer (CSO)**, at Senior Executive Service level, is responsible for investigating, responding to, and reporting on security incidents, and for maintaining procedures in accordance with PSPF and any other regulatory requirements.
- The **Chief Information Security Officer (CISO)**, formally required under PSPF 2025 (Requirement 0011), supports the CSO for cyber security incidents, is accountable to the Accountable Authority for cyber security risks (Requirement 0013), and must report on cyber security risk at each Audit Committee meeting and biannually on progress of the cyber security strategy and uplift plan.

**Incident management requirements (Section 3.6 of PSPF 2025):**

The PSPF defines a **significant security incident** as any:

- action (deliberate, reckless, negligent, or accidental) that fails to meet protective security requirements and results in, or may result in, loss, damage, corruption, or disclosure of official information or resources
- attempt to gain unauthorised access to official information or resources
- approach from anyone seeking unauthorised access to official resources
- event that harms, or may harm, the security of Australian Government people, information, or resources

**Mandatory incident reporting obligations (Section 3.6.3 of PSPF 2025):**
Significant or externally reportable security incidents must be reported to the relevant authority. This includes:

- Significant cyber security incidents reported to **ASD** (PSPF incident reporting requirement, Section 3.6.3)
- Significant security incidents reported to the **Department of Home Affairs** as they arise, and summarised in the annual protective security report
- Incidents involving national security threats reported to **ASIO** (per the ASIO Act 1979)
- Incidents involving foreign government information reported to the originating entity and the relevant agreement authority
- Lessons learned from any incidents, investigations, reports, or reviews included in the annual protective security report (PSPF 2025, Table 2 addition)

**Investigation obligations (Section 3.6.2):**
Procedures must be developed, implemented, and maintained to investigate security incidents in accordance with the principles of the Australian Government Investigations Standards (AGIS) 2022. The principles of procedural fairness must be applied to all security investigations, with due regard to national security considerations.

**Business continuity requirements (Risk domain, PSPF 2025):**
Every NCE must develop, implement, and maintain a BCP that:

- minimises the impacts of significant business disruptions to critical services and assets
- lays out actions before, during, and after an incident to minimise damage and reduce recovery time
- documents procedures through which the entity can continue or recover its services to government
- details post-incident actions to limit loss or damage
- includes provisions for reduced service levels during disruption and for resuming normal operations within an acceptable period

PSPF Release 2024/2025 explicitly recommends ISO 22301:2019, ISO 22313:2020, ISO/TS 22318:2021, and ASD's Business Continuity in a Box as reference standards for BCP development.

**Technical cyber security requirements with incident/BCP relevance (PSPF 2025):**

- Essential Eight mitigation strategies implemented to **Maturity Level 2** (mandatory for NCEs since July 2022). The Regular Backups strategy is directly critical to BCP and recovery capability.
- A **cyber security strategy and uplift plan** must be developed, implemented, and maintained in accordance with the ISM and the Guiding Principles to Embed a Zero Trust Culture (the PSPF cyber security strategy and uplift plan requirement). <!-- TODO: verify requirement number against current PSPF list of requirements -->
- Participation in **ASD's Cyber Security Partnership Program** is mandatory for NCEs (PSPF Direction 003-2024, July 2024).
- NCEs using threat intelligence sharing platforms must share cyber threat information with ASD (PSPF Direction 003-2024).
- Contractual terms with service providers must require them to **report any actual or suspected security incidents** to the entity, and to follow reasonable direction from the entity during incident investigations.
- Annual **Cyber Security Survey** must be submitted to ASD.

**Annual reporting:**
The annual protective security report must be provided to the entity's Minister and submitted to the Department of Home Affairs. The Accountable Authority must approve and attest to the report. Entities must cooperate with the Department of Home Affairs' assurance activities.

### Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 (SOCI Act, as amended)

The SOCI Act applies to operators of critical infrastructure assets across 22 asset classes in 11 sectors (energy, water, communications, healthcare, financial services, food and grocery, education, space technology, transport, defence industry, and data storage and processing). The Enhanced Response and Prevention (ERP) Act 2024 amended the SOCI Act; most provisions took effect December 2024.

**Mandatory cyber incident reporting (Positive Security Obligation — all applicable assets):**

- **Critical (significant impact) incidents:** Notify the ACSC within **12 hours** of becoming aware of an incident having a significant impact on the critical asset's ability to deliver services.
- **Relevant-impact incidents:** Notify the ACSC within **72 hours** of becoming aware of an incident that has a relevant impact on the critical asset's availability, reliability, or integrity.
- Notification can be made verbally (1300 CYBER1) or in writing via the ACSC's online reporting portal.

**Critical Infrastructure Risk Management Program (CIRMP):**
Responsible entities must adopt, maintain, and comply with a written CIRMP that addresses hazards including cybersecurity, supply chain, personnel, and physical security. Annual CIRMP reports must be submitted and board-signed, with the first reports for the 2023-24 year due by 28 September 2024. The five designated frameworks for CIRMP are: ASD's Essential Eight, NIST CSF, ISO 27001, the Australian Energy Sector Cyber Security Framework (AESCSF), and C2M2, plus an equivalent-framework pathway.

**Systems of National Significance (SoNS) — Enhanced Cyber Security Obligations (ECSOs):**
Assets designated as SoNS by the Minister for Home Affairs must also:

- develop and maintain a **cyber security incident response plan** (formally required, must be exercised)
- undertake periodic **vulnerability assessments**
- provide the Australian Signals Directorate with **system information access** capability upon request
- participate in **cyber security exercises** directed by the Minister

**ERP Act 2024 extensions:**

- Government assistance powers extended from cyber-only to **all types of incidents** (including terrorist attacks, natural disasters, and physical disruptions) — reflecting the SOCI Act's all-hazards approach.
- **Data storage systems** holding business-critical data for a primary critical infrastructure asset now fall within SOCI Act scope (effective April 2025).
- Telecommunications CIRMP obligations under a new Part 2D (replacing old Telecommunications Act provisions) commenced April 2025.
- Mandatory **ransomware payment reporting rules** under the Cyber Security Act 2024 commenced for all SOCI entities in May 2025 (entities with turnover > AUD $3 million).

### Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) — Notifiable Data Breaches Scheme

An **eligible data breach** occurs when: there is unauthorised access to, or disclosure of, personal information held by the entity; a reasonable person would conclude this is likely to result in serious harm to affected individuals; and the entity has not been able to prevent the likely serious harm.

Entities must:

- assess a suspected eligible breach within **30 days** of becoming aware.
- notify the **OAIC** and affected individuals **as soon as practicable** once an eligible breach is identified.
- contain the breach and assess it without unreasonable delay.

The Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 introduced a new tiered civil penalty regime, giving the OAIC a more flexible enforcement toolkit. The Federal Court decision in Australian Information Commissioner v Australian Clinical Labs Ltd (2025, medium-neutral citation unverified — confirm the [2025] FCA citation number before relying on it) established that "reasonable steps" to protect personal information are assessed in light of the organisation's actual incident response capability and the adequacy of its response plan.

### Cyber Security Act 2024 (Cth)

- Ransomware payment reporting: Entities with annual turnover exceeding **AUD $3 million**, and SOCI responsible entities, that make a ransom payment must report to **ASD via ReportCyber** within **72 hours of payment**.
- The report must include: ransomware/malware variant, vulnerabilities exploited, amount demanded and paid, payment method, and information to assist government response.
- **Before any payment**, the **Australian Sanctions Office Consolidated List** must be checked. Paying a sanctioned entity is illegal regardless of whether the payer was aware of the sanctions status.
- The Act establishes the legislative framework for the **National Cyber Security Coordinator (NCSC)** to oversee a whole-of-government response to significant cyber security incidents.

### APRA CPS 234 / CPG 234

Applies to all APRA-regulated entities (ADIs, general insurers, life insurers, private health insurers, superannuation funds, and non-operating holding companies).

- Must notify APRA of **material cyber security incidents** within **72 hours** of becoming aware.
- Must notify APRA of **material information security control weaknesses** that cannot be remediated within expected timeframes within **10 business days** of becoming aware.
- Information security capabilities must be maintained commensurate with the size and extent of threats to information assets.
- Annual testing of incident response plans is expected.
- Obligations extend to material service providers: APRA-regulated entities must ensure third parties maintain information security commensurate with the risks they pose.

## How It Works in Practice

### Incident Management

**Team structure** varies by size. Small organisations use a shared Incident Response Team (IRT). Larger organisations operate a Central IRT (policy, standards, coordination) with Distributed IRTs per geography or business unit, and may outsource specialist functions including digital forensics and crisis communications. In Australian Government entities under the PSPF, the CSO leads incident management with the CISO supporting for cyber security incidents.

**Typical roles:**

- Incident Manager / Incident Commander — overall coordination and decision authority
- Technical Lead / Incident Handler — analysis, containment, eradication
- Legal Counsel — evidence handling, regulatory obligations, law enforcement engagement
- Communications Lead — internal messaging and external messaging to customers, media, and regulators
- HR Representative — involved when the incident involves employee conduct or insider threat
- Executive Sponsor — authorises major containment actions, approves public statements
- Business Continuity Coordinator — activates BCP/DRP if service degradation warrants it

**The response lifecycle:**

```
[Prepare] --> [Identify] --> [Contain] --> [Eradicate] --> [Recover] --> [Lessons Learned]
     ^                                                                          |
     |_________________________ Continuous Improvement ________________________|
```

- **Prepare:** Policies, playbooks, team training, communication trees, regulatory notification templates, forensic readiness, out-of-band communication channels, pre-agreed escalation authority, tabletop exercises. Under PSPF 2025, participation in ASD's Cyber Security Partnership Program is now mandatory for NCEs.
- **Identify:** Verify whether an event qualifies as an incident. Assign ownership. Establish chain of custody for potential evidence. Determine severity and escalate. Under PSPF, assess whether the event meets the definition of a significant security incident requiring external reporting.
- **Contain:** Limit exposure. Isolate affected systems. Preserve volatile evidence in order of volatility before taking containment actions that would destroy it. Activate the communication plan. Trigger BCP/DRP if warranted. Initiate the regulatory notification register.
- **Eradicate:** Determine root cause. Remove it. Confirm no lateral spread. Do not move to recovery until eradication is confirmed.
- **Recover:** Restore systems to the SDO. Validate restoration. Confirm all regulatory notification obligations have been met. Declare normal operations.
- **Lessons Learned:** Post-incident report. Root cause analysis. Improvement plan. Under PSPF 2025, lessons learned from incidents must be incorporated in the annual protective security report.

**Forensic readiness** means establishing before any incident: centralised logging with sufficient retention, write-blocking capability, forensic imaging tooling (SHA-256 hash verification), chain of custody forms, pre-agreed legal authority to collect evidence (including from cloud environments). For Australian Government entities, forensic investigation procedures must align with AGIS 2022, including evidence collection to a standard of proof appropriate to the likely proceedings (administrative, civil, or criminal).

**Evidence handling for digital evidence:**

- Collect most volatile data first (RAM before disk)
- Create verified forensic images; never work from originals
- Verify integrity via SHA-256 at collection and at every subsequent examination
- Maintain unbroken chain of custody — document every handoff with time, person, and purpose
- Store evidence in tamper-evident, access-controlled media
- Engage legal counsel on law enforcement engagement early

**Out-of-band communication** is a non-negotiable preparation requirement. During ransomware, primary collaboration systems are often unavailable, encrypted, or monitored by the attacker. Incident response teams must have pre-established alternative channels accessible without the affected infrastructure. Contact lists must be stored and accessible offline.

**Triage and classification:**

```
                | Low Impact | Med Impact | High Impact |
  High Urgency  |     3      |     2      |      1      |
  Med Urgency   |     4      |     3      |      2      |
  Low Urgency   |     5      |     4      |      3      |

  Priority 1 = immediate response; Priority 5 = scheduled resolution
```

**Regulatory notification register** must be maintained from the moment a potential eligible breach is identified, tracking: time of awareness, applicable obligations and deadlines, draft content and approval chain, and notifications sent and confirmed. Pre-map all applicable Australian obligations before an incident occurs — not during one.

**Ransomware payment decision process (Australia):**

1. Engage legal counsel and the CISO immediately
2. Check the Australian Sanctions Office Consolidated List before any payment is contemplated — paying a sanctioned entity is a criminal offence regardless of knowledge
3. Assess whether payment is permitted under applicable insurance policies
4. If payment proceeds, report to ASD via ReportCyber within 72 hours (turnover > AUD $3M, or SOCI responsible entity)
5. Comply with any applicable SOCI Act reporting obligations simultaneously

**Incident management metrics:**

- Mean time to detect (MTTD)
- Mean time to respond (MTTR)
- Mean time to contain (MTTC)
- Percentage of incidents resolved within RTO
- Percentage of regulatory notifications made within mandated timeframes
- Repeat incidents with same root cause
- Total cost of incidents (direct remediation plus regulatory, reputational, and productivity impacts)

### Business Continuity Planning

**The BCP lifecycle:**

```
 [Project Planning]          [BC Plan Monitoring,
  (Policy, Scope) <--------- Maintenance & Updating] <----- [BC Plan Testing]
        |                                                           ^
        v                                                           |
 [Risk Assessment]                                         [BC Awareness Training]
        |                                                           ^
        v                                                           |
 [Business Impact] -----> [BC Strategy] -----> [BC Plan Development]
  [Analysis (BIA)]          Development        + Strategy Execution
```

**Recovery site options:**

```
Cold Site ---------> Warm Site --------> Hot Site --------> Mirror / DRaaS
(weeks to activate)  (days to activate)  (hours to activate) (near-instant)
   Lowest cost                                                  Highest cost
```

- **Cold site:** Basic infrastructure (power, cooling, flooring). Equipment sourced on activation. Viable only where MTD is high.
- **Warm site:** Network and peripheral equipment in place. Needs data restoration and software configuration.
- **Hot site:** Fully configured; compatible hardware and software already in place; needs only data and staff.
- **Mirror site:** Runs in parallel with primary on a load-sharing basis. Near-zero RTO. Appropriate only for mission-critical systems.
- **DRaaS:** Cloud-hosted recovery capability. Cloud provider SLAs do not automatically satisfy business RTO requirements — validate explicitly.
- **Mobile sites:** Deployable trailers with pre-configured systems. Useful when no fixed recovery site exists nearby.
- **Reciprocal agreements:** Rarely suitable — partner IT resources typically run at near-capacity.

**BCP plan hierarchy:**

```
Business Continuity Plan (BCP)
├── Continuity of Operations Plan (COOP)     — maintaining ops at alternate site
├── Business Recovery / Resumption Plan      — restoring normal ops post-COOP
├── Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)             — IT systems and data restoration
├── Cyber Incident Response Plan (CSIRP)     — security-specific response
├── Occupant Emergency Plan (OEP)            — life safety and building evacuation
├── Crisis Management Plan (CMP)             — strategic leadership during a crisis
└── Crisis Communications Plan               — internal and external communications
```

**BCP testing progression:**

1. Document review (paper test) — confirms the plan is consistent, current, and accessible offline
2. Tabletop walkthrough — identifies procedural gaps without operational disruption
3. Tabletop with injected complications — time pressure, key person unavailable, backup system fails
4. Component testing — infrastructure and recovery systems tested in isolation
5. Full functional test — end-to-end recovery including application and data restoration; confirms RTOs
6. Surprise test — no-notice activation; reveals real versus assumed operational readiness

**Safety of people is the highest priority** in any continuity or disaster activation. Plans must address staff welfare, building evacuation, alternative work locations, and communication with families before IT recovery decisions are made.

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Plans that have never been tested.** The most common failure mode. Contact lists are outdated, escalation paths are unclear, backup systems have not been verified, and staff have no idea what the plan says. Under PSPF 2025, BCP testing is a mandatory requirement, not an optional practice.

**RTOs that contradict backup architectures.** A business unit declares an RTO of four hours; the backup strategy requires 18 hours for tape restoration. The gap surfaces under incident pressure.

**Evidence destroyed during response.** First responders reboot affected systems, destroying volatile memory containing encryption keys and active attacker tooling. For Australian Government entities, this may also compromise compliance with AGIS investigation requirements. Forensic readiness must include explicit instruction not to reboot affected systems before RAM capture.

**Incident classification without pre-delegated escalation authority.** Containment speed directly determines blast radius and regulatory exposure. Pre-delegated authority to isolate systems, revoke credentials, and take services offline must be documented before an incident occurs.

**Regulatory notification as an afterthought.** Australian organisations must simultaneously manage multiple reporting channels with different timeframes (12 hours, 72 hours, and the NDB assess-within-30-days then notify-as-soon-as-practicable clock). These cannot be planned during an incident. The IRP must pre-map every applicable obligation with a named owner and drafted notification content.

**Ransom payment without sanctions checking.** Paying a sanctioned individual or entity is illegal under Australian law regardless of knowledge. The Australian Sanctions Office Consolidated List must be checked explicitly before any payment. This step must be a named procedure in the IRP.

**Scope limited to IT systems.** BCPs that treat continuity as an IT problem omit critical dependencies on people, physical locations, suppliers, and manual processes. The 2020 pandemic exposed this at scale.

**No out-of-band communication plan.** Teams discover mid-incident that their primary collaboration platform is encrypted or monitored by the attacker, and have no fallback.

**Lessons-learned as a formality.** Under PSPF 2025, lessons learned must be included in the annual protective security report. This creates a formal accountability mechanism, but only if the lessons-learned process is genuine and produces tracked improvement actions.

**Vendor and third-party gaps.** PSPF 2025 requires contractual terms to mandate that service providers report actual or suspected security incidents to the entity and follow the entity's direction during investigations. APRA CPS 234 extends information security obligations to material service providers. These contractual obligations must be verified before an incident, not negotiated during one.

## Connections to Other Domains

**Cyber Governance and Management (1):** Governance structures — including the Accountable Authority, CSO, and CISO roles mandated under PSPF 2025 — directly drive incident and BCP accountability. Security governance failures are frequently root causes of inadequate incident response capability.

**Cyber Risk Management (2):** The BIA feeds risk treatment decisions. RTOs, RPOs, and SDOs translate risk tolerance into technical and operational requirements. Under PSPF, the CIRMP (SOCI Act) and entity security risk management programme must incorporate business continuity risk.

**Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance (3):** Australian notification obligations (OAIC, APRA, ACSC, ASD, ASIO, Department of Home Affairs) are legal constraints that must be embedded in IRP procedures before an incident. Evidence handling intersects with potential litigation, criminal investigation, and AGIS compliance.

**Privacy and Data Protection (4):** Whether an incident is an eligible data breach under the Privacy Act NDB scheme must be assessed during identification and triage. Notification content and timing obligations must be pre-prepared.

**Vendor Management (5):** PSPF 2025 and APRA CPS 234 both extend incident management obligations to service providers. The SOCI Act's all-hazards approach means supply chain failures are themselves a category of critical infrastructure incident. Vendor contractual terms must require incident reporting and cooperation with investigations.

**Asset and Change Management (6):** An accurate, current asset register is foundational to incident scoping and BCP. PSPF 2025 requires entities to maintain a technology asset stocktake. SOCI Act entities must maintain a Critical Infrastructure Asset Register. Change management failures are a common cause of both incidents and failed recoveries.

**Security Operations and Monitoring (12):** The SOC provides primary detection. SIEM, EDR, and SOAR tooling feed the identification phase. Mean time to detect (MTTD) directly determines how long an attacker has been present before containment — a critical driver of regulatory exposure under tight notification timeframes.

**Threat Intelligence (11):** Current threat intelligence informs playbook development and triage prioritisation. Under PSPF Direction 003-2024, NCEs must share cyber threat intelligence with ASD via the CTIS platform.

**Cloud Management (14):** Shared responsibility models affect accountability during cloud-hosted incidents. DRaaS SLAs must be validated against business RTOs. Evidence collection from cloud environments requires pre-established agreements. Australian Government entities must use cloud providers and data centre providers certified under the Australian Government Hosting Certification Framework.

**Cyber Architecture (17):** Architectural decisions — network segmentation, zero-trust controls, immutable backup storage, out-of-band management networks — directly determine how containable and recoverable an organisation is. PSPF 2025 mandates zero trust culture principles in the cyber security strategy and uplift plan.

**Cyber Testing (10):** Tabletop exercises, red team operations, and full BCP/DRP tests verify whether plans and controls actually work. PSPF 2025 requires tested incident response plans. SOCI SoNS entities must participate in ministerially directed cyber exercises. Testing findings must feed the plan improvement cycle.

**Cyber Culture and Education (18):** Staff are both the most common initial infection vector and the first line of detection. A no-blame reporting culture materially affects how quickly incidents are identified and escalated. Training must include explicit instruction on what to do — and not to do — when a suspected incident occurs.

**Physical Security (20):** Physical access to data centres and network infrastructure is both a continuity dependency and an incident vector. The SOCI ERP Act 2024 extended government assistance powers to non-cyber incidents including physical disruptions and natural disasters, reflecting the all-hazards approach. BCPs must account for physical site unavailability.
