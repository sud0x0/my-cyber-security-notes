# Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Legal, regulatory, and compliance (LRC) is the domain that ensures an organisation's security programme meets its obligations under law, regulation, and contract — and can demonstrate that it does. It sits between governance (which sets direction) and the rest of the security programme (which executes), translating external obligations into internal requirements.

Compliance is not a destination. It is a continuous monitoring process that records and demonstrates whether the controls in place actually satisfy the obligations imposed on the organisation. This note covers the sources of obligation, the major Australian and international instruments, and how compliance is operated in practice.

---

## Why It Matters

Failures in this domain produce consequences that are immediate, quantifiable, and often public.

**Financial penalties.** The Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) carries maximum penalties for serious or repeated interferences with privacy of the greater of AUD 50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover, introduced by the Privacy Legislation Amendment (Enforcement and Other Measures) Act 2022 (December 2022). The EU GDPR ceiling is €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover. APRA imposed a AUD 250 million capital charge on Medibank Private following its 2022 breach, establishing that enforcement is real and escalating — APRA's trajectory is toward court-based enforcement, not just capital charges.

**Regulatory action.** Regulators can revoke licences, impose enforceable undertakings, mandate remediation programmes, and compel independent audits. APRA's tripartite assessment programme across 300+ entities revealed systemic gaps including incomplete asset classification and inadequate control testing — gaps that have since attracted licence conditions against specific entities such as United Super and Cbus in 2024–2025.

**Personal liability.** Australia's Financial Accountability Regime (FAR) makes individual executives personally accountable for CPS 234 compliance obligations. ASIC has signalled that directors of listed companies who fail to manage cyber risk may breach their duties under the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth). The statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, operative by June 2025, allows individuals to sue directly for intrusion upon seclusion or misuse of personal information.

**Operational disruption.** Non-compliance discovered during due diligence can kill a merger, block a government contract, or collapse a partnership. Evidence of compliance must be ready, not assembled retrospectively.

**Reputational damage.** Mandatory breach notification means regulatory failure becomes public. The Medibank and Optus breaches demonstrated how quickly public trust dissolves. Regulatory investigations are now reported in the press as they happen.

Legal and regulatory requirements are a responsibility of the board and senior management, not the security team alone.

## Core Concepts

**Three sources of obligation.** LRC obligations come from three distinct but overlapping sources: legal, regulatory, and contractual. All three translate into internal policies, standards, controls, and audit evidence.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                  OBLIGATION SOURCES                     │
├──────────────┬──────────────────┬───────────────────────┤
│     LEGAL    │   REGULATORY     │     CONTRACTUAL       │
│              │                  │                       │
│ Statute law  │ Sector regulator │ Customer/supplier     │
│ Common law   │ standards (e.g.  │ contracts, SLAs,      │
│ Court orders │ CPS 234, HIPAA)  │ PCI DSS, SCCs         │
│              │                  │                       │
│ Non-optional │ Non-optional     │ Binding within        │
│              │                  │ the relationship      │
└──────────────┴──────────────────┴───────────────────────┘
                         │
                         ▼
              Internal policies, standards,
              controls, and audit evidence
```

**Compliance is not the same as security.** A system can pass every compliance test and still be trivially compromised. Controls are mapped to requirements; requirements are written for a minimum standard at a point in time. Risk management must drive control selection. Compliance provides the floor, not the ceiling.

**Jurisdiction determines obligation.** An Australian organisation processing data about EU residents is subject to GDPR as well as the Privacy Act. A financial services firm regulated by APRA must comply with CPS 234 regardless of whether it also holds ISO 27001 certification. Scope must be defined before obligations can be identified. Where multiple obligations overlap, the most stringent applies unless the instruments specify otherwise.

**Due care and due diligence.** Due care is doing what a reasonable person would do — implementing appropriate controls. Due diligence is the ongoing work of verifying that those controls are working. Both are required for a defensible compliance position.

**Frameworks vs regulations.**

```
   Regulations              Frameworks / Standards
   ────────────             ──────────────────────
   Mandatory                Generally voluntary unless
   Enforced by law          mandated by a regulator
   Penalties for            Enforced by contract or
   non-compliance           market expectation
   Jurisdiction/sector      Provide structure for
   specific                 demonstrating compliance
                            Can be cited by regulators as
                            the "reasonable steps" standard
```

Regulators citing "failure to follow industry best practice" are using a framework as an evidential standard. ISO 27001, the Essential Eight, and the ISM all function this way in Australian regulatory proceedings even when not legally mandated for the entity in question.

**Control mapping and harmonisation.** Most mature organisations maintain a compliance register mapping each obligation to the controls that satisfy it, the evidence available, the control owner, and the review date. The practical problem is overlap: the same technical control (for example, MFA) often satisfies obligations under CPS 234, the Privacy Act, PCI DSS, and ISO 27001 simultaneously. Mapping this explicitly avoids redundant effort and reveals genuine gaps that no single framework would expose.

**Regulatory change management.** Regulations change continuously. A compliance programme must include a process for monitoring regulatory updates, assessing their impact on existing controls and policies, assigning remediation ownership, and updating the compliance register. Regulators also issue "soft law" — enforcement actions, Dear CEO letters, findings publications, and industry letters — that shapes how rules are applied in practice. These must be tracked alongside formal legislative and regulatory changes.

**Policy lifecycle.** Regulatory obligations translate into policies; policies translate into controls; controls generate evidence. Each layer must be kept current.

```
Regulatory obligation
        │
        ▼
  Internal policy  <── reviewed when regulation changes
        │
        ▼
  Technical / operational control
        │
        ▼
  Evidence (logs, test results, attestations)
        │
        ▼
  Compliance register  <── auditable, continuously maintained
```

**Data retention and records management.** Many regulatory instruments impose specific retention periods and require secure disposal at the end of the data lifecycle. Non-compliance includes both retaining data too long (unnecessary exposure) and destroying it prematurely (destroying evidence). Legal hold obligations imposed during litigation or a regulatory investigation override standard retention schedules immediately. Disposal must be verifiable — certificates of destruction for physical media, cryptographic erasure or verified wiping for digital storage.

## How It Works in Practice

### Building the Obligation Inventory

The starting point is identifying which laws, regulations, and contracts apply. This requires input from legal counsel, the business, and security — not only IT. The obligation inventory must be reviewed whenever the organisation enters a new jurisdiction, acquires a business, launches a new product, changes a key vendor relationship, or a regulatory change is notified.

### Gap Assessment and Remediation

Current controls are mapped against identified obligations. Gaps are documented, risk-rated, and fed into the remediation programme. When non-compliance is identified, investigation must begin with a risk assessment to determine severity and urgency. Noncompliance must be reported to the audit committee — not absorbed within the security team.

### The Compliance Register

A living document — typically maintained in a GRC platform (ServiceNow GRC, RSA Archer, OneTrust, 6clicks, or purpose-built tools) or, at minimum, a controlled spreadsheet — capturing:

- Obligation reference and source instrument
- Control(s) satisfying the obligation
- Evidence type and location
- Control owner and approver
- Last tested / next review date
- Current status: compliant, gap, or remediation in progress

### Regulatory Change Management

A dedicated process monitors regulatory updates from relevant regulators — OAIC, APRA, ASD, Home Affairs, ASIC, and applicable sector regulators. Updates include not only formal legislative and regulatory amendments but also enforcement action outcomes, regulatory findings publications, and Dear CEO letters, all of which shape how rules are applied in practice. Each change is assessed for impact on the compliance register. Decisions about impact and remediation are documented.

### The Australian Government Compliance Stack

For Commonwealth entities and any private sector organisation supplying to government or operating critical infrastructure, Australian compliance is layered and interlinked. Understanding how the instruments stack is essential — they are not alternatives; many apply simultaneously.

State and territory entities that hold Commonwealth classified information must also comply with the PSPF. Systems of Government Significance (SoGS), declared critical government digital systems, carry additional mandatory cybersecurity obligations.

**Who the Privacy Act covers.** The Privacy Act applies to most Australian Government agencies regardless of size, to organisations with annual turnover exceeding $3 million, and to some smaller entities regardless of turnover. The latter include all private sector health service providers (private hospitals, day surgeries, medical practitioners, pharmacists, allied health professionals, complementary therapists) with no turnover threshold, businesses that trade in personal information, and businesses that handle consumer credit information or tax file numbers. State and territory government agencies are generally not covered by the federal Privacy Act, with the exception of ACT government agencies, which are explicitly covered. Most states and territories have their own public sector privacy legislation, though South Australia has no general privacy act covering its public sector agencies.

**PSPF in practice for Commonwealth entities.** NCEs must conduct an annual self-assessment against all PSPF mandatory requirements and report to their portfolio minister and the Department of Home Affairs. The first evidence-based compliance report (2024–25) found 92% of entities at Effective compliance and 8% at Moderate compliance (unverified — confirm against the PSPF Assessment Report). The PSPF is not static — Directions can be issued at any time outside the annual release cycle. Direction 001-2025 (DeepSeek removal) and Direction 001-2026 (Cisco SD-WAN inventory) both required immediate action.

**PSPF reach into the private sector.** Any organisation that stores, processes, transmits, or can influence the protection of Commonwealth information or assets is caught by PSPF requirements — through contract terms, deed conditions, panel head-agreements, or formal Directions. This includes cloud providers (requiring IRAP assessment at the relevant classification level), managed service providers, facilities managers, and contractors with access to government systems. Non-conformance affects tender eligibility and contract continuity.

**SOCI CIRMP in practice.** The CIRMP must be written, board-approved, and address all hazards across four categories: cyber and information security (must comply with a recognised framework — Essential Eight, NIST CSF, ISO 27001, AESCSF, C2M2, or an equivalent framework), personnel, supply chain, and physical security and natural hazards (a single combined category). The CIRMP must be reviewed whenever there is a material change to the asset or its risk environment. An annual report is due to the Department of Home Affairs within 90 days of 30 June. CISC can direct entities to remediate serious deficiencies. Systems of National Significance (SoNS) face enhanced obligations including mandatory vulnerability assessments, incident response exercises, and near-real-time threat sharing with ASD. The three Positive Security Obligations commenced in stages (asset registration and incident reporting from 2022, the CIRMP obligation from 2023), the cybersecurity framework obligation under Section 8 of the CIRMP Rules took effect 18 August 2024, and the first board-approved annual CIRMP reports were due 28 September 2024.

**Dual compliance — government entities with critical infrastructure assets.** Government agencies that own or operate critical infrastructure are simultaneously subject to PSPF and SOCI obligations. Neither instrument reconciles the other — the entity must maintain a programme satisfying both. The 72-hour ransomware payment clock under the Cyber Security Act runs in parallel with the PSPF's ASD notification requirements and SOCI's 12-hour significant cyber incident reporting window. These are three separate obligations with three separate clocks.

### Multi-Regulator Breach Notification

A ransomware incident at an APRA-regulated entity in a SOCI-regulated sector that results in a data breach can trigger all of the following simultaneously, each with different triggers, different content, and different receiving regulators:

```
Incident confirmed
      │
      ├──► NDB (OAIC): notify as soon as practicable once an
      │         eligible data breach is confirmed
      │         (trigger: data breach likely to cause serious harm)
      │
      ├──► CPS 234 (APRA): notify within 72 hours of a material
      │         information security incident
      │         (trigger: material incident, not necessarily a breach)
      │
      ├──► Cyber Security Act (ASD/Home Affairs): notify within
      │         72 hours of making a ransomware payment
      │         (trigger: the payment itself, not the incident)
      │
      └──► SOCI (ASD/CISC): two-tier for critical infrastructure assets
                - Significant cyber incidents: report to ASD within 12 hours
                - Other notifiable incidents: report to CISC within 72 hours
                (trigger: incident affecting a registered CI asset)
```

Pre-built notification workflows with pre-approved template content and identified decision-makers are not optional. Organisations that have not rehearsed these workflows will miss deadlines under incident pressure.

### Evidence Management

Evidence must be collected continuously, not assembled before an audit. Logs, configuration screenshots, test results, policy acknowledgements, training completion records, and vendor attestations are all evidence. The ISACA IT Audit Framework (ITAF) — General standards (1000 series), Performance standards (1200 series), Reporting standards (1400 series) — defines the structure auditors apply when examining this evidence. Evidence must be dated, version-controlled, tamper-evident, traceable to the control being evidenced, and stored in an auditor-accessible location.

### Data Retention

Retention schedules must be documented against regulatory requirements for each data category. The Privacy Act requires personal information not be retained longer than necessary for the purpose for which it was collected. Legal holds imposed during litigation or a regulatory investigation override standard schedules immediately and must be communicated to teams managing the relevant data. Disposal must be verifiable.

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Treating compliance as a project.** Organisations pass an audit, declare success, and stop. Controls drift, systems change, and new obligations emerge. APRA's tripartite assessments found that many entities had not maintained asset classification or conducted control testing on an ongoing basis — gaps present at the time of original certification but discovered years later.

**Defining scope too narrowly.** A PCI DSS programme that excludes cloud-hosted cardholder data, a CPS 234 programme that omits third parties managing information assets, or an Essential Eight assessment that excludes OT networks will fail on examination. Scope decisions must be documented and defensible.

**Jurisdiction blindness.** An Australian company collecting data from EU residents through a website is subject to GDPR. An organisation with AUD 3M+ turnover that pays a ransom is subject to the Cyber Security Act. Many organisations discover these obligations only when a regulator contacts them.

**Legal and technical language mismatch.** Legal teams interpret obligations in ways that do not translate clearly into technical controls. Security teams implement controls that do not satisfy the legal intent. The gap is usually invisible until an auditor or regulator identifies it. Controls must be reviewed by someone who understands both the legal text and the technical implementation.

**Conflating certification with compliance.** ISO 27001 certification does not satisfy CPS 234. PCI DSS compliance does not satisfy the Privacy Act. Certifications demonstrate that a programme exists. They do not substitute for understanding and satisfying each specific obligation.

**Point-in-time third-party assurance.** Reviewing a vendor's SOC 2 report once at onboarding does not satisfy ongoing CPS 234 assessment obligations. Third-party compliance requires continuous monitoring. APRA's tripartite findings specifically identified third-party control gaps as a pervasive system-wide weakness.

**Missing soft law.** APRA industry letters, OAIC determinations, and ASIC regulatory guidance shape how formal obligations are interpreted and enforced in practice. Organisations that monitor only formal legislation and miss this layer operate with an incomplete picture of their obligations.

**Reactive breach notification.** The 72-hour clocks under CPS 234 and the Cyber Security Act, and the "as soon as practicable" standard under the NDB scheme, are enforced more aggressively than many organisations expect. Notification workflows must be pre-built, rehearsed, and kept current.

**Treating PSPF as government-only.** Private sector organisations commonly believe the PSPF does not apply to them. It does — through contract terms, deed conditions, panel agreements, and Directions — for any organisation that handles Commonwealth information or assets. Non-conformance with PSPF requirements is increasingly identified during government tender evaluations and can disqualify a supplier. Organisations working in the government market must maintain a PSPF compliance map, not just a general ISO 27001 or Essential Eight programme.

**Treating SOCI as a registration exercise.** Entities that register their critical infrastructure assets and submit a CIRMP annual report believe they are done. The CIRMP is a living programme, not a document. It must be updated when material changes occur to the asset or its risk environment, and it must be underpinned by actual controls that are tested. CISC can direct remediation of serious CIRMP deficiencies, and the board is accountable for approving the programme.

**Missing the 12-hour SOCI incident reporting clock.** SOCI requires significant cyber security incidents affecting critical infrastructure assets to be reported to ASD within 12 hours. This is distinct from the 72-hour clock for other notifiable incidents and from the CPS 234 72-hour material incident notification. Most incident response plans are built around 24-hour or 72-hour windows and will miss the 12-hour SOCI obligation if it is not explicitly built in.

**Absorbing noncompliance internally.** Noncompliance must be reported to the audit committee, not managed quietly within the security team. Suppressing findings is itself a governance failure. Under FAR, named executives bear personal accountability for doing so in APRA-regulated entities.

## Connections to Other Domains

- **[Cyber Governance and Management (1)](01.%20Cyber%20Governance%20and%20Management.md):** LRC obligations translate into governance structures. The board and senior management are accountable for compliance. Governance must establish how obligations are identified, assigned, and reported before LRC can operate effectively.

- **[Cyber Risk Management (2)](02.%20Cyber%20Risk%20Management.md):** Non-compliance is a risk to be assessed and managed. Where obligations cannot be fully met, residual risk must be formally accepted at the appropriate level. Investigation of identified non-compliance must start with a risk assessment.

- **[Privacy and Data Protection (4)](04.%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Protection.md):** Privacy law is the largest and most dynamic area of LRC for most organisations. The Privacy Act, APPs, NDB scheme, GDPR, and sector-specific rules all sit at the intersection. These two domains must be governed together.

- **[Vendor Management (5)](05.%20Vendor%20Management.md):** Third-party obligations extend compliance requirements across the supply chain. CPS 234 requires ongoing assessment of third parties' information security capability. GDPR requires Standard Contractual Clauses for transfers to non-adequate countries. PSPF Directions and contract terms extend PSPF obligations to private sector suppliers handling Commonwealth information. SOCI CIRMP explicitly requires supply chain hazard assessment and management. DISP Essential Eight ML2 requirements flow through the defence supply chain.

- **[Asset and Change Management (6)](06.%20Asset%20and%20Change%20Management.md):** Compliance scope depends on knowing what assets exist and what data they hold. Asset classification is a prerequisite for identifying which obligations apply. Change management processes must include a compliance impact assessment step to avoid introducing new gaps.

- **[Network and Communication Security (7)](07.%20Network%20and%20Communication%20Security.md):** Telecommunications security obligations for critical assets migrated from the Telecommunications Act into SOCI Part 2D from April 2025, with bespoke CIRMP rules (TSRMP Rules 2025). PSPF Technology domain mandates Protective DNS and gateway security for Commonwealth entities. Network segmentation and logging satisfy obligations across PSPF, SOCI, CPS 234, and PCI DSS simultaneously.

- **[Identity and Access Management (8)](08.%20Identity%20and%20Access%20Management.md):** Separation of duties (SOX), least privilege, MFA (Essential Eight Maturity Level 1 and above), audit logging of privileged access, and periodic access reviews are all compliance-driven control requirements. IAM programme design must account for the applicable regulatory baseline.

- **[Cryptography (9)](09.%20Cryptography.md):** GDPR treats encrypted breached data as unlikely to cause harm, which can remove or reduce notification obligations. Export controls on cryptographic products create additional legal compliance obligations in some jurisdictions.

- **[Cyber Testing (10)](10.%20Cyber%20Testing.md):** Compliance testing (controls exist) and substantive testing (controls produce correct outputs) are both formal audit concepts under ITAF. Penetration testing results and vulnerability assessment reports are compliance evidence under CPS 234, PCI DSS, and the Essential Eight. Testing scope must align with compliance scope.

- **[Threat Intelligence (11)](11.%20Threat%20Intelligence.md):** Regulators expect controls to be commensurate with the threat landscape. CPS 234 explicitly requires information security capability to scale with evolving threats and vulnerabilities. Threat intelligence feeds into the risk assessment that justifies control selection and, if a breach occurs, into the regulatory response narrative.

- **[Security Operations and Monitoring (12)](12.%20Security%20Operations%20and%20Monitoring.md):** Continuous monitoring detects compliance drift and generates the audit evidence regulators require. Log integrity is a compliance requirement under most frameworks. SIEM and monitoring coverage must align with the scope defined in the compliance register.

- **[Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning (13)](13.%20Incident%20Management%20and%20Business%20Continuity%20Planning.md):** Breach notification is a compliance obligation with hard deadlines across five regulators for some organisations. Incident response plans must incorporate regulatory notification workflows with pre-identified decision-makers. BCP and DR testing are compliance requirements under CPS 230 (from July 2025) and SOCI CIRMP. PSPF requires government entities to develop and maintain contingency plans as part of the Risk domain. SOCI SoNS entities must conduct mandatory incident response exercises.

- **[Cloud Management (14)](14.%20Cloud%20Management.md):** Cloud deployments create compliance complexity. PSPF Technology domain requires Commonwealth entities to use IRAP-assessed cloud services for workloads at OFFICIAL: Sensitive and above. The Hosting Certification Framework governs hosting providers for government workloads. Data residency requirements, the shared responsibility model, and cross-border data transfer obligations all require explicit mapping against applicable regulations — GDPR for EU data, Privacy Act for Australian personal information.

- **[Software Development Security (15)](15.%20Software%20Development%20Security.md):** Secure development controls are compliance requirements under SOCI (for critical infrastructure software), PCI DSS (Requirements 6.x), and HIPAA. The Essential Eight includes application hardening. Compliance requirements must be built into software from the design phase, not added after release.

- **[Cyber Architecture (17)](17.%20Cyber%20Architecture.md):** Architecture decisions (data residency, encryption, network segmentation, logging design) have direct compliance implications. Compliance gaps identified during architecture review must be escalated to governance before systems go into production.

- **[Cyber Culture and Education (18)](18.%20Cyber%20Culture%20and%20Education.md):** Compliance training is a direct obligation under GDPR, the Privacy Act (reasonable steps include staff training), and most certification frameworks. Awareness programmes must include the legal consequences of non-compliance, not only threat scenarios.

- **[LLM Security (19)](19.%20LLM%20Security.md):** Automated decision-making systems are now subject to transparency obligations under the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 (Cth), with disclosure requirements operative from December 2026. The EU AI Act is entering force. Compliance obligations for AI systems are a rapidly growing area requiring proactive engagement from security and privacy teams.

- **[Physical Security (20)](20.%20Physical%20Security.md):** Physical access controls are required by PCI DSS (Requirement 9), HIPAA physical safeguards, ISO 27001 Annex A, and SOCI. Physical access logs, visitor registers, and CCTV retention periods are compliance evidence items with their own retention obligations.
