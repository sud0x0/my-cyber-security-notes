# Cryptography

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Cryptography is the discipline of securing information and communications through mathematical techniques that control who can read, modify, or authenticate data. It sits underneath almost every other security control — TLS protecting network traffic, certificates authenticating identities, hashes verifying file integrity, and encrypted storage protecting data at rest all depend on cryptographic primitives working correctly.

In practice, cryptography is less about building algorithms and more about selecting, configuring, and managing them correctly over time. The hardest part is not the maths — it is key lifecycle management, algorithm agility, and keeping pace with deprecations and emerging threats. The cryptographic landscape is also undergoing its most significant transition in decades: the shift to post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is now underway and requires active planning from every organisation, including those outside government.

---

## Why It Matters

Poor cryptographic practice has caused some of the most consequential breaches on record:

- **Weak or deprecated algorithms**: The 2012 LinkedIn breach exposed 117 million passwords hashed with unsalted SHA-1. Without a salt, rainbow table attacks trivially cracked the hashes at scale.
- **Broken TLS configurations**: POODLE (2014), BEAST (2011), and DROWN (2016) exploited weak TLS versions and cipher suites still enabled on production servers — entirely preventable through configuration.
- **Certificate mismanagement**: DigiNotar (2011) issued fraudulent certificates enabling man-in-the-middle attacks against Iranian users of Google services. The failure was both procedural and architectural.
- **Hardcoded and shared keys**: IoT and embedded devices routinely ship with hardcoded symmetric keys or default private keys baked into firmware. An attacker who extracts one device can compromise every other device running the same firmware.
- **Harvest now, decrypt later (HNDL)**: Nation-state actors are capturing encrypted traffic today, planning to decrypt it once sufficiently capable quantum computers exist. Data with long-term sensitivity is already at risk if protected only by classical asymmetric algorithms.

The consequences of poor cryptography extend beyond data exposure — they include loss of integrity guarantees, broken authentication, inability to prove non-repudiation, and regulatory breach under frameworks including the Australian Privacy Act 1988, the ASD ISM, GDPR, and PCI DSS v4.0.1.

## Core Concepts

### Confidentiality through Encryption

Encryption transforms plaintext into ciphertext using a key and algorithm. Without the correct key, the ciphertext is computationally infeasible to reverse. The fundamental distinction is between:

- **Symmetric encryption**: The same key encrypts and decrypts. Fast, efficient, suited to bulk data. Examples: AES-256-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305.
- **Asymmetric encryption**: A public key encrypts; only the corresponding private key decrypts. Slower, used for key exchange and digital signatures. Examples: RSA, ECDH (key exchange), ML-KEM (post-quantum key encapsulation).

These are almost always combined in practice: asymmetric mechanisms establish a session key, symmetric algorithms protect the actual data. This is called a **hybrid cryptosystem**.

**Block vs stream ciphers**: Block ciphers (AES) operate on fixed-size blocks and require a mode of operation. Stream ciphers (ChaCha20) encrypt one byte or bit at a time. Neither is inherently superior — the choice depends on use case and platform.

**Modes of operation**: The mode determines how successive blocks are processed. AES-ECB (Electronic Codebook) is broken — identical plaintext blocks produce identical ciphertext blocks, leaking patterns. The correct modern default is an Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data (AEAD) mode: AES-GCM provides confidentiality and integrity in a single pass. ChaCha20-Poly1305 is preferred where hardware AES acceleration is absent.

### Integrity through Hashing

A cryptographic hash function produces a fixed-length digest from arbitrary input. The same input always produces the same digest. Any change to the input produces a completely different digest. Hash functions are one-way — recovering the input from the digest is computationally infeasible.

Approved hash functions per NIST FIPS 180-4 (SHA-2 family): SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-512. Per NIST FIPS 202 (SHA-3 family): SHA3-256, SHA3-384, SHA3-512. SHA-1 is deprecated for digital signatures and should not be used for new applications. MD5 is broken and must not be used for any security purpose.

Hash functions underpin digital signatures, file integrity verification, HMAC-based authentication, key derivation, and — when combined with a salt and a slow algorithm — password storage.

### Authentication through Digital Signatures and MACs

A **digital signature** applies an asymmetric private key to a hash of the data. Anyone with the corresponding public key can verify the signature, confirming both that the data has not changed and that it came from the holder of the private key. Signatures provide **non-repudiation** — the signer cannot plausibly deny signing.

A **Message Authentication Code (MAC)** achieves integrity and authentication using a shared symmetric key. HMAC (Hash-based MAC) is the standard construction. MACs do not provide non-repudiation — because both parties hold the same key, either could have produced the code.

### Key Establishment and Exchange

Before two parties can communicate securely, they must agree on a shared secret without transmitting it in the clear. **Diffie-Hellman (DH)** and its elliptic curve variant **(ECDH)** accomplish this over an untrusted channel. TLS 1.3 uses ephemeral ECDH or a key encapsulation mechanism (KEM) to establish a session key, then uses that session key symmetrically. The use of ephemeral keys provides **perfect forward secrecy (PFS)** — compromise of a long-term private key does not expose past session keys.

### Public Key Infrastructure (PKI)

PKI is the trust system that binds public keys to identities using certificates issued by Certificate Authorities (CAs). An X.509 certificate is a signed data structure asserting that a particular public key belongs to a particular entity, signed by a CA whose own certificate chains up to a trusted root.

```
Trust hierarchy:

  Root CA (self-signed, kept offline in HSM)
      └── Intermediate CA (signed by Root)
              └── End-Entity Certificate (signed by Intermediate)
                      └── Subject: server or user identity + public key
```

When this chain of trust breaks — through CA compromise, certificate misissuance, or failed revocation — the entire authentication model fails. Revocation is handled via Certificate Revocation Lists (CRLs) or the Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP). Certificate Transparency (CT) logs provide a public, append-only record of issued certificates, enabling detection of misissuance.

### Key Management and Key Lifecycle

Key management is operationally the hardest part of cryptography. The key lifecycle per NIST SP 800-57 covers:

```
Generate → Register → Distribute → Install → Use → Rotate/Renew → Revoke → Archive → Destroy
```

- **Generation**: Must use a cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator (CSPRNG). Entropy quality is critical — poor randomness is a common and catastrophic failure mode.
- **Storage**: Keys must never be stored in plaintext. HSMs provide tamper-resistant storage. Cloud KMS services and secrets managers are acceptable alternatives when properly configured.
- **Cryptoperiod**: The time span during which a key is authorised for use. Longer cryptoperiods increase the value of a compromised key. NIST SP 800-57 provides cryptoperiod recommendations per key type.
- **Rotation**: Keys should be rotated before the cryptoperiod expires, and immediately upon suspected or confirmed compromise.
- **Revocation**: The key and any associated certificate must be revoked and that revocation published promptly upon compromise.
- **Destruction/Zeroisation**: All copies of a key — including in-memory copies — must be explicitly zeroised when no longer needed. Do not rely on garbage collection.

**Key types and hierarchy**:

```
Root/CA Key (offline HSM — never used for data)
    └── Key Encryption Key (KEK) — wraps DEKs, never encrypts user data
            └── Data Encryption Key (DEK) — encrypts actual data; short-lived
                    └── Session Key — ephemeral, single use
```

### Algorithm Agility

Cryptographic algorithms have finite lifespans — they are deprecated when weaknesses are found or computing power advances. An architecture that hardcodes a specific algorithm is brittle. **Algorithm agility** means treating the algorithm as a configurable parameter, not a constant. This is essential infrastructure for PQC migration and for responding to future deprecations without rebuilding systems.

### Cryptographic Module Validation

**FIPS 140-3** (superseding FIPS 140-2) defines security requirements for cryptographic modules — hardware or software implementing cryptographic functions. The NIST/CCCS Cryptographic Module Validation Program (CMVP) validates modules at four security levels (Level 1 through 4). Validated modules are listed publicly and procurement should reference the current validated list.

In Australian government contexts, the ISM requires ASD-Approved Cryptographic Algorithms (AACAs) and ASD-Approved Cryptographic Protocols (AACPs). For OFFICIAL: Sensitive and PROTECTED data, cryptographic equipment, applications, or libraries must have completed a Common Criteria evaluation against an ASD-endorsed Protection Profile. For SECRET and TOP SECRET, High Assurance Cryptographic Equipment (HACE) is required. The relevant international standards are ISO/IEC 19790:2025 and ISO/IEC 24759:2025.

### The Post-Quantum Transition

Classical asymmetric algorithms — RSA, DSA, ECDSA, and DH — derive their security from problems (integer factorisation, discrete logarithm) that quantum computers can solve efficiently using Shor's algorithm. Symmetric algorithms and hash functions are significantly less affected — AES-256 and SHA-384/SHA-512 remain viable with appropriate parameter sizes.

**Current NIST PQC standards (finalised August 2024):**

| Standard | Algorithm Name                   | Type                        | Mathematical Basis |
| -------- | -------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------ |
| FIPS 203 | ML-KEM (from CRYSTALS-Kyber)     | Key encapsulation mechanism | Module lattice     |
| FIPS 204 | ML-DSA (from CRYSTALS-Dilithium) | Digital signature           | Module lattice     |
| FIPS 205 | SLH-DSA (from SPHINCS+)          | Digital signature           | Hash-based         |

**In development:**

- **FIPS 206 (FN-DSA, from FALCON)**: A lattice-based digital signature scheme. Offers smaller signatures than ML-DSA — suited for bandwidth-constrained environments and compressing certificate chains. As of August 2025, the draft was submitted to NIST for Initial Public Draft approval. Final standard expected 2026–2027. FN-DSA is harder to implement correctly (requires floating-point arithmetic) and is expected to be used in specific contexts (root and intermediate certificates) rather than as a general replacement for ML-DSA.
- **HQC** (selected March 2025): A code-based KEM selected as a mathematically diverse backup to ML-KEM. Draft standard expected 2026, final 2027.

**Hybrid cryptography during transition**: Many implementations are running classical and PQC algorithms in parallel (e.g., X25519 + ML-KEM in TLS handshakes). This provides security that degrades gracefully if either algorithm is broken, and is the recommended transition approach for internet protocols.

**Australian ISM PQC requirements**: ISM-1917 (introduced March 2024) requires organisations to consider future cryptographic requirements and dependencies. Controls ISM-1990 to ISM-1995 adopt specific parameter sets of ML-KEM and ML-DSA as AACAs. Commonwealth entities are expected to be using approved PQC algorithms by 2030. APRA-regulated entities must assess PQC migration risk as an operational risk under CPS 230.

```
Cryptographic primitive map:

  Data ──► Hash Function ─────────────────────────► Digest (integrity)
                 │
                 ▼
  Private Key + Digest ──► Digital Signature ──────► Verified with Public Key
                                                      (authenticity + non-repudiation)

  Plaintext ──► AEAD Encrypt (AES-256-GCM) ──────► Ciphertext + Auth Tag
                 │
                 └── Session key from ──► ECDH or ML-KEM key exchange

  Symmetric Key ──► Wrapped by KEK (AES-256 Key Wrap) ──► Stored securely

  Password ──► KDF (Argon2id) + Unique Salt ─────► Stored credential hash

  Key lifecycle:
  Generate → Distribute → Store (HSM/KMS) → Use → Rotate → Revoke → Destroy
```

### Data States and Encryption Coverage

Cryptographic controls must address all three data states:

- **Data at rest**: Full disk encryption (BitLocker with AES-256, Linux dm-crypt/LUKS), database-level encryption, object storage encryption. The ISM requires full disk encryption or an equivalent control for OFFICIAL: Sensitive data and above.
- **Data in transit**: TLS 1.3 (minimum TLS 1.2 with strong cipher suites for legacy systems), IPsec, SSH, secure VPN protocols. The ISM requires ASD-Approved Cryptographic Protocols for data traversing networks that are not under organisational control.
- **Data in use**: Confidential computing — Intel TDX, AMD SEV-SNP, and ARM CCA use hardware-based Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) to protect data while it is being processed. This is a maturing area relevant to cloud workloads, AI inference, and multi-party computation.

## How It Works in Practice

### Cryptographic Policy and Standards Document

The security function defines a **cryptographic standard** — a formal document specifying approved algorithms, minimum key lengths, prohibited algorithms, approved modes of operation, key management requirements, and configuration requirements for protocols such as TLS and SSH. This document is then referenced by application development, infrastructure engineering, and vendor procurement. Without it, teams default to library or platform defaults, which may not align with risk appetite or regulatory requirements.

### TLS Configuration

Most organisations' highest-volume cryptographic surface is TLS. Common failures: permitting TLS 1.0/1.1 (deprecated and must be disabled), weak cipher suites (RC4, 3DES, RSA key exchange without forward secrecy), and certificate validation disabled in application code. TLS 1.3 should be the minimum for new deployments; TLS 1.2 with strong, forward-secret cipher suites is acceptable for legacy compatibility with explicit justification.

Tools: Mozilla SSL Configuration Generator (https://ssl-config.mozilla.org) for server configuration templates; testssl.sh and sslyze for active testing of deployed services.

### PKI and Certificate Lifecycle Management

Certificate sprawl — thousands of certificates across a large environment with no central inventory — routinely causes outages when certificates expire unexpectedly, and creates security gaps when private keys are compromised but revocation is not actioned. Certificate Transparency logs (https://certificate.transparency.dev) enable detection of unexpected issuance against your domains.

Certificate Lifecycle Management (CLM) platforms (Venafi, Keyfactor, HashiCorp Vault PKI secrets engine) provide automated discovery, renewal, and revocation. Short-lived certificates (hours to days rather than one to two years) reduce the blast radius of key compromise and are the direction of travel for zero-trust environments.

### Key Management Infrastructure

Keys must never be stored in plaintext in configuration files, code repositories, or databases. The tooling hierarchy:

- **HSMs** (Thales, Entrust, AWS CloudHSM, Azure Dedicated HSM): Tamper-resistant hardware that stores keys and performs operations without ever exporting key material. Required for high-assurance and classified environments.
- **Cloud KMS** (AWS KMS, Azure Key Vault, Google Cloud KMS): Managed key management with audit logging and rotation automation. Appropriate for most commercial workloads. Understand the shared responsibility boundary — customer-managed keys (CMKs) vs provider-managed keys have different risk profiles and data sovereignty implications.
- **Secrets managers** (HashiCorp Vault, AWS Secrets Manager): Handle application credentials and sensitive configuration. Not a substitute for an HSM where algorithm-level validation is required.

### Cryptographic Inventory (CBOM)

Before migrating to PQC, organisations must know what algorithms they are using and where. A **Cryptographic Bill of Materials (CBOM)** documents every algorithm, key length, protocol, library, certificate, and cryptographic dependency across the environment — analogous to an SBOM for software. This inventory is the necessary first step for any PQC migration programme and is required by ISM-1917 for Commonwealth entities. NIST's NCCoE PQC migration project provides tooling references and methodology.

### Password and Credential Storage

Passwords must never be stored as plaintext or using fast, general-purpose hash functions (MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256 alone). The correct approach is a purpose-built, slow password hashing function with a unique per-password salt:

- **Argon2id** (preferred — OWASP recommended, winner of the Password Hashing Competition)
- **bcrypt** (widely supported; acceptable)
- **scrypt** (memory-hard; acceptable)
- **PBKDF2** (acceptable for FIPS-validated environments only; use SHA-256 or SHA-512 with a high iteration count)

### Entropy and Randomness

Cryptographic operations depend on unpredictable randomness. A CSPRNG seeded with insufficient entropy is a catastrophic failure mode — predictable key generation has broken real-world systems. Use the OS-provided CSPRNG (`/dev/urandom`, `getrandom()`, `CryptGenRandom`, `BCryptGenRandom`). Never seed with predictable values (timestamps, PIDs, counters). Virtualised and cloud environments require attention to entropy pool availability at boot, particularly for ephemeral instances.

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Using deprecated algorithms**: MD5 and SHA-1 remain in use in legacy systems despite being broken for collision resistance. 3DES persists in payment terminals and legacy banking integrations. TLS 1.0 and 1.1 survive on forgotten internal services. Organisations often do not know the full extent of algorithm use across their environment — which is why CBOM matters.

**Rolling custom cryptography**: Developers implement their own encryption schemes, XOR-based obfuscation, or variant hash functions. Custom cryptography is almost always broken — the failure modes are subtle and non-obvious. Use vetted, peer-reviewed algorithms from established libraries (OpenSSL, libsodium, BouncyCastle, Go's `crypto` package). Never implement cryptographic primitives from scratch.

**Encryption without authentication**: Encrypting data without verifying its integrity allows an attacker to modify ciphertext in a controlled way (padding oracle attacks, bit-flipping attacks). Use AEAD modes (AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305) that provide confidentiality and integrity together. Do not combine raw encryption and MACs manually unless you have deep cryptographic expertise and a specific reason.

**Key reuse and poor randomness**: Using the same key for multiple purposes, or generating keys with weak or predictably seeded random number generators, undermines the security model. Keys must be purpose-specific, generated with CSPRNGs, and rotated on schedule.

**Certificate management failures**: Expired certificates causing production outages, wildcard certificates covering too broad a surface, private keys not rotated after personnel changes, and no monitoring of Certificate Transparency logs are routine findings in large environments.

**Inadequate revocation**: A certificate or key is only as trustworthy as the revocation process. Soft-fail OCSP (where the browser accepts a certificate even if OCSP is unreachable) provides weaker assurance than OCSP stapling or short-lived certificates. CRLs that are not checked promptly provide false assurance. Design revocation into the system, not as an afterthought.

**Treating PQC migration as a future problem**: HNDL is not a future threat — adversaries capturing traffic today will decrypt it once quantum capability arrives. Data that must remain confidential for ten or more years is already exposed if protected only by RSA or ECDH. The PSPF post-quantum cryptography requirement (introduced July 2025) mandates that approved PQC algorithms be used for newly procured cryptographic equipment and software — procurement is already a compliance gate. <!-- TODO: verify requirement number against current PSPF list of requirements --> Commonwealth entities must use approved PQC algorithms by 2030 under the ISM. APRA-regulated entities must assess PQC migration as an operational risk under CPS 230.

**Relying on encryption as a substitute for access control**: Encrypting data and placing the decryption key in the same location as the ciphertext provides minimal protection. Key management architecture determines the actual security level.

**Confusing encoding with encryption**: Base64, hex encoding, and URL encoding are not encryption — they provide no confidentiality. Data encoded in Base64 is trivially decoded. This appears regularly in application code as misguided obfuscation.

**Not planning for key compromise**: Many organisations have no documented procedure for responding to a key or CA compromise. Key compromise is a security incident. The incident response plan must include procedures for revocation, rotation, affected-party notification, and — in the case of CA compromise — trust store removal.

## Connections to Other Domains

- **Cyber Architecture (17)**: Algorithm agility, PKI trust models, HSM integration, hybrid PQC deployment patterns, and migration paths are architectural decisions that cannot be retrofitted cheaply. Cryptographic requirements must be designed in at the architecture level.
- **Network and Communication Security (7)**: TLS configuration, VPN protocols (IKEv2/IPsec), SSH key management, DNSSEC, and MACsec directly implement cryptographic controls. Cipher suite selection, forward secrecy enforcement, and protocol version control are network security decisions with cryptographic consequences.
- **Identity and Access Management (8)**: Certificate-based authentication, token signing (JWT, SAML), FIDO2/WebAuthn, and MFA hardware tokens all rely on cryptographic primitives. PKI is the backbone of certificate-based identity. Expired or compromised certificates directly break authentication controls.
- **Software Development Security (15)**: Developers must use approved libraries, handle key material correctly in code, avoid hardcoding secrets or keys in source, implement AEAD encryption, and apply correct password storage practices. Secure coding standards must address cryptographic requirements explicitly.
- **DevOps and DevSecOps (16)**: Secrets management in CI/CD pipelines, TLS for internal service mesh communication, signing of container images and build artefacts (Sigstore, cosign, Notary), and rotation of pipeline credentials all require cryptographic discipline. Hardcoded credentials in pipelines are a recurring finding.
- **Asset and Change Management (6)**: The CBOM — knowing which algorithms are used by which systems — is an asset management function. Certificate inventory and rotation schedules require asset register integration. Change control must include cryptographic impact assessment for library and dependency updates.
- **Cyber Risk Management (2)**: PQC migration risk, key compromise risk, cryptographic debt in legacy systems, and third-party cryptographic dependencies are material risks requiring formal treatment. The 2030 ISM deadline should be reflected in risk registers now.
- **Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance (3)**: The Australian Privacy Act 1988, ASD ISM, PSPF, SOCI Act, GDPR, PCI DSS v4.0.1, APRA CPS 234 and CPS 230, and sector-specific regulations mandate specific cryptographic controls. The PSPF post-quantum cryptography requirement makes PQC a procurement gate as of July 2025. <!-- TODO: verify requirement number against current PSPF list of requirements --> The ISM's 2030 PQC deadline and NIST SP 800-131A deprecation schedules directly affect compliance posture. Critical infrastructure entities must address cryptographic controls as part of their CIRMP cyber and information security hazard obligations.
- **Vendor Management (5)**: Third-party systems and SaaS platforms must meet the organisation's cryptographic standard. Procurement requirements should specify minimum TLS versions, approved algorithms, module validation levels, and PQC readiness timelines. Certificate management across vendor-operated systems is a recurring gap in third-party risk reviews.
- **Cloud Management (14)**: Cloud providers offer managed KMS, HSM services, and certificate management. Default settings are not always secure and must be explicitly configured. Key ownership (customer-managed vs provider-managed) has direct implications for data sovereignty, portability, and incident response.
- **Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning (13)**: Key and CA compromise are security incidents requiring immediate response — revocation, rotation, affected-party notification. The incident response plan must include specific procedures for cryptographic key compromise. Key backup and recovery procedures must be tested as part of BCP exercises to avoid permanent data loss.
- **LLM Security (19)**: Model APIs and inference endpoints expose sensitive data over network connections. TLS configuration and API key management are cryptographic concerns. Prompt injection via Base64-encoded or otherwise obfuscated instructions is an emerging attack vector.
- **Physical Security (20)**: HSMs and cryptographic hardware require physical access controls. FIPS 140-3 Level 3 and Level 4 devices include tamper evidence and tamper response mechanisms. Physical access to lower-assurance cryptographic devices can allow key extraction attacks. Physical destruction procedures for cryptographic hardware must be documented and verified.

## Australian Compliance Obligations

Cryptography sits at the intersection of several overlapping Australian regulatory frameworks. The obligations below apply to different populations — understanding which frameworks apply to your organisation is the starting point.

### Protective Security Policy Framework (PSPF)

The PSPF is published by the Department of Home Affairs and sets mandatory protective security requirements for non-corporate Commonwealth entities. It extends in practice to most contractors and suppliers handling Commonwealth data, as these obligations are typically passed down through contracts. PSPF Release 2026 took effect 1 July 2026 and is now the current release; Release 2025 was published 24 July 2025. Verify current requirements against the annual release page.

**How PSPF governs cryptography:**

The PSPF's Technology Domain (Section 13) requires that technology systems be authorised to the highest classification of information they process, store, or communicate. The ISM is the dependent technical manual that specifies _how_ that protection is achieved — including all cryptographic controls. Compliance with the PSPF technology domain therefore requires compliance with ISM cryptographic controls.

**Classification-based encryption requirements (PSPF Section 9.3):**

The PSPF sets minimum handling protections tied to the Australian Government Security Classification System. Encryption obligations scale with classification:

```
OFFICIAL              — Encryption recommended for public network transmission
OFFICIAL: Sensitive   — Encryption required for transmission over public or
                        insufficiently secure networks using AACPs; AES-256
                        (AACA) required for data at rest
PROTECTED             — ASD-Approved Cryptographic Protocol required; full
                        disk encryption using AACA mandatory; Common Criteria
                        evaluated cryptographic equipment required
SECRET                — High Assurance Cryptographic Equipment (HACE) required
TOP SECRET            — HACE required; additional ASD-specific communications
                        security doctrine applies
```

**PSPF Post-Quantum Cryptography requirement (new in PSPF 2025, Section 13.10.2):** <!-- TODO: verify requirement number against current PSPF list of requirements -->

Approved PQC cryptographic algorithms must be used for newly procured cryptographic equipment and software, in accordance with the ISM's guidelines for cryptography. This makes PQC readiness a procurement requirement now — not just a future migration target. Any new cryptographic system procured for a Commonwealth entity must support ASD-Approved PQC algorithms (ML-KEM and ML-DSA as adopted under ISM controls ISM-1990 to ISM-1995).

**IRAP assessments:**

Systems processing PROTECTED and above must be assessed by an Information Security Registered Assessors Program (IRAP) assessor against the ISM. Cryptographic controls — algorithm selection, key management, TLS configuration, and certificate management — are core areas of IRAP assessment. An IRAP assessment finding against cryptographic controls can block system authorisation.

**Who is subject to PSPF:**

- All non-corporate Commonwealth entities (departments and agencies)
- State and territory government entities that have adopted the PSPF or equivalent
- Contractors and suppliers to the Commonwealth who handle OFFICIAL: Sensitive or higher information — the obligation flows through contracts

### Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 (SOCI Act)

The SOCI Act (as amended most recently by the ERP Act, December 2024, and supporting rules effective April 2025) governs security obligations for responsible entities across 11 critical infrastructure sectors: energy, communications, data storage and processing, financial services and markets, water and sewerage, health care and medical, higher education and research, food and grocery, transport, space technology, and the defence industry.

**How SOCI governs cryptography:**

The SOCI Act does not prescribe specific cryptographic algorithms. Instead, it creates a risk-based obligation through the Critical Infrastructure Risk Management Program (CIRMP). Cryptographic controls are relevant to SOCI in three ways:

**1. Cyber and information security hazard vector (CIRMP Rules, Part 2A):**
Responsible entities must establish and maintain processes or systems that, so far as is reasonably practicable, minimise or eliminate any material risk of a cyber and information security hazard occurring, and mitigate the relevant impact on the CI asset. A "relevant impact" explicitly includes threats to the _confidentiality_ of asset information. Encryption of data at rest and in transit, key management, and access controls are direct controls for the confidentiality obligation.

**2. Data storage systems holding cryptographic material (ERP Act 2024, effective December 2024):**
The ERP Act explicitly confirmed that data storage systems holding _business critical data_ form part of a primary CI asset. The explanatory memorandum lists encryption keys, algorithms, and operational system code as examples of business critical data. This means the systems storing cryptographic key material are themselves subject to SOCI obligations — they must be secured, and risks to them identified and managed in the CIRMP.

**3. Telecommunications sector — confidentiality obligation (TSRMP Rules, effective April 2025):**
Responsible entities for critical telecommunications assets must protect those assets to ensure the _confidentiality of communications carried on and information contained on the asset_. Encryption is the primary technical control for this obligation.

**CIRMP cyber security framework requirement:**
From August 2024, responsible entities must align their cyber risk management approach with one of five recognised frameworks: the Essential Eight, NIST Cybersecurity Framework, ISO 27001, C2M2, or the AESCSF (energy sector). Each of these frameworks includes cryptographic controls as part of their control sets. Achieving the required maturity level under any of these frameworks implies adequate cryptographic hygiene.

**Incident reporting timelines:**
Significant cyber incidents affecting a CI asset must be reported to the ASD within 12 hours; other notifiable incidents within 72 hours. A key compromise that affects the confidentiality or integrity of a CI asset falls within scope. Incident response plans for CI entities must account for cryptographic incidents — key compromise, CA compromise, and unintended exposure of encrypted operational data.

**CIRMP annual reporting:**
Entities must submit a board-approved annual CIRMP report within 90 days of the end of their financial year. The report covers effectiveness of risk mitigation measures, including cyber controls. Cryptographic control gaps identified during the period should be reflected in this report.

**Who is subject to SOCI:**

- Responsible entities for critical infrastructure assets across the 11 sectors
- Data storage and processing providers whose systems store or process business critical data for a CI asset (must be notified by the CI entity)
- Critical telecommunications assets owned or operated by carriers or relevant carriage service providers (from April 2025)

Many organisations are subject to both frameworks simultaneously — a Commonwealth-contracted data centre operator, for example, may be a CI entity under SOCI and also subject to PSPF through its contracts. The ISM's cryptographic controls serve as a common technical baseline under both regimes.
