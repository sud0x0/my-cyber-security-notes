# Identity and Access Management

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Identity and Access Management (IAM) is the discipline of ensuring that the right entities — people, services, and devices — can access the right resources under the right conditions, and that every other combination is denied. It sits at the intersection of security, operations, and governance, touching every other part of the security programme simultaneously. IAM is not just about user accounts. It covers the full lifecycle of an identity: creation, proofing, credential issuance, authentication, authorisation, privilege management, federation, monitoring, and deprovisioning. It also extends to non-human identities: service accounts, application credentials, API keys, machine certificates, and increasingly AI agents. Get any stage wrong and the others can unravel.

The shift to hybrid work, cloud services, SaaS, and API-first architectures has made IAM more complex and more critical than it has ever been. Perimeter-based network security has largely collapsed as the primary control boundary. Identity is now the new perimeter.

---

## Why It Matters

**Credential compromise** is the leading initial access vector in breaches globally. The 2023 Okta breach, the Microsoft Exchange Online breach (2023), and the Snowflake customer account compromises (2024) all traced back to compromised credentials or failures in identity controls — not exotic exploits.

**Privilege abuse** — by external attackers and malicious insiders — is consistently enabled by over-provisioning. Accounts accumulating rights over time ("privilege creep") give attackers a much larger blast radius when credentials are stolen.

**Orphaned accounts** left active after staff leave or change roles are a persistent, easily exploited gap. Threat actors regularly compromise accounts belonging to former employees that IT never disabled.

**Lateral movement** depends heavily on identity misconfigurations. Once inside a network, an attacker pivots by exploiting poorly scoped service accounts, misconfigured Active Directory trust relationships, or credentials cached in memory (pass-the-hash, Kerberoasting).

**Non-human identity sprawl** is a growing risk. API keys, service account passwords, and machine credentials are often long-lived, broadly scoped, and stored insecurely. They outnumber human identities in most modern environments by an order of magnitude.

**Compliance failure** follows from poor IAM. Access reviews that cannot be demonstrated, privileged access that cannot be traced to an individual, and identity records that do not match HR data are routine audit findings under ISO 27001, SOC 2, PCI-DSS v4.0.1, and APRA CPS 234.

## Core Concepts

### IAAA: The Foundational Framework

All access control systems are built on four sequential services, commonly referred to as AAA — or IAAA when Identification is made explicit:

```
Identification   "I am Alice"          Claim an identity (username, employee ID, card)
      |
Authentication   "Prove you are Alice"  Verify the claim (password, token, biometric)
      |
Authorisation    "What can Alice do?"   Grant or deny access based on proven identity
      |
Accountability   "What did Alice do?"   Log and audit actions; enables non-repudiation
```

**Non-repudiation** is the property that ties an action to an identity such that the actor cannot credibly deny having performed it. This depends entirely on individual accounts — shared accounts destroy accountability entirely, which is why they should never be permitted.

### Identity vs Account

An identity represents a real-world entity. An account is the technical mechanism used to represent that identity in a system. One person may have many accounts (standard user, admin, service, shared). Good IAM maps every account back to a single accountable identity.

### Authentication Factors

Authentication factors fall into three categories. Combining two or more from different categories constitutes multi-factor authentication (MFA):

```
Type 1 — Something you know    Password, PIN, passphrase, security question
Type 2 — Something you have    Hardware token, smart card, OTP device, mobile push
Type 3 — Something you are     Fingerprint, retina, iris, face, voiceprint (biometrics)

Context-based attributes (used alongside factors, not as standalone replacements):
  Where you are    Geolocation, IP address, network segment
  What you do      Behavioural biometrics: typing cadence, mouse movement patterns
```

**Biometric error rates** matter in operational deployments:

- **False Rejection Rate (FRR) / Type I Error**: A legitimate user is rejected. High FRR produces frustrated users who look for workarounds.
- **False Acceptance Rate (FAR) / Type II Error**: An impostor is accepted. High FAR is a security failure.
- **Crossover Error Rate (CER)**: The sensitivity point where FRR equals FAR. A lower CER indicates a more accurate system and is the primary metric for comparing biometric implementations.

### Identity Proofing

Before an identity is created in a system, the claimed real-world identity must be verified. NIST SP 800-63A (part of the 800-63-4 suite) defines Identity Assurance Levels (IAL):

```
IAL1   Self-asserted. No real-world identity verification required.
IAL2   Evidence of real-world identity verified remotely or in-person.
IAL3   In-person proofing with a trained representative. Highest assurance.
```

Weak identity proofing is a persistent gap — particularly for contractor and vendor onboarding, where checks are often far less rigorous than for permanent employees.

### Authentication Assurance Levels (AAL)

NIST SP 800-63B defines the robustness of the authentication process:

```
AAL1   Single factor. A memorised secret (password) is acceptable.
AAL2   Multi-factor required. A phishing-resistant MFA option must be offered.
AAL3   Hardware cryptographic authenticator required. Phishing-resistant by design.
       Examples: FIDO2 security keys, smart cards with PKI certificates.
```

SMS OTP is no longer adequate for high-assurance contexts under NIST SP 800-63-4 (August 2025). All three major cloud providers (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud) mandated MFA for administrative access in 2024.

### Access Control Models

```
DAC (Discretionary Access Control)
  Owner decides who accesses their objects
  Each object has an access control list (ACL / DACL)
  Owners can delegate access to other subjects
  Risk: owners may over-share or make poor decisions
  Used: Windows NTFS ACLs, most Linux filesystems, macOS

MAC (Mandatory Access Control)
  System enforces access based on clearance vs classification
  Subject profile:  security clearance + need-to-know category
  Object label:     classification level + category
  Access granted only when: clearance >= classification
                        AND need-to-know includes the object's category
  Implements the Bell-LaPadula lattice: no read up, no write down
  Used: SELinux, specialised government/defence systems

RBAC (Role-Based Access Control)
  Permissions assigned to roles; users assigned to roles
  Administrator assigns: user --> role --> permissions
  Not directly:          user --> permissions
  Scalable and auditable; the most widely deployed model
  Risk: role explosion, accumulated roles over time (privilege creep)
  Used: Active Directory, most enterprise applications

         [Users]       [Roles]      [Permissions]
           Alice  -->  Analyst  <-- Read reports
           Bob    -->  Admin    <-- Modify configs
           Carol  -->  Analyst

Rule-Based Access Control
  Access governed by static rules independent of identity
  Examples: time-of-day restrictions, source IP filtering
  Often implemented in firewalls, NAC, and proxy systems

ABAC (Attribute-Based Access Control)
  Access decisions evaluate multiple attribute sets simultaneously:
    Subject attributes  (role, department, clearance, location, device)
    Resource attributes (classification, owner, sensitivity)
    Environmental       (time of day, device posture, risk score)
  Most flexible and granular model; policy complexity rises accordingly
  Used: AWS IAM policies, Azure RBAC conditions, XACML policy engines

Adaptive / Risk-Based Authentication
  Access decisions dynamically adjusted based on real-time risk signals:
    Login location anomaly, device health, unusual behaviour patterns,
    threat intelligence feeds, time since last strong authentication
  Example: step-up authentication triggered by a login from a new country
  The operational foundation of Zero Trust implementations
```

### The IAM Lifecycle (Joiner-Mover-Leaver)

```
[JOINER]                  [MOVER]                 [LEAVER]
    |                        |                         |
HR onboarding           Role change              Termination / resignation
    |                        |                         |
Create identity         Modify entitlements       Suspend immediately
    |                        |                         |
Provision accounts      Revoke previous role      Revoke all access
    |                        |                         |
Enrol MFA               Grant new role            Archive audit logs
    |                        |
              Periodic access reviews
              (certification campaigns)
```

The JML process is the operational backbone of IAM. Its reliability depends on HR system integration. If HR and IAM are disconnected, manual processes fill the gap and accounts are missed. The first trigger for deprovisioning in some organisations is a security incident rather than an offboarding process — this is a governance failure.

### Least Privilege and Need-to-Know

Least privilege: grant only the access needed to perform a defined function. Need-to-know: even within an access level, limit exposure to the specific information required for the task. Neither is a one-time decision — both require periodic review because roles and responsibilities change.

### Separation of Duties (SoD)

No single identity should be able to complete a high-risk transaction end-to-end without a second person's involvement. The person who can approve a payment should not be able to execute it. SoD is a core control in financial systems (SOX, PCI-DSS) and is frequently violated by accumulated role assignments that build up over time.

### Privileged Access

Privileged accounts — administrative, root, or elevated — are the highest-value targets. PAM disciplines these accounts through:

- **Credential vaulting**: passwords stored in a vault, checked out per-session, automatically rotated after use
- **Session recording**: all privileged sessions captured for audit and forensic purposes
- **Just-in-time (JIT) access**: privileges granted on demand for a defined window, not permanently
- **Just-enough access (JEA)**: the specific commands or functions available during a privileged session are scoped to the task
- **Break-glass accounts**: emergency access accounts with strong controls on activation, notification, and mandatory post-use review

### Non-Human Identities

Service accounts, API keys, machine certificates, OAuth client credentials, and CI/CD pipeline tokens are non-human identities. They typically outnumber human identities and are frequently:

- Long-lived (passwords never rotated, tokens never expiring)
- Broadly scoped (granted permissions far beyond actual need)
- Poorly governed (no owner, excluded from access reviews)
- Stored insecurely (hardcoded in source code, committed to repositories)

Best practice: short-lived credentials via OIDC workload identity federation for CI/CD pipelines (eliminates static secrets entirely), secrets management tooling (HashiCorp Vault, AWS Secrets Manager, Azure Key Vault) for everything else, and explicit ownership of every non-human credential in the identity inventory.

### Centralised vs Decentralised Access Control

```
Centralised      Single authority manages all access policy and decisions.
                 Consistent enforcement; easier to audit; single point of failure.
                 Examples: Active Directory, Okta, Azure Entra ID

Decentralised    Resource owners make local access decisions (DAC model).
                 Flexible but inconsistent policy; difficult to audit at scale.
                 Common for data owner-controlled access to specific datasets.

Hybrid           Central policy with delegated local administration.
                 Most enterprise environments operate a hybrid model.
```

### Zero Trust and IAM

Zero Trust is not a product; it is a design principle: never trust, always verify, assume breach. IAM is the primary execution point — every access request must be authenticated and authorised based on identity, device health, location, and context, regardless of network position. The network is not a trust boundary. Continuous evaluation replaces a single authentication event at the perimeter.

## How It Works in Practice

### Roles in an IAM Programme

- **Identity / Business Owner**: Approves access to their resources; completes access review certifications. This is the most important role and often the weakest link — owners do not take reviews seriously without real accountability structures.
- **IAM Team**: Operates the identity platform, manages JML processes, provisioning workflows, and access review campaigns.
- **Help Desk**: Front-line for password resets, account unlocks, and MFA recovery — a high-risk function that requires strong identity verification before any account action. Help desk social engineering is a primary attack vector (the MGM Resorts 2023 breach began with a vishing call to the help desk).
- **Security Operations**: Monitors IAM events for anomalies — impossible travel, off-hours privileged access, privilege escalation, MFA fatigue/bypass attempts.
- **HR**: The authoritative source of identity state. Joiners, movers, and leavers must flow from HR into the IAM system automatically — not through email chains.
- **Audit / Risk**: Reviews access certifications, validates SoD controls, assesses PAM programme effectiveness, and provides independent assurance to the board.

### Core Operational Processes

**1. Provisioning**: Account creation linked to an HR event or an approved access request. Role-based baseline provisioning via RBAC reduces per-user decisions and delays. Automation eliminates human error.

**2. Access Request and Approval**: Formal workflow for access beyond the RBAC baseline. Owner approval is required for sensitive resources. Time-bound access should be the default for project or temporary needs.

**3. Periodic Access Reviews (Certification Campaigns)**: Systematic review of who has access to what, completed by resource owners on a defined schedule — quarterly for privileged accounts, annually for standard. This is a primary SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 audit artefact. Tooling that surfaces only changed or anomalous entitlements improves review quality over blanket confirmation of everything.

**4. Deprovisioning**: Triggered by HR termination or role change. For standard leavers: disable on the last day. For high-risk roles (IT admin, finance, HR, executives): disable when notice is given or when a high-risk departure is identified. Automation is materially more reliable than manual ticketing.

**5. Privileged Access Management**: Credential vaulting, session recording, JIT elevation, break-glass procedures, and service account governance. PAM tooling should be a foundational investment — without it, privileged access cannot be governed.

**6. MFA Deployment and Management**: Phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2/passkeys, hardware security keys) should be the baseline for all privileged access and sensitive applications. SMS OTP should be treated as a fallback of last resort, not a default. Passkey adoption accelerated materially in 2024–2025.

**7. Identity Governance (IGA)**: The overarching programme managing role definitions, SoD policies, access certification, and entitlement lifecycle. IGA tooling provides the audit trail that regulators and auditors require.

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Orphaned and dormant accounts** remain active months or years after staff leave. Automated deprovisioning linked to HR offboarding is the correct control. Many organisations rely on manual tickets that are raised late, or not at all.

**Privilege creep** accumulates through project access, temporary grants, and role changes where old access is never revoked. The JML process must include a "rescind prior access" step for movers, not only a "grant new access" step.

**Shared accounts and service accounts without owners** make accountability impossible. Five people using the same admin account means no individual action can be attributed. Service accounts frequently carry excessive rights, long-lived passwords, and no nominated owner.

**MFA gaps on high-value targets**: Requiring MFA only for the corporate VPN while cloud management consoles, IGA tooling, and backup infrastructure remain password-only is a persistent misconfiguration. Attackers pivot to the weakest authentication path available.

**Help desk as an attack vector**: Social engineering to reset MFA is a proven attack technique. Identity verification procedures for account recovery must be as strong as the authentication they are resetting. Vishing scripts targeting help desk staff are well-documented and widely used.

**Over-reliance on network location**: Assuming that presence on the corporate network is sufficient evidence of trusted identity is dangerous in hybrid and cloud environments. Network location is not identity.

**Federation misconfigurations**: Overly permissive SAML assertions, weak JWT validation, or unconditional trust of federated IdP claims can allow cross-tenant privilege escalation. SAML and OIDC configurations require dedicated security review — not just functional testing.

**Access reviews treated as compliance theatre**: Approvers clicking "confirm" for hundreds of entitlements they have not read provides false assurance. Effective reviews require manageable scope, business-relevant context, and real accountability for what is signed off.

**Non-human identity governance absent**: API keys, service account passwords, and pipeline tokens are rarely included in access reviews, rarely rotated, and frequently over-scoped. In cloud environments, misconfigured IAM roles on service principals and workload identities are a leading cause of breach.

**Password policy misalignment with current guidance**: NIST SP 800-63-4 explicitly advises against mandatory periodic password rotation unless there is evidence of compromise. Forcing frequent rotation leads to predictable patterns and user workarounds. Organisations enforcing 90-day rotation while not enforcing MFA have their priorities inverted.

## Connections to Other Domains

- **[Cyber Governance and Management (1)](01.%20Cyber%20Governance%20and%20Management.md):** IAM policy — acceptable use, access control, privilege management — requires executive ownership and board-level visibility. Access governance structures, including data and system ownership, are governance artefacts. Without governance, IAM devolves into operational firefighting.

- **[Cyber Risk Management (2)](02.%20Cyber%20Risk%20Management.md):** Excessive privilege, shared accounts, missing MFA, and ungoverned non-human identities are quantifiable risks. IAM control gaps belong in the risk register with treatment owners and timelines.

- **[Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance (3)](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md):** The Australian Privacy Act 1988 and GDPR require that access to personal data is restricted and auditable. PCI-DSS v4.0.1, APRA CPS 234, and SOX contain specific IAM requirements. Access logs are frequently required as legal evidence in litigation and regulatory investigations.

- **[Privacy and Data Protection (4)](04.%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Protection.md):** Data minimisation and purpose limitation apply directly to access design — only those who need to process personal data should have access to it. IAM is the enforcement mechanism for privacy-by-design.

- **[Vendor Management (5)](05.%20Vendor%20Management.md):** Third-party access is one of the highest-risk IAM scenarios. Vendors with standing privileged access represent a persistent supply chain risk. JIT vendor access with session recording and time-bound credentials should be the baseline.

- **[Asset and Change Management (6)](06.%20Asset%20and%20Change%20Management.md):** Access should reflect asset classification. Confidential and critical assets require stronger access controls and more frequent reviews. Change management records should document who had access during a change window and under what authorisation.

- **[Network and Communication Security (7)](07.%20Network%20and%20Communication%20Security.md):** Network segmentation (VLANs, micro-segmentation) and NAC implement access control at the network layer, complementing IAM at the identity layer. Zero Trust architectures align both — identity and network controls inform each other's decisions in real time.

- **[Cryptography (9)](09.%20Cryptography.md):** PKI underpins certificate-based authentication, smart card logon, FIDO2, and code signing. SSH key management, API tokens, and secrets management are applied cryptography problems that IAM must govern. Post-quantum cryptography migration will eventually require re-examining all cryptographic authenticators in the IAM stack.

- **[Cyber Testing (10)](10.%20Cyber%20Testing.md):** Penetration testing routinely exploits IAM weaknesses — password spraying, credential stuffing, Kerberoasting, pass-the-hash, Golden Ticket attacks, and misconfigured federation are standard attack paths. Red team exercises validate IAM controls in ways that configuration audits alone cannot.

- **[Threat Intelligence (11)](11.%20Threat%20Intelligence.md):** Credential stuffing, AiTM phishing, MFA fatigue attacks, and account takeover are identity-targeting threats. Threat intelligence informs authentication policy — for example, integrating Have I Been Pwned (HIBP) to block known compromised passwords at the directory level.

- **[Security Operations and Monitoring (12)](12.%20Security%20Operations%20and%20Monitoring.md):** SIEM rules and UEBA detect anomalous identity behaviour — impossible travel, off-hours privileged access, mass permission changes, and lateral movement. IAM event logs (authentication, authorisation failures, privilege escalation, account modifications) are among the highest-value SIEM inputs.

- **[Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning (13)](13.%20Incident%20Management%20and%20Business%20Continuity%20Planning.md):** Account compromise is a common incident trigger requiring account suspension, credential rotation, and forensic preservation of IAM logs. Break-glass emergency accounts require BCP-level planning — they must work when the primary IAM infrastructure is unavailable or compromised.

- **[Cloud Management (14)](14.%20Cloud%20Management.md):** Cloud IAM (AWS IAM, Azure RBAC, GCP IAM) introduces significant complexity — misconfigured roles, overly permissive policies, and unused service principal credentials are leading causes of cloud breaches. Multi-cloud environments compound the governance challenge and require deliberate cross-provider policy architecture.

- **[Software Development Security (15)](15.%20Software%20Development%20Security.md):** Applications implement their own authorisation logic, which must follow least privilege and SoD principles. OAuth 2.0 and OIDC are the standard protocols for delegating access between applications. Broken Access Control is the OWASP #1 risk. Hardcoded credentials and insecure secrets management are development-time IAM failures.

- **[DevOps and DevSecOps (16)](16.%20DevOps%20and%20DevSecOps.md):** CI/CD pipelines require service account governance and secrets management. OIDC workload identity federation eliminates static secrets in pipelines by issuing short-lived per-job tokens. Long-lived API keys in pipeline configurations should be treated as a critical finding during code review and security testing.

- **[Cyber Architecture (17)](17.%20Cyber%20Architecture.md):** IAM is a foundational architecture decision made early and changed rarely. Zero Trust architecture makes IAM the primary policy enforcement point. Directory design, federation trust models, PAM architecture, and identity assurance levels are security architecture deliverables — not operational decisions made after the fact.

- **[Cyber Culture and Education (18)](18.%20Cyber%20Culture%20and%20Education.md):** Phishing resistance depends on users understanding why they are enrolled in phishing-resistant MFA, not only how to use it. Access review quality depends on training resource owners to engage genuinely with the process. Social engineering exploits IAM by targeting the help desk — staff in those roles require specific, scenario-based social engineering awareness training.

- **[LLM Security (19)](19.%20LLM%20Security.md):** AI agents and LLM-powered tools require identities, credentials, and access scopes. Managing non-human identities for AI agents — applying least privilege, scoping API access, auditing tool calls — is an emerging and currently poorly governed IAM challenge. Prompt injection attacks can cause AI agents to act outside their intended scope, making authorisation controls for AI-accessible systems critical.

- **[Physical Security (20)](20.%20Physical%20Security.md):** Physical access control systems increasingly integrate with IAM — badge readers authenticate against directory services, visitor management creates temporary identities, and piggybacking defeats physical controls in the same way shared accounts defeat logical IAM. A terminated employee's building access should be revoked in the same JML process that disables their network account.

## Australian Compliance Requirements

_Full regulatory detail is in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md). Volatile figures are in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md)._


Australian organisations face a layered set of compliance obligations directly tied to IAM. The applicable frameworks depend on sector, whether the organisation handles Commonwealth information, and whether it owns or operates critical infrastructure.

### PSPF (Protective Security Policy Framework) — Release 2025

The PSPF is mandatory for non-corporate Commonwealth entities and applies in practice to most contractors and suppliers handling Commonwealth data. IAM obligations under the PSPF sit primarily in the personnel security and technology security domains.

**Personnel security requirements directly affecting IAM:**

- Access to systems and information is granted based on security clearance level and need-to-know — these two criteria must both be met, not just one.
- The Australian Government classification scheme (OFFICIAL, OFFICIAL: Sensitive, PROTECTED, SECRET, TOP SECRET) determines the minimum clearance required before access is granted. Caveats such as AUSTEO (Australian Eyes Only) and AGAO (Australian Government Access Only) impose additional need-to-know restrictions on top of classification.
- Foreign nationals accessing systems that process AUSTEO, AGAO, or REL (Releasable To) data must be specifically identified by nationality (ISM-0420).
- Personnel must be uniquely identifiable — shared accounts are not permitted except under strictly controlled circumstances, and even then users must remain individually identifiable (ISM-0414, ISM-0415).
- Contractors must be identified as such in identity records (ISM-1583).

**Technology security requirements — Zero Trust mandate:**

- PSPF 2025 formally mandates a Zero Trust culture across government (the Zero Trust Culture requirement, with amendments to the CISO appointment, CISO accountability, and cyber security strategy requirements). <!-- TODO: verify requirement number against current PSPF list of requirements --> This is not purely a technical control requirement — it is an organisational commitment to ongoing verification, minimum access, and data-centric protection.
- Entities must develop, implement, and maintain a cybersecurity strategy and uplift plan aligned to the ISM and the Guiding Principles for a Zero Trust Culture (Req. 0098).
- PSPF 2025 introduced new content on AI, quantum computing, and connected peripheral technologies — all with IAM implications for how non-human identities and emerging access surfaces are governed.

**Reporting:** Non-corporate Commonwealth entities must report PSPF compliance maturity annually. CISO accountability and reporting frequency have been increased in the 2025 release.

### ASD Information Security Manual (ISM)

The ISM is the primary technical control reference for Commonwealth entities and is referenced by the PSPF. It is updated quarterly; verify the current release at cyber.gov.au. IAM-relevant principles and controls include:

**Cybersecurity principles directly covering IAM:**

- PRO-11: Only trustworthy personnel are granted access to systems.
- PRO-12: Personnel and services are granted the minimum access required to undertake their duties (least privilege).
- PRO-13: Robust and secure identity, credential and access management is used to control access to systems.

**Key IAM controls from the ISM:**

**Break-glass accounts:** The ISM specifically requires that use of break-glass accounts is documented, including who used them, the reason, and all administrative activities performed. Credentials must be changed and tested after any authorised use by a party other than the custodian. Break-glass events must be centrally logged.

**IRAP assessment:** Systems holding OFFICIAL: Sensitive, PROTECTED, and SECRET information must undergo security assessment by the organisation's own assessors or an Infosec Registered Assessors Program (IRAP) assessor. IAM controls are a core component of IRAP assessments.

### ASD Essential Eight — IAM Maturity Levels

The Essential Eight contains two controls directly governing IAM, with maturity level requirements that are mandatory for government and form the recognised framework baseline under SOCI CIRMP rules:

```
Restrict Administrative Privileges (Essential Eight #5)
  ML1   Requests for admin access are validated and approved; separate accounts
        for admin and standard use; admin accounts not used for email or browsing
  ML2   Admin accounts are disabled after 12 months if not revalidated;
        privileged access to systems is limited to only what is required
  ML3   Privileged access to systems is just-in-time; admin activities are logged
        and protected from modification and deletion; PAM solution in use

Multi-Factor Authentication (Essential Eight #6)
  ML1   MFA for remote access; MFA for privileged accounts
  ML2   MFA for all users accessing internet-facing services; phishing-resistant
        MFA for admin accounts
  ML3   Phishing-resistant MFA for all users; MFA events are logged and monitored
```

The Essential Eight Maturity Model is used as the approved cybersecurity framework baseline under SOCI CIRMP Rules (Section 8) for critical infrastructure entities that select it as their designated framework.

### SOCI Act 2018 (as amended) — Critical Infrastructure

The Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018, as amended by the Security of Critical Infrastructure and Other Legislation Amendment (Enhanced Response and Prevention) Act 2024 (ERP Act), imposes IAM obligations on responsible entities across 22 critical infrastructure asset classes, including electricity, gas, water, ports, banking, health, and telecommunications.

**Scope of IAM obligations under SOCI:**

The Critical Infrastructure Risk Management Program (CIRMP) must address cyber and information security hazards. Under Section 8 of the CIRMP Rules, responsible entities must establish and maintain processes to:

- Implement user identification, authentication, and authorisation controls to ensure only authorised individuals access critical systems.
- Apply RBAC to assign access only as needed, support access reviews, and enforce SoD.
- Maintain audit capabilities — monitoring user activity to detect breaches, misuse, or abuse.
- Automate identity lifecycle management covering employee onboarding, role changes, and offboarding.
- MFA or passwordless authentication is required for all users with access to critical infrastructure systems under current guidance.
- Real-time behavioural analysis and alerting for anomalous access is required.

**Systems of National Significance (SoNS)** face enhanced cyber security obligations on top of CIRMP, including the requirement to provide system information (including all identity and access logging) to the Australian Signals Directorate on request and to maintain cybersecurity incident response plans that include identity-related scenarios.

**Key compliance dates:**

- CIRMP cyber security framework obligation: effective August 2024; first annual board-approved report due September 2024.
- ERP Act amendments: took effect December 2024; data storage system and telecommunications sector reforms operational from April 2025.
- Ransomware payment reporting under the Cyber Security Act 2024: commenced May 2025 for entities with turnover above AUD $3 million.

**Annual reporting:** CIRMP annual reports must be approved by the board, council, or other governing body and submitted within 90 days of the end of each financial year. The report must confirm whether the program was current, detail any variations, and describe how it was effective in mitigating hazard impacts during the year.

**Recognised frameworks under CIRMP Rules:** Responsible entities may select one of five designated frameworks: AESCSF (energy sector), NIST CSF, ISO 27001/27002, Essential Eight, or C2M2, plus an equivalent-framework pathway. IAM controls are assessed under whichever framework is selected.

### Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles

The Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) requires that access to personal information is limited to those with a legitimate need. The Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) impose access design obligations:

- APP 6 (Use or disclosure of personal information): Access must be limited to purposes for which information was collected. IAM must enforce purpose-based access scoping.
- APP 11 (Security of personal information): Reasonable steps to protect personal information include access controls — the absence of least privilege or access reviews is a potential APP 11 breach.
- The Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme under Part IIIC requires notification of breaches involving personal information that are likely to cause serious harm — most credential compromise incidents involving personal data will trigger this obligation.

The Privacy Act reforms introduced by the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 strengthen security obligations and increase penalties, making IAM failures more consequential for regulated entities.
