# Cyber Culture and Education

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Cyber culture and education encompasses the people-side of security: building the attitudes, behaviours, knowledge, and workforce capability that make technical controls actually work in practice. NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1 (September 2024), now titled _Building a Cybersecurity and Privacy Learning Program_, formalises this as a three-tier model and integrates privacy learning as a co-equal discipline. It spans three distinct but interconnected activities, awareness, training, and education, defined in Core Concepts.

This domain sits at the intersection of human resources, risk management, and security governance. Without it, every other security investment is contingent on people doing the right thing by accident or instinct. In Australia it carries direct regulatory weight under the PSPF and the SOCI CIRMP personnel-hazard rules, and the 2023–2030 Cyber Security Strategy makes workforce development a national priority, all detailed in the compliance section below.

---

## Why It Matters

People, not technology, are where most breaches begin, which is why culture and education are a control layer rather than a soft extra. The evidence is consistent year on year.

The Verizon 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report (DBIR), based on over 22,000 incidents and 12,195 confirmed breaches, found that approximately 60% of all confirmed breaches involved a human element — through error, manipulation, or misuse. That proportion has remained consistent year over year, demonstrating that technical controls alone do not resolve the human problem.

The SANS 2025 Security Awareness Report — drawing on over 2,700 practitioners across 70+ countries — found that 80% of organisations rank social engineering as the number one human-related risk, now amplified by AI-generated deepfakes and voice cloning. Phishing remains the leading vector; incorrect handling of sensitive data has moved to second place.

**What goes wrong without it:**

- Phishing succeeds not because employees are unaware of it as a concept, but because they lack habituated scrutiny to catch convincing, AI-personalised lures in operational context
- Insider threats — negligent or malicious — go undetected longer in organisations without a speak-up culture or clear reporting channels; the DBIR 2025 found that in EMEA, nearly 29% of breaches originated from internal actors
- Security controls are bypassed informally when staff perceive them as friction; shadow IT and credential sharing are cultural failures before they are technical ones
- Privileged users carry disproportionate risk when role-specific training is absent or generic
- Compliance-tick training — annual, passive, clicked through in 15 minutes — creates legal cover but produces no behaviour change; this is confirmed every time a breach post-mortem reveals 100% completion records alongside phishing-driven compromise
- The 2025 ISC2 Cybersecurity Workforce Study found 88% of respondents experienced at least one significant security consequence from a skills deficiency within their team; 69% experienced more than one

**Real-world examples of culture and training failure:**

- **Twitter (2020):** Phone-based social engineering of Twitter employees enabled account takeovers of high-profile accounts. Attackers bypassed all technical controls by manipulating staff directly — a pure culture and training failure.
- **Uber (2022):** An attacker impersonating IT support convinced a contractor to approve an MFA push notification, granting full network access. No technical exploit — solely human manipulation.
- **Snowflake breach (2024):** Multiple major customers including AT&T and Ticketmaster were compromised because MFA was not enforced and credentials had been harvested via infostealers. The DBIR 2025 cited MFA enforcement and credential hygiene awareness as the key preventive measures that were absent.
- **Optus (2022) / Medibank (2022):** These Australian breaches — exposing the data of tens of millions of Australians — directly prompted the 2023–2030 Australian Cyber Security Strategy and subsequent legislative reforms. They underscored that security culture and incident response preparedness failures have national consequences.

## Core Concepts

**Awareness, training, and education.** The domain spans three distinct but interconnected activities:

- **Awareness** — broad-based communication aimed at shifting attention and attitude across the entire workforce; focuses attention on an issue rather than teaching a skill
- **Training** — role-specific skill development targeted at people whose work carries direct security responsibilities; seeks to produce competency in a specific function
- **Education** — deeper, career-oriented learning that develops security professionals over time; builds judgment and expertise rather than task-specific skills

**Culture is not training.** Training transfers knowledge and skills. Culture determines what people actually do when the choice is inconvenient, unobserved, or ambiguous. A strong security culture exists when employees report anomalies without prompting, question unusual requests instinctively, and treat security as part of their job rather than an imposition from IT. The PSPF articulates this explicitly as one of its five foundational principles: _developing and fostering a positive security culture is critical to security outcomes._

**The awareness-training-education continuum** requires all three tiers to function in combination. Phishing simulations build reflexes. Role-based training builds skills. Professional education builds judgment. Confusing or substituting one for another produces gaps — most commonly, organisations run awareness-only programmes and call them training.

**Role-based differentiation is not optional.** A finance officer needs to recognise BEC and invoice fraud. A developer needs secure coding practice. A sysadmin needs privileged access hygiene. A board member needs cyber risk framed in business terms. Under the PSPF, the distinction between general awareness training and _targeted training for specialist or high-risk personnel_ is encoded as two separate mandatory requirements (the security awareness training and targeted training requirements respectively in PSPF Release 2025).

**Behaviour change is the measure, not completion.** A 100% training completion rate means nothing if phishing click rates remain unchanged, incidents go unreported, or staff share credentials routinely. The DBIR 2025 found that user reporting rates increased fourfold after targeted training — that is the kind of outcome that matters.

**Culture as a model component.** The ISACA Business Model for Information Security (BMIS) treats culture as one of six core dynamic interconnections — alongside governance, architecture, human factors, emergence, and enabling and support — that directly shape how a security program functions and responds to change. A critical differentiator of the BMIS is that it places intentional security culture as a primary objective, not a byproduct of compliance.

**Risk culture shapes risk appetite.** The ISACA risk culture model recognises that organisations exist on a spectrum across three dimensions: risk-taking behaviour (conservative to aggressive), response to negative outcomes (blaming to learning), and compliance behaviour (non-compliant to actively compliant). Where an organisation sits on each dimension directly influences its security program design.

```
Risk Culture Dimensions (ISACA BMIS-derived)

  Risk-Taking Behaviour
  [Conservative / Risk-Averse] <-----------> [Aggressive / Risk-Taking]
                                  Informed

  Response to Negative Outcomes
  [Blaming Culture]            <-----------> [Learning Culture]

  Compliance Behaviour
  [Non-Compliant]              <-----------> [Actively Compliant]

  Target state: informed risk-taking + learning culture + active compliance
```

**Psychological safety is a prerequisite.** If the organisational response to an employee reporting a phishing click is blame or disciplinary action, the next employee stays silent. Near-misses — clicks caught, suspicious emails reported, unusual access flagged — are intelligence. Suppressing them by punishing disclosure is a direct operational liability.

**Adherence over compliance.** The ENISA Cybersecurity Culture Guidelines (2019) found that models enabling appropriate security behaviour were more effective than those using fear or punishment. ENISA recommends targeting adherence — active, willing participation — because novel threats require employees who can act independently when no existing policy covers the specific situation they face. This applies equally in Australian government contexts: the PSPF's requirement to foster a _positive_ security culture is substantively different from a requirement to achieve mere compliance.

**The workforce skills dimension is structural.** The cybersecurity talent crisis in 2025 is primarily a skills mismatch, not a headcount problem. The ISC2 2025 Workforce Study and SANS/GIAC 2025 Workforce Research Report independently found that 52% of cybersecurity leaders say the real issue is not the number of people but the lack of people with the right skills. Australia specifically needs an estimated 54,000 additional people skilled in cybersecurity operations and management by 2030 to meet demand (ACS, 2024 Horizon 2 submission). The 2023–2030 Australian Cyber Security Strategy identifies workforce development as one of its six national cyber shields.

**AI is a dual-edged force in this domain.** Attackers are using generative AI to craft hyper-personalised phishing lures, deepfake audio for CEO fraud, and automated social engineering at scale. Defenders are using AI to personalise training content and identify high-risk individuals. Both dynamics require awareness programs to evolve continuously — static annual training cannot keep pace with AI-accelerated social engineering.

## Australian Compliance Requirements in Detail

_Full regulatory detail is in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md). Volatile figures are in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md)._


### PSPF (Protective Security Policy Framework)

The PSPF — administered by the Department of Home Affairs and updated via an annual release cycle — sets mandatory protective security requirements for non-corporate Commonwealth entities and represents better practice for corporate Commonwealth entities, wholly-owned Commonwealth companies, and any organisation handling Commonwealth data.

**Security culture is a foundational PSPF principle.** The framework's five principles include: _"Developing and fostering a positive security culture is critical to security outcomes."_ This is not a supporting requirement — it is a foundational principle that underpins how all other PSPF requirements are to be implemented.

**Mandatory requirements directly relevant to this domain (PSPF Release 2025 — Security Planning, Incidents and Training):**

- **Security culture requirement:** The Accountable Authority and Chief Security Officer (CSO) must develop, implement, and maintain a program to foster a positive security culture in the entity and support the secure delivery of government business.
- **Security awareness training requirement:** Security awareness training must be provided to all personnel, including contractors, at engagement and annually thereafter.
- **Targeted training requirement:** Targeted security training must be provided to personnel, including contractors, in specialist or high-risk positions.

The distinction between the awareness training and targeted training requirements mirrors the awareness-versus-training continuum: all-staff baseline awareness is not sufficient for high-risk roles. PSPF Release 2025 also introduced a **Zero Trust Culture** requirement under the Technology domain (section 14.1.1 <!-- TODO: verify requirement number against current PSPF list of requirements -->), which mandates that entities develop, implement, and maintain a cybersecurity strategy and uplift plan aligned with the ISM and the _Guiding Principles to Embed a Zero Trust Culture_ — explicitly including continuous education and training as components of that culture.

**PSPF reporting shifted from maturity to compliance from November 2024.** Entities now report binary compliance against requirements rather than self-assessed maturity levels. This means awareness and training requirements are now directly auditable obligations, not aspirational targets.

**Practical implications for Australian Government entities:**

- Onboarding security awareness training is mandatory at engagement — not within 30 days, but at the point of access provisioning
- Contractors are explicitly in scope — entities cannot treat third-party personnel as outside the training obligation
- The CSO holds accountability for the culture program alongside the Accountable Authority — this needs to be reflected in the CSO's terms of reference and reporting line
- Incident reporting and security culture are linked: the ANAO has noted that significant security incident data is used to identify vulnerabilities in government security awareness training and inform whole-of-government outreach

### SOCI Act 2018 and CIRMP

The SOCI Act covers 11 critical infrastructure sectors: communications, data storage and processing, defence industry, energy, financial services and markets, food and grocery, healthcare and medical, higher education and research, space technology, transport, and water and sewerage. The Enhanced Response and Prevention Act 2024 (ERP Act) extended and strengthened obligations; the CISC began a compliance audit cycle in 2024–25 after the education-first period.

**Personnel hazard obligations under CIRMP Rules 2023:** Responsible entities must establish and maintain processes and systems to:

- Identify critical workers and their access to critical components of the asset
- Assess the suitability of critical workers before granting access
- Manage material risks from malicious or negligent personnel
- Implement effective onboarding and offboarding processes to control access
- Minimise or eliminate material risks arising from personnel

The CISC guidance references **workforce management security** (Part 10 of CIRMP guidance), **AS/NZS ISO/IEC 27002 — Human Resources Security**, and **AS 8000 — Fraud and Corruption Control** as supporting references. Insider threats are explicitly named as a personnel hazard category; security awareness training that reduces negligent insider risk is a material control within the personnel hazard domain.

**Proposed CIRMP enhancements (consultation paper, late 2025):** The Department of Home Affairs released a consultation paper proposing three new personnel security obligations, including requiring responsible entities to implement a personnel security plan, strengthen background checking, and explicitly assess personnel material risks through a foreign ownership, control, or influence (FOCI) lens. These are expected to take effect for most entities by 30 June 2028.

**Cybersecurity framework requirement:** From August 2024, CIRMP entities must align cyber risk management to one of five recognised frameworks: Essential Eight (Maturity Level 1 minimum), ISO/IEC 27001, NIST CSF, C2M2, or the AESCSF. All five frameworks include security awareness and training as a component.

**Systems of National Significance (SoNS):** Entities whose assets are declared SoNS face Enhanced Cyber Security Obligations (ECSO) on top of CIRMP, including mandatory cyber security exercises to test incident response capabilities — a form of mandatory simulation-based training.

### Australian Cyber Security Strategy 2023–2030

The strategy, released November 2023 and structured across three horizons, identifies workforce development as **Shield 5: Sovereign Cyber Industry and Workforce**.

Key commitments relevant to this domain:

- Growing and professionalising the national cyber workforce through expanded skills pipelines, improved diversity, and education and training reform
- More effective education and training systems in collaboration with Jobs and Skills Australia and the Jobs and Skills Council
- Encouraging cybersecurity careers at primary, secondary, and tertiary levels
- Accelerating investment in the domestic cyber industry and research capabilities

Horizon 1 (2023–25) focused on strengthening foundations including workforce baselines. Horizon 2 (2026–28) — currently in planning — is focused on scaling cyber maturity economy-wide, including workforce uplift. The ACS estimates Australia needs 54,000 additional cybersecurity professionals by 2030.

### Cyber Security Act 2024

The Cyber Security Act 2024 introduced mandatory ransomware payment reporting (effective May 2025 for entities over $3M turnover or SOCI entities), a Cyber Incident Review Board for post-incident no-fault reviews, and IoT security standards (staged to March 2026). The Cyber Review Board's post-incident review function has cultural implications: sharing root cause findings from major incidents publicly is a mechanism for driving industry-wide learning and awareness uplift.

## How It Works in Practice

### Program Ownership and Governance

The security awareness program typically sits under the CISO or information security manager, with delivery shared across the organisation. HR owns onboarding integration. Legal owns regulatory mapping. Communications assists with content design. Business unit managers drive accountability within their teams.

In Australian Government entities, accountability is explicit: the Accountable Authority and CSO are jointly responsible for the culture program under the PSPF security culture requirement. This must be reflected in governance documents, not just treated as a CISO initiative.

The SANS 2025 data found that a minimum of 2.8 dedicated FTEs is required to meaningfully shift user behaviour at scale, and four or more FTEs over five to ten years is needed to embed genuine cultural change. Under-resourcing is the most common reason programs stagnate at compliance level.

### Program Architecture

A functional program operates across four layers simultaneously:

```
LAYER 1 — All-Staff Awareness
  Purpose: Shift attention and attitude across the entire workforce
  Australian mandatory baseline:
    - At engagement (PSPF Req 24, applies to contractors too)
    - Annually thereafter
  Content includes:
    - Monthly phishing simulations with immediate contextual feedback
    - Short-form, timely communications tied to real, current threats
    - Physical security: tailgating, clean desk, visitor management
    - Smishing and vishing awareness (not just email phishing)
    - AI tool acceptable use and associated risks
    - Classification and protective marking obligations (for government)
    - Incident reporting: who to call, when, and why

LAYER 2 — Role-Based Training
  Purpose: Build specific skills for functions with elevated risk
  Australian mandatory baseline:
    - Specialist and high-risk personnel (PSPF Req 25)
    - Critical workers with access to critical asset components (CIRMP)
  Target groups and content:
    Finance / accounts payable:
      BEC, invoice fraud, wire transfer authorisation controls
    HR:
      Credential phishing, data handling, PII obligations under
      the Privacy Act 1988 and Australian Privacy Principles (APPs)
    Developers:
      OWASP Top 10, secure coding, secrets management, dependency
      hygiene, AI tool acceptable use
    Sysadmins / privileged users:
      Credential theft, MFA bypass, least-privilege hygiene,
      Essential Eight implementation expectations
    Executives / board:
      Cyber risk in business terms, PSPF Accountable Authority
      obligations, SOCI board sign-off requirements, deepfake
      and impersonation fraud
    Procurement / vendor management:
      CIRMP supply chain hazard obligations, FOCI risk
      assessment, third-party access controls

LAYER 3 — Security Team Development
  Purpose: Build and sustain technical expertise in the security function
  Key mechanisms:
    - Technical certifications and continuing professional development
    - Hands-on labs, Capture the Flag (CTF), red team exercises
    - Tabletop exercises and crisis simulations (mandatory for SoNS
      entities under ECSO)
    - Threat hunting and adversary emulation
    - Mentoring programs and career pathway planning
    - Rotation through different security functions

LAYER 4 — Leadership and Culture
  Purpose: Embed security as an organisational value
  Key mechanisms:
    - Regular CISO / CSO communication to all staff on the threat landscape
    - Board-level cyber briefings in business and risk language
    - Metrics reported in risk and business terms, not technical terms
    - Visible leadership participation in training (including board)
    - Positive reinforcement of secure behaviour
    - Post-incident learning reviews shared organisation-wide
    - No-blame reporting culture enforced visibly by senior leaders
```

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Treating the PSPF awareness and targeted training requirements as a single checkbox.** A single all-staff e-learning module satisfies neither requirement properly. The awareness training requirement demands a genuine awareness program at engagement and annually. The targeted training requirement demands substantively different, targeted training for high-risk personnel. Entities that produce one module and claim compliance with both are exposed to audit findings.

**Treating contractors as out of scope.** Both the PSPF awareness and targeted training requirements explicitly include contractors. CIRMP personnel hazard obligations extend to critical workers regardless of employment status. Supply chain personnel working on-site or with system access are within scope.

**Neglecting the CIRMP personnel hazard domain.** Many SOCI entities focus their CIRMP on cybersecurity framework compliance and physical security, leaving the personnel hazard section underdeveloped. The CISC's compliance posture moved from education to active audit in 2024–25. Personnel hazard controls — including insider risk management and onboarding/offboarding processes — are now auditable.

**Treating annual training as sufficient compliance.** The PSPF requires training at engagement and annually. The CIRMP requires ongoing management of personnel hazards. Neither framework treats annual-only training as adequate for high-risk roles or critical workers. Best practice — consistent with all relevant frameworks — is continuous, role-differentiated reinforcement throughout the year.

**Punishing reporters.** If the response to an employee reporting a click or a near-miss is blame or disciplinary action, the next employee stays silent. Near-misses are intelligence. Suppressing them by punishing disclosure creates invisible exposure that compounds over time.

**Measuring activity instead of outcomes.** Hours of training delivered, modules completed, and headcount reached are inputs. Behaviour change metrics — click rate trends, report rate, incident attribution — are outcomes. PSPF compliance reporting requires evidence; completion records without behaviour evidence do not demonstrate the program's effectiveness.

**Missing smishing and vishing.** Phishing simulations focused only on email miss growing attack vectors. The SANS 2025 report specifically identified smishing and vishing as growing in frequency and sophistication; training that only addresses email creates false coverage.

**Neglecting the Zero Trust Culture obligation (PSPF 2025 entities).** PSPF Release 2025 introduced a new mandatory requirement to embed a Zero Trust Culture aligned with the ISM and the Guiding Principles to Embed a Zero Trust Culture. This explicitly includes continuous education and training. Entities that have not mapped their awareness and training program against this new requirement may be non-compliant.

**No executive engagement.** A program existing only at staff level signals that security is IT overhead. Under the PSPF security culture requirement, the Accountable Authority is jointly accountable for the culture program — not just the CSO. Under SOCI, boards must approve and sign off on CIRMPs. These are governance obligations, not suggestions.

**Neglecting the security team's own development.** The ISC2 2025 study found 24% of security professionals lacked time or resources for staff training. Skills gaps in the security team translate directly to misconfigured controls, missed detections, and delayed response. The 2023–2030 Australian Cyber Security Strategy treats workforce development as a national priority precisely because this gap is acute domestically.

## Connections to Other Domains

- **[Cyber Governance and Management (1)](01.%20Cyber%20Governance%20and%20Management.md):** The PSPF security culture requirement places joint accountability for the security culture program with the Accountable Authority and CSO — this is a governance obligation, not a management one. Culture metrics belong in board reporting alongside technical posture indicators.

- **[Cyber Risk Management (2)](02.%20Cyber%20Risk%20Management.md):** Risk culture shapes risk appetite directly. The ISACA BMIS risk culture model maps to how organisations perceive and respond to risk. High-risk roles identified in the risk register should drive the training calendar. CIRMP requires a risk-based approach to all four hazard categories including personnel.

- **[Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance (3)](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md):** Australian compliance obligations are material: the PSPF security culture and training requirements are mandatory and now compliance-reported (not maturity-reported). CIRMP personnel hazard obligations are auditable from 2024–25. The Privacy Act 1988 and Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) add specific data handling training obligations for entities holding personal information. The Cyber Security Act 2024's ransomware reporting requirements need to be embedded in incident training.

- **[Privacy and Data Protection (4)](04.%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Protection.md):** Staff need to understand Australian Privacy Principles, the Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme under the Privacy Act 1988, and protective marking obligations under the PSPF. NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1 explicitly integrates privacy learning with security learning.

- **[Vendor Management (5)](05.%20Vendor%20Management.md):** PSPF contractor obligations mean vendor staff with access to Commonwealth data are within scope of training requirements. CIRMP supply chain hazard obligations require assessment of third-party personnel risks. Third-party involvement in breaches doubled in DBIR 2025.

- **[Asset and Change Management (6)](06.%20Asset%20and%20Change%20Management.md):** Personnel involved in change and configuration processes need specific training. CIRMP requires responsible entities to identify critical workers — those with access to critical components — which maps directly to privileged asset access management.

- **[Identity and Access Management (8)](08.%20Identity%20and%20Access%20Management.md):** Privileged users are the primary target of social engineering and credential theft. Role-specific training for administrators and privileged account holders directly reduces IAM risk. The PSPF targeted training requirement (for specialist and high-risk roles) applies directly to privileged access holders.

- **[Threat Intelligence (11)](11.%20Threat%20Intelligence.md):** The PSPF 2025 now requires entities to connect to the ASD's Cyber Threat Intelligence Sharing Platform. Threat intelligence from this platform should feed training content directly — when ACSC advisories identify active campaigns targeting Australian government entities or sectors, awareness content should be updated accordingly and not wait for the next annual cycle.

- **[Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning (13)](13.%20Incident%20Management%20and%20Business%20Continuity%20Planning.md):** The PSPF links incident management and training requirements within the same security planning, incidents and training area. Tabletop exercises and crisis simulations are mandatory for SoNS entities (SOCI ECSO) and best practice for all others. Post-incident reviews should feed back into training content.

- **[Software Development Security (15)](15.%20Software%20Development%20Security.md):** Developer security training — secure coding, OWASP Top 10, supply chain risk, secrets management — is a critical and distinct sub-domain of role-based education under the PSPF targeted training requirement.

- **[DevOps and DevSecOps (16)](16.%20DevOps%20and%20DevSecOps.md):** Shifting security left requires developers and operations staff to internalise security as craft, not governance friction. That shift is achieved through education and culture change, not tooling alone.

- **[LLM Security (19)](19.%20LLM%20Security.md):** DBIR 2025 found 15% of staff accessed generative AI tools, with 72% using personal email accounts — creating data exfiltration risk. PSPF entities handling OFFICIAL: Sensitive or classified information face specific risks from AI tool misuse. Awareness programs must address acceptable use of AI tools, data classification obligations when using AI, and the threat of AI-generated social engineering.

- **[Physical Security (20)](20.%20Physical%20Security.md):** Tailgating, piggybacking, physical social engineering, and visitor management are human-behaviour threats. The PSPF addresses these in its physical domain; awareness training for these behaviours belongs in the all-staff program.
