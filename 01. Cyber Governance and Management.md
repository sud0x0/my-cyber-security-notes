# Cyber Governance and Management

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Governance and management are not synonyms, and conflating them causes real organisational damage.

**Governance** is the system through which an organisation is directed and controlled at the highest level. It sets direction, defines accountability, establishes risk appetite, and asks: _Are we doing the right things, in the right way, getting them done well, and seeing the benefit?_ Governance is owned by the board and senior leadership, not by the security team.

**Management** translates governance direction into action. It plans, builds, operates, and monitors the security program day-to-day. The CISO and security leadership sit here.

**Compliance** monitors whether the controls and procedures implementing policy are actually being followed. It is a function of management, not a substitute for it.

Cyber governance sits at the intersection of corporate governance and information technology. In Australia, it is also a legal obligation across multiple regulatory regimes. The mechanisms by which an organisation ensures its security program is aligned to business objectives, resourced appropriately, and accountable for outcomes are not optional for any significant Australian entity.

---

## Why It Matters

When governance is absent or weak, predictable failures occur. All are documented in real incidents:

**Security treated as an IT problem.** When the board does not own cyber risk, investment is left to IT budget cycles and cut under cost pressure. At Equifax (2017), the CSO (Chief Security Officer) reported to the Chief Legal Officer, not the CIO or the board. This created an accountability gap: IT operational and security responsibilities were separated, with security personnel designing controls they had no direct authority over IT staff to implement. The board's Technology Committee received warnings about security weaknesses but failed to act. The breach exposed the personal information of approximately 148 million consumers. The US Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations found Equifax chose efficient business operations over security protocols. The House Oversight Committee described it as entirely preventable. When the CEO was eventually notified, he waited nearly three weeks before informing the full board, a delay that reflected the absence of established governance escalation paths. _Source: US House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, The Equifax Data Breach (December 2018) - <https://oversight.house.gov/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Equifax-Report.pdf>; US Government Accountability Office, GAO-18-559 (August 2018) - <https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-18-559>_

**Strategy disconnected from business.** Security programs not anchored to business objectives generate controls that protect the wrong things or at the wrong cost.

**No accountability for risk decisions.** Without defined risk ownership, risk decisions default to whoever is most technically confident, who may lack the authority or business context to make them.

**Metric theatre.** Organisations report on inputs (patches applied, training completions) rather than outcomes (risk reduction, detection capability), giving leadership a false picture of security posture.

**Regulatory exposure.** In Australia, the _Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018_ (SOCI Act), the _Cyber Security Act 2024_, APRA CPS 234, and the Protective Security Policy Framework (PSPF) all impose documented board-level accountability. Boards that cannot demonstrate active, documented governance face regulatory action, civil penalties, and, for listed entities, disclosure obligations.

**Legal liability.** Australian directors face personal liability exposure under s.180 of the _Corporations Act 2001_ for failure to exercise reasonable care and diligence in overseeing cyber risk. ASIC has explicitly stated that failing to ensure the organisation's risk management framework adequately addresses cybersecurity risk could constitute a breach of directors' duties, and has signalled its willingness to pursue enforcement action against boards that treat cyber as a peripheral concern. Ignored red flags, failed security audits, CISO warnings, and unpatched known vulnerabilities can support such action. The _ASIC v RI Advice Group_ (2022) case, while prosecuted under AFSL obligations, established that cybersecurity risk management is a licensee obligation and reinforced ASIC's appetite to pursue cyber-related governance failures. The US Caremark doctrine (Delaware, 1996) is sometimes cited as an analogy; it holds that boards breach their oversight duty by utterly failing to implement a reporting system, or by consciously ignoring red flags in a system they have established, but it has no direct legal force in Australia and should not be treated as equivalent to Australian directors' duties.

## Core Concepts

### The Governance Hierarchy

Security governance flows from business objectives downward. Each layer must be traceable to the one above it:

```
Business Objectives
        |
Information Security Strategy   <-- Roadmap, priorities, risk appetite
        |
Security Program                <-- Control objectives, resource allocation
        |
Security Policy                 <-- What must be done (mandatory)
        |
Standards / Baselines           <-- Compulsory rules / minimum security levels
        |
Procedures / Guidelines         <-- Step-by-step instructions / advisory
        |
Controls and Processes          <-- Technical and operational implementation
```

The architecture must implement the policies. The policies must reflect the strategy. The strategy must serve the business. Any layer not traceable to the one above is operating on assumption.

### The Governance–Management Distinction

```
GOVERNANCE (Board / Executive)
  - Set strategic direction
  - Define risk appetite and tolerance
  - Hold management accountable
  - Approve security program investment

        |  direction flows down; escalation flows up
        v

MANAGEMENT (CISO / Security Leadership)
  - Develop and execute the security strategy
  - Build, run, and monitor the program
  - Report on performance
  - Escalate risk decisions that exceed tolerance

        |
        v

COMPLIANCE
  - Verify controls operate as designed
  - Monitor adherence to policy
  - Report exceptions and non-compliance
```

### The Six Outcomes of Effective Security Governance (ISACA CISM)

1. **Strategic alignment:** Security objectives map to business objectives.
2. **Risk management:** Risk is identified, quantified, and treated within appetite.
3. **Value delivery:** Security investment is optimised and demonstrably returns value.
4. **Resource management:** People, technology, and budget are used efficiently.
5. **Performance measurement:** Progress and effectiveness are tracked with meaningful metrics.
6. **Assurance process integration:** Audit, compliance, and assurance functions work together.

### The Three Lines Model (IIA, 2020)

```
First Line   | Operational management
             | Business units, IT operations, system owners
             | -- Own and manage risk day-to-day

Second Line  | Risk and compliance functions
             | CISO function, risk team, legal, compliance
             | -- Challenge, oversee, and support the first line

Third Line   | Independent assurance
             | Internal audit, external audit
             | -- Independently assess governance and control effectiveness
```

The second line does not do the first line's job. When these boundaries blur, accountability disappears.

### Due Care and Due Diligence

The legal and ethical underpinning of security governance, related but distinct:

- **Due diligence:** the investigative, planning, and oversight activities a responsible person undertakes to understand a situation and make informed decisions. Top-down and continuous. Developing a security framework, conducting risk assessments, setting policy, reviewing vendor security are all acts of due diligence. It is what governance _does_. Under the PSPF, the Security Plan and CIRMP under SOCI are formal expressions of due diligence.

- **Due care:** the operational application of those policies; taking the reasonable precautions expected of a competent professional. Applying patches, enforcing access controls, running training, and responding to incidents are acts of due care. It is what management _does_.

Failure to exercise due care is **negligence**. Under APRA CPS 234 and the SOCI Act, failure to maintain information security capability (a due care obligation) can trigger regulatory intervention.

### Risk Appetite, Tolerance, and Capacity

```
Risk Appetite   -- How much risk the organisation is willing to accept
                   to pursue its objectives (strategic, board-set)

Risk Tolerance  -- Acceptable variation around the appetite statement
                   for a specific risk or objective (operational)

Risk Capacity   -- Maximum loss the organisation can absorb before
                   its continued existence is threatened
```

All three must be documented and reviewed regularly. Under the PSPF, the Security Plan must document the entity's risk tolerance. Under the SOCI CIRMP Rules, responsible entities must identify and manage all material risks to the asset. Risk decisions must reference these thresholds explicitly rather than treating the statement as a governance artefact.

### Policy Hierarchy

```
         Security Policy
         (What must be done)
                 |
    ┌────────────┼──────────────┬──────────────┐
    |            |              |              |
Standards    Procedures     Baselines      Guidelines
(Mandatory   (Step-by-step  (Minimum       (Recommended
 rules)       instructions)  security       practices)
                             levels)
```

Policies state what is required. Standards state the compulsory rules for how. Baselines define minimum security levels (the floor). Procedures give step-by-step instructions. Guidelines are advisory. Under APRA CPS 234, the board must ensure the entity maintains an information security policy that is reviewed at least annually.

### Metrics: KPI, KRI, and KCI

```
KCI (Key Control Indicator)         KPI (Key Performance Indicator)
  Measures whether a specific          Measures how well the security
  control is working as intended       program performs toward its goals
         |                                      ^
         |   feeds into                         |
         v                                      |
      KRI (Key Risk Indicator)  ----------->    |
        Leading signal that a risk is
        approaching or exceeding tolerance
```

All three should be SMART. Board reporting must use business-impact language (financial exposure, operational risk, regulatory consequence), not raw technical counts.

### Building a Security Strategy

1. Identify business requirements for information security.
2. Identify security objectives that satisfy those requirements.
3. Identify and value information assets.
4. Classify assets according to criticality and sensitivity.
5. Implement processes to ensure all assets have defined owners.
6. Define the gap between current state and desired state.
7. Develop the roadmap: priorities, sequencing, and resource requirements.

The strategy must align to business strategy. Under the PSPF, each entity must have a Security Plan approved by the Accountable Authority that details security goals, strategic objectives, threats and risks, risk tolerance, and how the entity will deliver against the PSPF requirements.

## Australian Regulatory Compliance Landscape

This section covers the governance obligations only. The full regulatory landscape is in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md).

### PSPF: Protective Security Policy Framework

_PSPF Release 2026 took effect 1 July 2026 and is the current release. The obligations below were introduced by Release 2025; verify current requirements against the annual release page._

The PSPF's six domains, its history, and its scope are covered in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md). For governance, the Governance domain is what matters: the Accountable Authority owns security risk, approves the Security Plan, and reports annually to the portfolio minister and Home Affairs. The governance policies mandate:

| PSPF Governance Requirement                         | What It Mandates                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Accountable Authority                               | The head of entity (Secretary, CEO, or equivalent) is accountable for security. They own the risk. They approve the Security Plan. They report annually to their minister and to Home Affairs.                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| Management Structures and Responsibilities          | Mandatory appointment of a **Chief Security Officer (CSO)** at appropriate seniority (SES is the standard), who chairs the Security Governance Committee. Mandatory appointment of a **CISO** at appropriate seniority (not required to be SES), who sits on the Committee and reports to or works closely with the CSO. |
| Security Planning and Risk Management               | Entity must maintain a written **Security Plan** approved by the Accountable Authority. Must detail: security goals and strategic objectives; threats, risks, and vulnerabilities; risk tolerance; and delivery against PSPF requirements.                                                                                                                                                                        |
| Security Maturity Monitoring / Compliance Reporting | Entities must monitor their implementation of PSPF requirements and report annually. PSPF Release 2024 shifted from maturity reporting to compliance reporting, a more demanding standard.                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| Reporting Security Incidents                        | Must report incidents to ASD for cyber matters, and to other relevant bodies based on the nature of the incident.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| Security for Contracted Services                    | Entity is accountable for security risks from procuring goods and services. Contracts must contain proportionate security terms and conditions. Contractors must comply with relevant PSPF requirements.                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| Security for International Sharing                  | Must adhere to international agreements to which Australia is a party.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |

Release 2025 added governance-relevant obligations carried into Release 2026: mandatory identification and reporting of Foreign Ownership, Control or Influence (FOCI) risks to Home Affairs, expanded CISO accountability and more frequent CISO reporting, and a mandated cybersecurity strategy and uplift plan. The PSPF–ISM–IRAP relationship (policy, controls, independent assessment) and the full list of Release 2025 changes are covered in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md).

### SOCI Act: Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 (as amended)

The SOCI Act's scope (sectors, asset classes, the three Positive Security Obligations, SoNS, and the 2024 amendments) is covered in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md). For governance, the point is accountability: the **CIRMP is a board-approved governance document**. The board or governing body must approve the CIRMP and its annual report to CISC (within 90 days of the financial year end), and remains accountable for the program being a living, tested capability rather than a filing exercise. Where a government agency also operates a critical infrastructure asset, it must satisfy both PSPF and SOCI obligations at once.

### Cyber Security Act 2024

The Cyber Security Act 2024 is covered in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md). For governance, the material obligation is ransomware response: entities above AUD $3 million turnover, and SOCI responsible entities, must report a ransomware or extortion payment to ASD within 72 hours. The decision to pay (or not) and the 72-hour reporting duty must be pre-assigned to a decision-maker, embedded in incident response procedures, and exercised at board level.

### APRA CPS 234: Information Security

CPS 234 applies to APRA-regulated entities (banks, insurers, superannuation trustees) and is widely used as a governance benchmark beyond financial services. Its scope and enforcement history are covered in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md). The governance obligations are:

| CPS 234 Requirement             | What It Mandates                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Board accountability            | The board is responsible for ensuring the entity maintains information security in a manner commensurate with the size and extent of threats. This is not delegatable.                                               |
| Defined roles                   | Clear information security roles and responsibilities across the three lines of defence.                                                                                                                             |
| Information security capability | The entity must maintain an information security capability commensurate with the size and nature of threats. Capability must be assessed against the entity's size, business context, and third-party dependencies. |
| Policy                          | A board-endorsed information security policy framework must be maintained and reviewed at least annually.                                                                                                            |
| Third-party management          | The entity must assess the information security capability of related parties and third-party providers that could adversely affect the entity.                                                                      |
| Incident notification           | Notify APRA within 72 hours of becoming aware of a material information security incident or a material weakness that has or could be exploited.                                                                     |
| Audit                           | Internal auditor must assess the adequacy of controls at least annually for higher-impact information assets.                                                                                                        |

### Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs)

The Privacy Act's coverage and reform timeline are in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md), and the privacy domain in depth is in [note 04](04.%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Protection.md). For governance, the obligation is APP 11: entities must take reasonable steps to protect personal information from misuse, interference, loss, and unauthorised access. The Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme requires notification to the OAIC and affected individuals when an eligible data breach is reasonably likely to result in serious harm. Governance structures must include data classification, access controls, and breach response pathways.

### ASD Essential Eight and the ISM

The Essential Eight and ISM are covered technically in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md) and in the domain notes that apply them. For governance, two points matter. First, ML2 is the mandated Essential Eight baseline for non-corporate Commonwealth entities, and from ML2 and above the Essential Eight requires documented incident response plans and reporting of cyber security incidents to the CISO or equivalent and to ASD. Second, the board sets the maturity target as a risk decision, not the security team alone.

### What Good Governance Looks Like for an Australian Entity

**Government entity (PSPF subject):**

- Accountable Authority has approved a current Security Plan that addresses all six PSPF security domains.
- A CSO at appropriate seniority (typically SES) is appointed, with a documented Security Governance Committee.
- A CISO is appointed at appropriate seniority, holds a security clearance appropriate to the role (unverified — confirm any minimum clearance level against the PSPF), and sits on the Security Governance Committee.
- Annual compliance reporting to Home Affairs is completed using the PSPF Reporting Portal.
- The entity participates in the ASD Cyber Security Partnership Program.
- A Technology Asset Stocktake covering all internet-facing systems has been completed.
- A Technology Security Risk Management Plan exists for all internet-facing systems.
- FOCI risks are identified and reported in procurement processes.
- ISM controls are applied proportionate to data classification levels.
- IRAP assessment is current for systems handling classified information.
- Privacy Act obligations (APP 11, NDB) are addressed within governance structures.

**Critical infrastructure operator (SOCI subject):**

- A written CIRMP is in place, covering all four hazard categories.
- The CIRMP aligns with one of the five designated cybersecurity frameworks (Essential Eight, NIST CSF, ISO 27001, AESCSF, C2M2) or an equivalent framework.
- The board or governing body has approved the CIRMP and the annual report.
- Annual CIRMP reports are submitted to CISC within 90 days of financial year end.
- Data storage systems used in connection with critical assets are included in the CIRMP.
- Incident reporting processes are in place: 12 hours for significant cyber incidents, 72 hours for others.
- Third-party and supply chain risks are addressed in the CIRMP.
- Privacy Act obligations (APP 11, NDB) are addressed within governance structures.

**APRA-regulated entity (CPS 234 subject):**

- The board has endorsed the information security policy framework (reviewed at least annually).
- Clear roles and responsibilities are documented across the three lines of defence.
- Information security capability is assessed at least annually and is commensurate with threat size.
- Third-party information security capability is assessed for all providers that could adversely affect the entity.
- Incident notification process to APRA is established: 72 hours for material incidents or material weaknesses.
- Privacy Act obligations (APP 11, NDB) are addressed within governance structures.

### Capability Maturity (CMMI / COBIT 2019)

```
Level 0 | Incomplete    -- Process does not exist or fails consistently
Level 1 | Initial       -- Ad hoc; success depends on individuals
Level 2 | Managed       -- Defined and repeatable at project or team level
Level 3 | Defined       -- Standardised across the organisation
Level 4 | Quantitative  -- Measured and actively managed using data
Level 5 | Optimising    -- Continuously improving based on performance data
```

Most mid-sized organisations operate at Level 2–3. For Essential Eight, maturity levels are separate (0–3) but conceptually parallel. The PSPF previously used a maturity model; from Release 2024 it shifted to compliance-based reporting, which is a more demanding standard, entities must now demonstrate that requirements are met, not merely that they have progressed toward meeting them.

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Security governance delegated entirely to IT.** When the board treats cyber as an IT problem, risk appetite is never formally set, investment is under-resourced, and accountability is diffuse. The Equifax case is the canonical example, the CSO reported to the Chief Legal Officer rather than having a direct line to the board, creating a structural accountability gap. Under the PSPF, the SOCI Act, and CPS 234, this is also a compliance failure, these frameworks explicitly assign accountability to the Accountable Authority or board, not to the IT function.

**Treating the PSPF annual report as a paperwork exercise.** The 2024 shift from maturity to compliance reporting means entities must demonstrate actual implementation, not just self-assessed progress. Reports are subject to quality assurance review by Home Affairs. CISC has directions powers to require remediation of seriously deficient CIRMPs.

**Policy without enforcement.** Organisations write policies to satisfy auditors and then do not monitor compliance. Policy that is not enforced creates false assurance.

**Confusing compliance with security.** Achieving PSPF compliance, Essential Eight ML2, or CPS 234 certification does not mean the organisation is secure. These are minimum standards. Ongoing risk management is required.

**CIRMP treated as a document rather than a program.** The SOCI CIRMP is not a filing exercise, it is a living program that must be reviewed and updated as risks change, and must be genuinely complied with across all four hazard categories. Regulators have noted that many initial CIRMPs were too generic.

**CISO without authority or seniority.** Under the PSPF, the CISO must be at an appropriate seniority level to make informed cyber security leadership decisions. A CISO buried in middle management cannot fulfil the role the PSPF prescribes, particularly as the 2025 release increases their accountability and reporting obligations. Note: the PSPF does not require the CISO to be at SES level (unlike the CSO), but seniority must be proportionate to the entity's size and complexity.

**Incident escalation paths not established.** At Equifax, the CEO waited nearly three weeks to inform the full board after confirming the breach. Under the SOCI Act, significant cyber incidents must be reported to ASD within 12 hours. Under CPS 234, APRA must be notified within 72 hours of a material incident. Governance must establish internal escalation timelines that support these external obligations, not just the external deadlines.

**Ransomware response not integrated into governance.** With the Cyber Security Act 2024 now in force, entities with turnover above $3 million must report ransomware payments within 72 hours. This obligation must be embedded in incident response procedures and exercised in tabletop scenarios at board level. Decision-making authority for whether to pay (or not) must be pre-defined.

**Risk appetite defined in theory, ignored in practice.** Many organisations document a risk appetite statement that nobody references when making actual security decisions. Risk appetite must be operationalised as a decision filter, including in CIRMP and Security Plan governance.

**FOCI risks not assessed in procurement.** PSPF Direction 001-2024 and PSPF 2025 now require entities to identify and manage Foreign Ownership, Control or Influence risks in technology procurement. This is a governance obligation, not just a security technical review. Procurement decisions for internet-facing technology now require a FOCI assessment.

**Privacy Act scope misunderstood.** A common error is treating the Privacy Act as applying only to private sector entities above the $3 million turnover threshold. In fact, most Australian Government agencies are covered regardless of turnover, and all private sector health service providers, including sole-practitioner GPs, pharmacists, and allied health professionals, are covered regardless of size.

## Connections to Other Domains

Cyber governance is the foundation from which all other security domains draw their authority and direction:

- **[Cyber Risk Management (2)](02.%20Cyber%20Risk%20Management.md):** Governance sets risk appetite (PSPF Security Plan, CPS 234, CIRMP). Risk management operationalises it. The CIRMP under SOCI is itself a governance document requiring board approval.
- **[Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance (3)](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md):** The SOCI Act, Cyber Security Act 2024, CPS 234, PSPF, and Privacy Act all impose governance obligations. Non-compliance is reported to the Accountable Authority (PSPF), to APRA (CPS 234), or to CISC (SOCI). Legal and regulatory requirements are a board-level responsibility.
- **[Privacy and Data Protection (4)](04.%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Protection.md):** The Privacy Act 1988 and APPs apply to most Australian Government agencies and all private health service providers regardless of turnover, as well as private sector entities above $3M turnover. Governance structures must address data classification, access controls, and the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme. Data classification under the PSPF (OFFICIAL, OFFICIAL: Sensitive, PROTECTED, SECRET, TOP SECRET) is a governance and information security obligation.
- **[Vendor Management (5)](05.%20Vendor%20Management.md):** The PSPF requires contracted providers to comply with relevant PSPF requirements. The SOCI Act requires CIRMP to address supply chain risks. CPS 234 requires assessment of third-party information security capability. FOCI risk management under PSPF 2025 directly applies to technology vendors.
- **[Asset and Change Management (6)](06.%20Asset%20and%20Change%20Management.md):** The PSPF Technology Asset Stocktake and the CIRMP asset register are governance outputs. Asset ownership decisions require Accountable Authority oversight.
- **[Network and Communication Security (7)](07.%20Network%20and%20Communication%20Security.md):** Network policies and ISM controls flow from governance direction. The PSPF Gateway Security Standard (released with PSPF 2025) applies to all internet-facing connectivity.
- **[Identity and Access Management (8)](08.%20Identity%20and%20Access%20Management.md):** The PSPF requires appropriate security clearances for access to classified information. CPS 234 access controls are a governance mandate.
- **[Cryptography (9)](09.%20Cryptography.md):** ISM cryptographic standards are referenced by the PSPF. PSPF 2025 introduces post-quantum cryptography requirements, a governance decision about when and how to transition.
- **[Cyber Testing (10)](10.%20Cyber%20Testing.md):** IRAP assessment of ICT systems against ISM controls is a PSPF/ISM governance requirement. CIRMP compliance may require penetration testing or framework assessments.
- **[Threat Intelligence (11)](11.%20Threat%20Intelligence.md):** PSPF 2025 mandates participation in the ASD Cyber Security Partnership Program and cyber threat intelligence sharing. This is now a governance obligation, not an optional activity.
- **[Security Operations and Monitoring (12)](12.%20Security%20Operations%20and%20Monitoring.md):** Escalation paths for SOCI incident reporting (12 hours, 72 hours) and CPS 234 APRA notification (72 hours) must be built into SOC operational procedures and backed by governance authority.
- **[Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning (13)](13.%20Incident%20Management%20and%20Business%20Continuity%20Planning.md):** Under PSPF Release 2024, entities must maintain a business continuity plan. Under SOCI, CIRMPs must address all hazard types. Under the Cyber Security Act 2024, ransomware payment decisions and 72-hour reporting must be integrated into incident response.
- **[Cloud Management (14)](14.%20Cloud%20Management.md):** The ISM and PSPF govern cloud adoption for government entities, including IRAP assessment requirements, the Hosting Certification Framework (currently under reform), and data sovereignty. The PSPF Gateway Security Standard and SSE (Security Service Edge) guidance apply to cloud-connected environments.
- **[Software Development Security (15)](15.%20Software%20Development%20Security.md):** PSPF information security requirements apply to systems throughout their lifecycle. ISM controls include requirements for secure development. System authorisation under PSPF/ISM requires an Authorising Officer to accept residual risks before a system can operate.
- **[DevOps and DevSecOps (16)](16.%20DevOps%20and%20DevSecOps.md):** COBIT 2019 includes a DevOps focus area. Security in CI/CD pipelines must meet ISM controls for systems handling government data.
- **[Cyber Architecture (17)](17.%20Cyber%20Architecture.md):** Security architecture must implement governance policy and ISM controls. IRAP assessors assess architecture against the ISM. The PSPF Security Plan and ISM authorisation requirements shape architecture decisions.
- **[Cyber Culture and Education (18)](18.%20Cyber%20Culture%20and%20Education.md):** PSPF requires security awareness training for all personnel and contractors. Zero Trust Culture is now a PSPF 2025 requirement; entities must have an uplift plan. Culture change requires board-level commitment.
- **[LLM Security (19)](19.%20LLM%20Security.md):** PSPF 2025 introduces new policy content on AI. Decisions on AI use, LLM integration, and data ingestion into AI systems require governance-level policy. FOCI risk assessment must include AI-related technology vendors.
- **[Physical Security (20)](20.%20Physical%20Security.md):** The PSPF Physical Security domain governs facility access, secure areas, and information handling in physical environments. Physical and cyber governance must be coordinated under the CSO.
- **OT Security (21):** Operational Technology environments (SCADA, ICS, PLCs) introduce governance obligations distinct from IT. Under the SOCI Act, many OT-dependent sectors (energy, water, transport) are critical infrastructure assets subject to CIRMP requirements. The board must ensure OT risks are addressed in the CIRMP alongside IT risks; they cannot be treated as separate programs. Governance must define ownership of OT assets, establish OT-specific incident escalation paths, and ensure the CIRMP covers OT-related hazards including physical-cyber interdependencies. The ISM provides OT-relevant controls, and IRAP assessment may apply to OT systems connected to government networks.
