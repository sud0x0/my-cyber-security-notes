# Cyber Risk Management

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Cyber risk management is the discipline of identifying, analysing, evaluating, and responding to risks that could affect the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of information and systems — in a way that is proportionate, documented, and aligned with organisational objectives. It sits at the intersection of security operations and business strategy. Without it, security investment is guesswork: you cannot know what to protect, how much to spend, or whether controls are working. Risk management converts technical findings into business language, enabling informed decisions by people who own the consequences.

In the Australian context, cyber risk management is no longer a discretionary discipline. For Commonwealth entities it is mandated by the Protective Security Policy Framework (PSPF). For critical infrastructure operators it is legally required under the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 (SOCI Act) and the Critical Infrastructure Risk Management Program (CIRMP) Rules. For any organisation holding personal information, the Privacy Act 1988 creates implicit risk management obligations tied to data breach liability. These frameworks are not separate compliance exercises — they all describe the same underlying practice.

---

## Why It Matters

When risk management is done poorly, the failures are predictable and expensive:

**Misallocated investment.** Security spend targets visible or familiar risks rather than what evidence says is most likely and damaging. Controls are built without reference to the risk register, and genuinely high-priority exposures go unaddressed.

**Compliance theatre replacing risk reduction.** Passing an audit does not mean risk has been managed — it means controls were present at a point in time. Target was PCI-DSS compliant when its 2013 breach occurred via a third-party HVAC vendor. The same failure mode applies to PSPF and CIRMP compliance: a submitted annual report is not evidence that risk is managed.

**Severity-score-driven vulnerability backlogs.** In 2024, over 40,000 new CVEs were published; only approximately 2% were exploited in the wild. Organisations that patch by CVSS score alone spend most of their remediation effort on vulnerabilities no attacker is actively using, while lower-scored vulnerabilities that are actively exploited sit further down the queue. This is a risk management failure, not a technical one.

**No risk ownership.** Without a named risk owner who has budget and authority, risk sits in a document and nothing gets done. Risk registers become audit artefacts. Under the PSPF and CIRMP, this is not just poor practice — it is non-compliance.

**Shock at residual risk.** Organisations implement controls without communicating what risk remains. When an incident occurs, leadership is genuinely surprised — because no one made explicit what accepting a risk actually meant.

**Third-party risk blindness.** Outsourcing does not transfer accountability. Third-party data breaches increased 49% year-on-year between 2023 and 2024 (SecurityScorecard, 2024). Under the SOCI Act, responsible entities remain accountable for risks arising from their supply chains. Under the PSPF, procurement-related risk — including Foreign Ownership, Control or Influence (FOCI) risk — is a mandatory management obligation.

Real-world consequences include regulatory fines, government-directed remediation under the SOCI Act, OAIC enforcement action under the Privacy Act (penalties for serious or repeated breaches of the greater of $50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover, introduced by the Privacy Legislation Amendment (Enforcement and Other Measures) Act 2022), and in critical infrastructure contexts, physical harm.

## Core Concepts

### Risk is not a threat, and CVSS is not a risk score

A threat is something that could happen. A vulnerability is the weakness that makes exploitation possible. Risk is the combination of how likely a threat event is and what it would cost if it did. Risk requires both dimensions.

**Risk = Likelihood × Impact**

CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) measures technical _severity_ — the theoretical worst-case impact of a vulnerability if exploited. It does not measure likelihood, asset criticality, business context, or network reachability. CVSS 4.0 (finalised late 2024) added an "Exploit Maturity" metric and split impact categories, moving somewhat closer to risk modelling — but it remains a severity indicator, not a risk indicator. A low-CVSS authentication bypass on an externally facing single sign-on gateway may be orders of magnitude riskier than a CVSS 9.8 buffer overflow in an isolated, air-gapped research network.

### Inherent, residual, and current risk

- **Inherent risk:** the risk level before any controls are applied — the baseline exposure from operating in a given environment.
- **Residual risk:** what remains after controls are implemented. This is what the organisation is actually living with.
- **Current risk:** the risk at a specific point in time, accounting for both controls in place and those still pending implementation.

The gap between inherent and residual risk is what controls are buying. If residual risk exceeds appetite, the organisation needs additional controls, a different response, or a documented acceptance decision.

### Risk appetite, tolerance, and capacity

- **Risk appetite:** the amount of risk the organisation is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives — a strategic statement set by leadership.
- **Risk tolerance:** the acceptable operational variation around that appetite — how far from the stated position management will allow before action is required.
- **Risk capacity:** the maximum loss the organisation can absorb without its continued existence being called into question.

These are distinct. Capacity sets the hard ceiling regardless of appetite. Under the PSPF, risk appetite must be formally established and documented at the entity level.

### Risk response options

### Quantitative, qualitative, and semi-quantitative assessment

**Quantitative** methods use numbers:

```
SLE = Asset Value (AV) × Exposure Factor (EF)
      [Single Loss Expectancy — estimated cost of one loss event]

ARO = Estimated losses per year
      [Annual Rate of Occurrence]

ALE = SLE × ARO
      [Annualised Loss Expectancy — expected annual cost of a risk]
```

Quantitative assessments support cost-benefit analysis and board-level communication but require reliable data. The FAIR model (Factor Analysis of Information Risk) extends this by decomposing risk into Loss Event Frequency and Loss Magnitude, with frequency driven by threat capability and asset resistance strength.

**Qualitative** methods assign ordinal values (High/Medium/Low, or 1–10) based on expert judgement. Faster and practical when data is scarce, but subjective and inconsistent without an anchored scale.

**Semi-quantitative** methods combine both: numerical scales (e.g., 0–100) derived from qualitative inputs. Useful when impact is quantifiable but likelihood is not. The NIST SP 800-30 five-level risk scale is a widely used reference and is compatible with ISM risk assessment methodology.

### Risk scenario development

A risk scenario describes a possible threat event whose occurrence will have an uncertain impact on organisational objectives.

**Threat scenario components:**

```
┌───────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Actor             │ Internal (staff, contractor), External           │
│                   │ (hacktivist, criminal, nation-state, competitor),│
│                   │ Regulator, Vendor, Nature                        │
├───────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Threat Type       │ Malicious, Accidental, Error, Failure, Flood,    │
│                   │ External requirement, Foreign interference       │
├───────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Event             │ Disclosure, Interruption, Modification, Theft,   │
│                   │ Destruction, Inappropriate use                   │
├───────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Asset/Resource    │ People, Organisational structures, Process,      │
│                   │ Facilities, IT infrastructure, Information,      │
│                   │ Applications, OT/ICS systems                     │
├───────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Frequency &       │ How often does this event occur?                 │
│ Loss Magnitude    │ What is the financial and operational impact?    │
└───────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Top-down scenarios** start from business goals and ask how a risk event would prevent their achievement. Meaningful to management; connects IT risk to business outcomes. Required for CIRMP "all-hazards" scoping.

**Bottom-up scenarios** start from specific technical or process situations developed by those closest to the work. Captures highly specific risks that top-down misses.

Both are needed. Top-down ensures relevance; bottom-up ensures completeness.

### Control types

```
BY NATURE:
  Administrative/Managerial — policies, procedures, training, governance
  Technical/Logical         — access controls, encryption, monitoring tools
  Physical/Operational      — locks, guards, environmental controls

BY FUNCTION:
  Preventive    — stop the event occurring (firewall, access control)
  Detective     — identify that an event has occurred (IDS, audit log)
  Corrective    — restore normal operations (backup restore, patching)
  Deterrent     — discourage threat actors (warning banners, CCTV)
  Compensating  — substitute when a primary control is not feasible
```

### Key metrics: KRIs, KCIs, KPIs

- **Key Risk Indicators (KRIs):** predictive signals that risk is increasing. Should combine lagging indicators (what has happened), leading indicators (what controls are in place), and trend data.
- **Key Control Indicators (KCIs):** diagnostic measures of whether a specific control is functioning. Examples: phishing emails not blocked by filters; administrators with rights to both test and production; unsolicited MFA prompts indicating password compromise reaching stage two.
- **Key Performance Indicators (KPIs):** measure progress toward security objectives. All three should use SMART metrics reviewed on a defined cadence.

### Capability maturity

A Capability Maturity Model (CMM) benchmarks the risk management programme itself across five levels: Initial (ad hoc, reactive), Repeatable (basic process discipline), Defined (documented and standardised), Managed (measured and controlled), Optimising (continuous improvement). The PSPF annual reporting process is itself a maturity-based self-assessment requiring entities to rate their implementation effectiveness — not merely tick a compliance box.

## Vulnerability Management and Risk-Based Prioritisation

Vulnerability management is a core risk management process — it is how technical exposures are identified, evaluated, and remediated. Done poorly, it generates a backlog of noise. Done well, it is a continuous, intelligence-driven feed into the enterprise risk register.

### The problem with CVSS-only prioritisation

Traditional vulnerability management ranks findings by CVSS score and patches downward. This fails for several reasons:

- Of 40,000+ CVEs published in 2024, only approximately 2% were exploited in the wild. CVSS-based queues include vast numbers of vulnerabilities no attacker is actively targeting.
- 28% of vulnerabilities actively exploited in Q1 2025 carried only "Medium" base CVSS scores.
- Attackers weaponise many flaws within hours or days of disclosure (unverified — confirm the specific time-to-exploitation figure against the current Mandiant M-Trends report). CVSS scores do not capture this velocity.
- CVSS does not consider asset criticality, network reachability, compensating controls, or actual exploitability in a specific environment.

Under PSPF Direction 002-2024, Commonwealth entities are required to identify and actively manage risks associated with vulnerable technologies — not simply scan and score them. exploitation of VPN and other edge-device vulnerabilities has repeatedly bypassed score-only prioritisation, which illustrates why score-only approaches are insufficient even in well-resourced environments.

### Risk-Based Vulnerability Management (RBVM)

RBVM prioritises remediation based on the actual risk a vulnerability presents to the organisation, not its theoretical severity. It incorporates:

- **Asset criticality:** a vulnerability on a customer-facing revenue system demands a different response than the same vulnerability on an isolated test machine. Under CIRMP, asset criticality mapping is a prerequisite for proportionate risk response.
- **Exploitability signals:** is there a weaponised exploit? Is it actively being used in the wild? Is the asset reachable from the threat environment?
- **Threat intelligence:** which threat actor groups are targeting this vulnerability? Which industries and sectors are being targeted? ASD's Annual Cyber Threat Report is a primary Australian source for this.
- **Business impact:** what would compromise of this asset cost the organisation in direct losses, regulatory exposure, and operational disruption?

The result is a contextual risk score per vulnerability instance — not a generic severity rating — that drives remediation prioritisation.

### The scoring ecosystem: CVSS, EPSS, KEV, and SSVC

```
┌───────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ SIGNAL        │ WHAT IT TELLS YOU                                     │
├───────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ CVSS 4.0      │ Technical severity. Worst-case impact if exploited.   │
│               │ Standardised but context-free. Severity ≠ risk.       │
├───────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ EPSS v4       │ Exploit Prediction Scoring System (FIRST, updated     │
│ (March 2025)  │ March 2025). ML-based probability (0–1) that a CVE    │
│               │ will be exploited in the next 30 days. Predictive,    │
│               │ not confirmatory. No score for zero-days until CVE    │
│               │ is published.                                         │
├───────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ CISA KEV      │ Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue. Confirmed  │
│               │ active exploitation in the wild. A medium CVSS on     │
│               │ KEV outranks most Critical CVEs not on KEV. Scanning  │
│               │ does not qualify as exploitation for KEV inclusion.   │
├───────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ SSVC          │ Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorisation     │
│               │ (CISA/CERT/CC). Decision tree model: exploitation     │
│               │ state, automatable exploitation, mission impact.      │
│               │ Produces actionable priority labels.                  │
├───────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ LEV           │ Likely Exploited Vulnerabilities (NIST, 2025).        │
│               │ Estimates probability a CVE has already been          │
│               │ exploited, based on historical EPSS patterns.         │
└───────────────┴───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Practical prioritisation logic:**

```
IF on CISA KEV → patch immediately regardless of CVSS
ELSE IF EPSS is high (e.g., >0.7) AND asset is internet-facing → urgent
ELSE IF CVSS is Critical AND weaponised exploit exists → high priority
ELSE IF asset criticality is high → elevate regardless of score
ELSE → normal maintenance cycle
```

Research published in 2025 found that combining KEV and EPSS alongside CVSS could reduce the urgent remediation workload by approximately 95% — from roughly 16,000 CVSS 7+ vulnerabilities down to approximately 850 with actual evidence or high exploitation probability.

### Converting technical findings to business risk

A scanner output list is not a risk register entry. Converting a vulnerability finding to a business risk requires:

1. **Asset valuation:** what is this system worth to the business? What processes depend on it? What data does it hold? For CIRMP entities, this maps to the "critical infrastructure asset" definition and its hazard categories.
2. **Exploitability assessment:** what is the actual likelihood of exploitation given the environment (network position, authentication requirements, available exploits, threat actor targeting)?
3. **Impact modelling:** direct costs (remediation, breach notification, legal), operational costs (downtime, recovery), secondary losses (regulatory fines, reputational damage, lost customers). For SOCI entities, also consider national-security-level consequences.
4. **Control effectiveness:** what compensating controls exist? Does MFA, segmentation, or EDR reduce the exploitability in this specific environment?
5. **Business risk statement:** express the finding in business terms — not "critical RCE in Apache server" but "unauthenticated remote code execution in the customer portal would allow an attacker to access the personal data of 1.2 million customers, triggering mandatory OAIC notification within 72 hours, with estimated remediation and legal costs of $X, and risk of penalty under the Privacy Act."

This conversion moves a vulnerability finding from the patching queue into the risk register as a managed risk with an owner, a response, and a treatment timeline.

### Continuous Threat Exposure Management (CTEM)

CTEM is a programme framework for continuously assessing and reducing the organisation's exploitable attack surface. It expands beyond traditional vulnerability scanning to include:

- **Scoping:** define what attack surfaces are in scope (internal, external, cloud, OT, identity, third-party).
- **Discovery:** continuous asset inventory and exposure identification, not periodic scans.
- **Prioritisation:** context-aware risk scoring incorporating threat intelligence, asset criticality, and exploitability.
- **Validation:** testing whether identified exposures are genuinely exploitable (breach and attack simulation, adversary emulation).
- **Mobilisation:** integrating findings into remediation workflows with clear ownership and SLAs.

CTEM is not a product — it is a programme. It represents the operationalisation of RBVM at enterprise scale, including cloud, identity, OT, and third-party attack surfaces that traditional scanner-based programmes miss.

## Australian Regulatory Context

_Full regulatory detail is in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md). Volatile figures are in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md)._


Australian organisations operate under a layered set of cyber risk management obligations. These are not separate compliance silos — they all impose the same underlying requirement: identify risks, implement proportionate controls, and demonstrate that you are managing rather than ignoring them.

### PSPF — Protective Security Policy Framework (Release 2025)

**Who it applies to:** Non-corporate Commonwealth entities (most departments and agencies). Mandatory requirements extend in practice to contractors and suppliers handling Commonwealth data or systems.

**What it requires for risk management:**

- Entities must establish and maintain a formal security risk management process (PSPF Risk domain).
- Risks arising from procurement and outsourcing must be actively managed, including FOCI (Foreign Ownership, Control or Influence) risks. Since PSPF Direction 001-2024, procurement of technology assets must include FOCI risk identification and management; identified risks must be reported to the Department of Home Affairs.
- PSPF Direction 002-2024 requires entities to identify and actively manage risks from vulnerable technologies they manage, including on behalf of other entities. By June 2025, a technology asset stocktake of all internet-facing systems was mandatory.
- PSPF Direction 003-2024 requires entities to share cyber threat information with ASD via the Cyber Threat Intelligence Sharing (CTIS) platform, operationalising the threat intelligence input to risk management.
- PSPF Requirement 0098 (introduced PSPF 2025, released July 2025) mandates development, implementation, and maintenance of a cybersecurity strategy and uplift plan to manage cybersecurity risks in accordance with the ISM and Zero Trust Guiding Principles.
- CISOs have increased reporting obligations under PSPF 2025, including more frequent reporting on cybersecurity matters to the audit, risk, and compliance committee.
- Annual PSPF maturity reporting is mandatory — entities must assess and report the effectiveness of their PSPF implementation, not merely assert compliance.

**Key interface with risk management:** The PSPF risk domain maps directly to enterprise risk management practice. Risk must be managed across six security domains: governance, risk, information, technology, personnel, and physical. Cyber risk does not sit in isolation.

### SOCI Act — Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 (as amended)

**Who it applies to:** Responsible entities for critical infrastructure assets across 11 sectors: energy, communications, data storage or processing, financial services and markets, water and sewerage, health care and medical, higher education and research, food and grocery, transport, space technology, and the defence industry. Amendments effective December 2024 and April 2025 expanded scope to include data storage systems holding business-critical data and brought telecommunications security into the SOCI Act framework.

**Critical Infrastructure Risk Management Program (CIRMP):**

The CIRMP is the centrepiece of the SOCI Act's positive security obligations. It is a written, "all-hazards" risk management programme that responsible entities must have, comply with, and report against annually. There is no prescribed format, but the CIRMP Rules (LIN 23/006) 2023 mandate that it must address all material risks across four hazard categories:

```
┌─────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ HAZARD CATEGORY         │ CIRMP REQUIREMENT                          │
├─────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Cyber and Information   │ Must establish and maintain a process to   │
│ Security                │ minimise or eliminate material cyber risk  │
│                         │ and mitigate relevant impacts. From August │
│                         │ 2024, entities must comply with one of     │
│                         │ five designated cybersecurity frameworks   │
│                         │ (Essential Eight, NIST CSF, ISO 27001,     │
│                         │ AESCSF, C2M2), or an equivalent framework. │
├─────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Supply Chain            │ Identify and manage supply chain risks to  │
│                         │ the critical infrastructure asset.         │
├─────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Personnel               │ Manage risks from personnel with access to │
│                         │ the asset.                                 │
├─────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Physical Security and   │ Manage risks to physical access and        │
│ Natural Hazards         │ integrity of the asset, and risks from     │
│ (combined category)     │ natural events that could affect it.       │
└─────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Annual reporting:** The CIRMP annual report must be board-approved and submitted to the Cyber and Infrastructure Security Centre (CISC) — or the Reserve Bank of Australia for payment system assets — within 90 days of the end of each financial year (by 28 September).

**Systems of National Significance (SoNS):** Approximately 220 assets had SoNS declarations as at March 2025. SoNS responsible entities face enhanced obligations including mandatory incident response planning, cyber security exercises, vulnerability assessments, and periodic system information reporting to ASD.

**Incident notification:** Mandatory cyber incident reporting under Part 2B. Significant cyber security incidents affecting a critical infrastructure asset must be reported to ASD within 12 hours; other reportable incidents within 72 hours.

**Government powers:** The SOCI Act (as amended December 2024) expanded the government's powers to issue directions to responsible entities and intervene in "serious incidents" — not just "serious cyber security incidents" — where the availability, integrity, or reliability of a critical infrastructure asset is affected. The Optus and Medibank incidents, where non-operational data storage systems fell outside the original SOCI scope, drove the 2024 expansion to include data storage systems holding business-critical data.

### ASD Information Security Manual (ISM)

The ISM is the ASD's technical cybersecurity framework. It is mandatory for non-corporate Commonwealth entities (via PSPF and PSPF Requirement 0098) and is one of the five acceptable cybersecurity frameworks for CIRMP compliance. It is recommended for any organisation seeking to demonstrate cybersecurity capability in the Australian government context.

The ISM is applied through the organisation's risk management framework — it does not replace it. The ISM's six-step security authorisation process (Define, Assess, Implement, Evaluate, Authorise, Monitor) aligns with the NIST RMF. Authorising officers must accept residual risk for each system before it is authorised to operate. The December 2024 ISM update:

- Explicitly requires CISOs to extend cybersecurity leadership to OT environments alongside IT systems.
- Mandates reporting on both IT and OT cybersecurity to the audit, risk, and compliance committee.
- Introduces controls for AI application development, specifically referencing OWASP Top 10 for LLM risks.
- Requires OT supply chain security assessments.

### ASD Essential Eight Maturity Model

The Essential Eight is ASD's prioritised set of eight baseline mitigation strategies, assessed across four maturity levels (0–3). It is:

- Mandatory for non-corporate Commonwealth entities under the PSPF.
- One of the five acceptable cybersecurity frameworks for SOCI CIRMP cyber and information security hazard compliance.
- A risk-based control baseline, not a compliance checklist — target maturity level should be determined by the threat environment the organisation faces, not by a single prescribed level.

The eight strategies are: application control; patch applications; configure Microsoft Office macro settings; user application hardening; restrict administrative privileges; patch operating systems; multi-factor authentication; regular backups.

### Cyber Security Act 2024

Passed November 2024, effective December 2024. Creates new obligations for businesses operating in Australia:

- **Ransomware payment reporting:** organisations with annual turnover above $3 million that make a ransomware payment must report to the Australian Signals Directorate within 72 hours of the payment being made. Reports must include: ransomware variant used, vulnerabilities exploited, ransom demanded and amount paid.
- **Minimum cybersecurity standards for smart devices** (IoT security).
- **ASD-led incident response powers** expanded.

The ransomware reporting obligation is directly relevant to risk management: an organisation that has accepted (or ignored) a risk, experienced a ransomware event, and paid a ransom must now report the vulnerability that was exploited — creating a closed-loop accountability mechanism.

### Privacy Act 1988 (and 2024 Amendments)

The Privacy Legislation Amendment (Enforcement and Other Measures) Act 2022 strengthened penalties for serious or repeated privacy breaches to the greater of $50 million, three times the value of any benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover. Under the Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme, entities must assess a suspected eligible breach within 30 days, and notify the OAIC and affected individuals as soon as practicable once an eligible breach is identified.

Risk management is the mechanism that operationalises Privacy Act compliance: identifying risks to personal information, implementing proportionate controls, and documenting the basis for decisions.

## How It Works in Practice

### Three lines of defence

```

│ here.            │ leadership.          │ committee.              │
└──────────────────┴──────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┘
```

**Key roles:**

- **Risk owner:** has authority and budget to decide the response and bears accountability for the outcome. Must be someone who can act. Under the PSPF and CIRMP, risk ownership must be formally assigned.
- **Control owner:** accountable for ensuring a specific control is designed, implemented, and operating — including budgeting, staffing, and monitoring.
- **Control stewards:** responsible for routine maintenance of controls on behalf of the control owner.
- **CISO:** Must directly report cybersecurity matters to the audit, risk, and compliance committee rather than via other executives, an ISM requirement introduced in the December 2024 update. Must coordinate between cyber and business risk teams. Under PSPF 2025, CISO accountability is explicitly strengthened.
- **Board / senior management:** sets risk appetite, receives posture reporting, approves the programme mandate. Under SOCI, the board must approve the CIRMP annual report before submission.

### Business risk management tooling

The tooling landscape spans four overlapping categories:

**GRC platforms** manage the enterprise risk register, treatment plans, compliance mappings, and reporting workflows. Examples: ServiceNow GRC, RSA Archer, MetricStream, LogicGate Risk Cloud, Centraleyes, 6clicks (commonly used in Australian government for PSPF and ISM compliance automation). Most now incorporate FAIR-based quantification modules and compliance framework mapping.

**Cyber Risk Quantification (CRQ) tools** convert cyber risk into financial exposure estimates using FAIR or Monte Carlo simulation. They produce loss exceedance curves and "what-if" scenario analyses. Examples: RiskLens (FAIR-native), Axio, Safe Security, Kovrr. CRQ outputs are increasingly expected in board and executive reporting, particularly for SOCI entities demonstrating proportionate risk management.

**Vulnerability and exposure management platforms** provide continuous asset discovery, vulnerability scanning, and RBVM-based prioritisation incorporating CVSS, EPSS, KEV, and asset criticality context. Examples: Tenable One, Qualys TruRisk, Rapid7 InsightVM, SAFE One (formerly Balbix), Wiz (cloud-native). These feed prioritised vulnerability risk data into the GRC register.

**Security ratings and TPRM platforms** provide continuous outside-in security posture measurement for the organisation itself and its vendor ecosystem. Examples: BitSight, SecurityScorecard, UpGuard. Relevant to CIRMP supply chain hazard obligations and PSPF procurement risk management.

**CTEM / exposure management platforms** provide continuous visibility across the full attack surface — internal, external, cloud, identity, OT — combining asset discovery, vulnerability data, threat intelligence, and attack path analysis. Examples: XM Cyber, Cymulate, Picus, Pentera.

The maturity trajectory: organisations typically progress from spreadsheet-based risk registers → GRC platforms → integrated CRQ and RBVM → full CTEM programme with quantified risk output feeding board-level reporting and CIRMP/PSPF compliance evidence.

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Treating compliance as a risk management programme.** PSPF and CIRMP annual reports demonstrate that a risk programme exists and was applied — they do not demonstrate that risk is actually managed. Organisations that conflate the two are repeatedly surprised by incidents that were "compliant."

**Patching by CVSS score only.** CVSS measures severity, not risk. This is the most common operational failure in vulnerability management. A programme that does not incorporate EPSS, KEV status, asset criticality, and exploitability context is spending most of its effort on vulnerabilities attackers are not using. Under PSPF Direction 002-2024, vulnerable technology risk must be actively managed — not just scored.

**Ignoring FOCI risk in procurement.** Since PSPF Direction 001-2024, procurement of technology assets without FOCI risk assessment and reporting to the Department of Home Affairs is non-compliant. The 2023 discovery of over 900 Chinese Government-linked surveillance devices across Commonwealth buildings is the operational example that drove this requirement.

**Risk registers never updated.** Define a review cadence and triggers for ad hoc updates: after incidents, major changes, new threat intelligence, and business strategy shifts. Under CIRMP, the risk management programme must be kept current — not filed and forgotten.

**Accepting risk without recording it.** Acceptance must be documented, deliberate, and made by someone with authority. Under the ISM, the authorising officer explicitly accepts residual risk as part of the authorisation to operate process. This is a formal act, not a tacit one.

**Inconsistent qualitative ratings.** "High" means different things to different assessors without an anchored scale. Define and publish the scale; train assessors against it.

**No link between risk and security investment.** Every major investment should trace back to a risk in the register. This is the basis on which Essential Eight target maturity levels are supposed to be set — by threat environment and risk context, not by regulatory prescription alone.

**Third-party risk assumed away.** Under SOCI, responsible entities remain accountable for supply chain hazards. Under PSPF, procurement risk — including FOCI — is a mandatory management obligation. Contractual transfer of liability is not transfer of risk.

**Misunderstanding residual risk.** Risk owners who approve a control without understanding what residual risk remains — and whether it is within appetite — are making uninformed decisions. Under the ISM, this residual risk must be explicitly accepted by the authorising officer.

**Vulnerability management disconnected from the risk register.** Scanner outputs and risk registers are often maintained in separate teams and systems, with no formal process to convert findings into risk register entries. PSPF Direction 002-2024 (technology vulnerability risk management) requires the connection.

**CIRMP treated as a documentation exercise.** The CIRMP is not a document — it is a programme. Many entities produce a written CIRMP for annual reporting without operating a functioning risk management programme throughout the year. CISC can direct entities to vary their CIRMP if it identifies a "serious deficiency."

**PSPF annual reporting as a compliance point, not a programme driver.** The shift from maturity self-reporting to compliance-based reporting (commenced 2025) means entities can no longer simply describe their aspirational maturity level. They must demonstrate implementation effectiveness.

## Connections to Other Domains

- **[Cyber Governance and Management (1)](01.%20Cyber%20Governance%20and%20Management.md):** Risk appetite, tolerance, and capacity are set at governance level. Under PSPF, governance and risk domains are explicitly linked. Under SOCI, the board must approve the CIRMP annual report — this is a governance requirement, not an administrative one. The three lines of defence is a governance construct applied directly to risk management.
- **[Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance (3)](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md):** PSPF, SOCI/CIRMP, Cyber Security Act 2024, and the Privacy Act all create legally enforceable cyber risk management obligations in Australia. Regulatory non-compliance is itself a risk category requiring assessment alongside technical risk. Fines, government-directed remediation, and licence risk belong in the register.
- **[Privacy and Data Protection (4)](04.%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Protection.md):** PIAs and DPIAs are risk assessments applied to personal data processing. The NDB scheme creates a mandatory incident reporting obligation — the assessment window (within 30 days of becoming aware) and the notify-as-soon-as-practicable requirement must be factored into incident response risk planning. The 2024 amendments to the Privacy Act significantly increased penalty exposure.
- **[Vendor Management (5)](05.%20Vendor%20Management.md):** Under SOCI CIRMP, supply chain hazard risk is a mandatory management category. Under PSPF, procurement risk — including FOCI — is mandatory. Third-party risk requires tiered assessment by supplier criticality, contractual security requirements, and ongoing monitoring via security ratings platforms.
- **[Asset and Change Management (6)](06.%20Asset%20and%20Change%20Management.md):** PSPF Direction 002-2024 required a technology asset stocktake of all internet-facing systems by June 2025. This is risk management, not just inventory — asset criticality scores are direct inputs to RBVM prioritisation. Change management must include a risk assessment gate before changes are approved.
- **[Cyber Testing (10)](10.%20Cyber%20Testing.md):** SoNS entities under SOCI are required to conduct vulnerability assessments and cyber security exercises. Penetration testing findings feed the risk register and RBVM queues. CTEM incorporates breach and attack simulation as a continuous validation capability.
- **[Threat Intelligence (11)](11.%20Threat%20Intelligence.md):** PSPF Direction 003-2024 requires Commonwealth entities to share cyber threat information with ASD and connect to the CTIS platform. ASD's Annual Cyber Threat Report and ACSC advisories are authoritative Australian threat intelligence sources. Without current threat intelligence, likelihood ratings are historical guesswork.
- **[Security Operations and Monitoring (12)](12.%20Security%20Operations%20and%20Monitoring.md):** SOC outputs are real-time risk signals. A mature programme connects SOC telemetry to KRIs. ASD's CTIS platform and threat-hunting capabilities (PSPF Direction 003-2024) create a government-to-entity feedback loop for emerging threats.
- **[Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning (13)](13.%20Incident%20Management%20and%20Business%20Continuity%20Planning.md):** SOCI Act mandatory cyber incident notification (12 hours for significant incidents, 72 hours for other reportable incidents) must be factored into incident response planning. Post-incident reviews update the risk register. BCP and DR planning is itself a risk response to availability and continuity risks — CIRMP explicitly requires contingency planning.
- **[Cloud Management (14)](14.%20Cloud%20Management.md):** Cloud adoption must be managed through IRAP-assessed cloud providers for Commonwealth entities (ISM and PSPF requirement). Cloud risk must be assessed per service model. The Hosting Certification Framework (currently under consultation) will further regulate cloud hosting for government data.
- **[Software Development Security (15)](15.%20Software%20Development%20Security.md):** Threat modelling (STRIDE, PASTA) at design is a risk identification activity. SBOMs and software composition analysis feed supply chain vulnerability risk — relevant to both CIRMP supply chain hazard obligations and ASD supply chain risk guidance.
- **[Cyber Architecture (17)](17.%20Cyber%20Architecture.md):** PSPF Requirement 0098 explicitly references Zero Trust as the architectural approach to cybersecurity risk management. Architecture decisions are risk decisions; the Zero Trust Guiding Principles operationalise risk-based access control at the architectural level.
- **[Cyber Culture and Education (18)](18.%20Cyber%20Culture%20and%20Education.md):** Human behaviour is one of the most significant risk factors. PSPF personnel security domain and CIRMP personnel hazard category both require active management of human risk. Awareness and training programmes are risk mitigation controls; their effectiveness should be tracked as a KCI.
- **[LLM Security (19)](19.%20LLM%20Security.md):** The December 2024 ISM update introduced AI application controls referencing OWASP Top 10 for LLMs. PSPF 2025 includes new material on AI. AI and LLM adoption introduces risk categories — model drift, data poisoning, prompt injection, training data exposure — that must be integrated into the enterprise risk management programme with their own threat scenarios, KRIs, and controls.
- **[Physical Security (20)](20.%20Physical%20Security.md):** CIRMP physical security hazard category and PSPF physical security domain both impose risk management obligations for physical access to assets. For OT and critical infrastructure environments, physical and cyber risk are deeply intertwined — a physical breach can be the precursor to a cyber event, and vice versa.
