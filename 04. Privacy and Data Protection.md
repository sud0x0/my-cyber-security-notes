# Privacy and Data Protection

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Privacy and data protection is the discipline of ensuring that personal information is collected, handled, stored, shared, and destroyed in ways that respect individuals' rights and meet legal obligations. It sits at the intersection of law, governance, risk management, and technical security — and touches every system that processes information about people.

The distinction between privacy and data protection matters in practice. Privacy is a human right and a social expectation — the individual's reasonable expectation of control over information about themselves. Data protection is the legal and operational framework that gives that expectation practical effect. You can have technically strong data protection controls (encryption, access controls, breach notification) without actually respecting privacy (collecting far more data than needed, using it in ways people would not expect). Genuinely good practice requires both.

Privacy sits inside the broader security landscape as a constraint on what you can do with data, not just how safely you store it. It affects system design, procurement, third-party contracts, incident response, architecture decisions, and cloud deployments. It is not a compliance checkbox owned by legal — it is an operational discipline that runs through the entire organisation.

For Australian organisations, the compliance landscape is layered: the Privacy Act applies broadly to personal information; the PSPF governs how Commonwealth entities classify and protect government information; the SOCI Act imposes risk management and incident reporting obligations on critical infrastructure; and the Cyber Security Act 2024 adds ransomware payment reporting and IoT device security. These frameworks overlap at the point where personal data, government data, and critical systems intersect — and they must be read together, not in isolation.

---

## Why It Matters

When privacy and data protection are done poorly, consequences fall into three categories.

**Regulatory and legal exposure.** GDPR fines can reach €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. As of early 2025, total cumulative GDPR fines exceeded €5.65 billion across 2,245+ recorded cases since 2018. The largest single fine was €1.2 billion against Meta Platforms Ireland (2023) for unlawful EU-US data transfers. In 2024 alone: LinkedIn was fined €310 million for unlawful behavioural analysis and targeted advertising, Uber €290 million for transferring driver data to the US without adequate safeguards, and Meta €251 million for a 2018 breach affecting 29 million users. In Australia, the Privacy Legislation Amendment (Enforcement and Other Measures) Act 2022 introduced civil penalties up to the greater of AU$50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover for serious interferences with privacy. Australia also introduced a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy (commenced 10 June 2025), allowing individuals to sue directly for up to AU$500,000 without needing to prove financial harm.

**Operational and reputational damage.** The Medibank breach (2022) exposed health claims data for 9.7 million Australians. Optus (2022) exposed personal data for up to 9.8 million customers. Both triggered regulatory investigations, class actions, and lasting reputational harm. In 2024, Australian organisations reported 1,113 notifiable data breaches to the OAIC — the highest annual total since the Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme began in 2018, a 25% increase over 2023. Malicious attacks accounted for the majority. The Australian Signals Directorate's Annual Cyber Threat Report 2023–24 found that around 71% of extortion-related incidents it responded to involved ransomware.

**Harm to individuals.** Identity fraud, financial loss, discrimination, and psychological harm are the downstream consequences of privacy failures. Health data exposure can affect insurance, employment, and personal safety. These are the actual stakes behind the legal obligations.

## Core Concepts

### Personal Information and Special Categories

Different frameworks define personal information differently, but the practical test is consistent: can this data, alone or in combination with other data, identify a living individual? GDPR's definition is deliberately broad — names, identifiers, location data, online identifiers, and any factors relating to physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity all qualify. Australia's Privacy Act 1988 defines personal information as information or an opinion about an identified individual, or an individual who is reasonably identifiable.

Special categories (GDPR Article 9) attract additional obligations and require explicit consent or another specific enumerated legal basis: racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data used for identification, health data, sex life and sexual orientation. The Australian Privacy Act refers to equivalent categories as "sensitive information." Children's data is treated as particularly sensitive under most frameworks — the OAIC is developing a Children's Online Privacy Code (due for registration by 10 December 2026), and the GDPR requires parental authorisation for children's consent below 16 (member states may lower this to a minimum of 13).

### The GDPR Article 5 Data Protection Principles

These seven principles sit at the core of GDPR and underpin most modern privacy law globally. They function as design requirements for any system handling personal data:

```
  1. Lawfulness, fairness and transparency
     Process data lawfully, fairly, and in a transparent manner to data subjects

  2. Purpose limitation
     Collect for specified, explicit, legitimate purposes; do not process
     further in a manner incompatible with those purposes

  3. Data minimisation
     Adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the purpose

  4. Accuracy
     Keep data accurate and up to date; erase or rectify inaccuracies promptly

  5. Storage limitation
     Keep in identifiable form no longer than necessary for the stated purpose

  6. Integrity and confidentiality
     Appropriate security against unauthorised access, loss, destruction, or damage

  7. Accountability
     The controller is responsible for, and must be able to demonstrate,
     compliance with all of the above
```

### Lawful Basis for Processing

Under GDPR Article 6, personal data can only be processed where at least one of six lawful bases applies. Controllers must identify the basis before processing begins and must inform data subjects of it at the point of collection:

```
  Consent           Freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous; withdrawable
                    at any time
  Contract          Necessary for performance of a contract with the data subject
  Legal obligation  Necessary for compliance with a statutory or common law
                    obligation (not contractual)
  Vital interests   Necessary to protect someone's life; narrow application
  Public task       Necessary for a task in the public interest or exercise of
                    official authority; mostly public bodies
  Legitimate        Necessary for the controller's or a third party's legitimate
  interests         interests, balanced against the data subject's rights;
                    requires a documented balancing test
```

Consent is not always the most appropriate basis. It can be withdrawn at any time, creating operational fragility for processing that is genuinely necessary. Where another basis applies, it is usually more appropriate to rely on that.

### Data Controllers and Processors

The controller determines the purpose and means of processing and bears primary accountability. The processor handles data on the controller's behalf under documented instructions. A single organisation can be both — a payroll provider is a controller for its own employee data, and a processor for its customers' employee data. In cloud environments, the cloud provider is typically the processor. This distinction determines who holds which legal obligation and must be reflected in Data Processing Agreements (DPAs).

### Data Minimisation and Purpose Limitation

Collect only what you need for a stated, legitimate purpose. Do not use data for purposes beyond what was specified at the time of collection. These principles drive most of the architectural and system design decisions that privacy requires — they are engineering constraints, not policy statements.

### Data Subject Rights

Individuals have rights that must be operationally supported, not just acknowledged in a privacy notice:

```
  ACCESS          Obtain a copy of data held and how it is used (30-day window)
  RECTIFICATION   Correct inaccurate or incomplete data
  ERASURE         Request deletion; subject to legal exceptions (obligations,
                  public interest, archiving, legal claims)
  RESTRICTION     Limit processing in specified circumstances
  PORTABILITY     Receive data in machine-readable format for transfer to
                  another controller (applies to consent or contract basis)
  OBJECTION       Object to processing based on legitimate interests; absolute
                  right for direct marketing; balancing test for other purposes
  AUTOMATED       Not be subject to solely automated decisions with legal or
  DECISIONS       similarly significant effects without human review
```

Supporting these rights requires knowing where data lives, who has it, and being able to act within regulatory timeframes. A 30-day response deadline is unachievable if data mapping is inadequate.

### Privacy by Design

Privacy controls are built into systems from inception. Ann Cavoukian's seven foundational principles (developed in the 1990s, formally published 2009) are referenced in GDPR Article 25 ("Data Protection by Design and by Default"):

1. Proactive not reactive — prevent privacy harms before they materialise
2. Privacy as the default — no action required by the individual to protect their privacy
3. Privacy embedded into design — not bolted on as an add-on
4. Full functionality — privacy and functionality are not zero-sum
5. End-to-end security — protection across the full data lifecycle
6. Visibility and transparency — open to scrutiny and verification
7. Respect for user privacy — keep it user-centric

GDPR Article 25 makes this legally binding for EU-regulated processing: data protection by design and by default must be implemented at the point of system design, not retrofitted post-deployment.

### The OECD Privacy Principles

The OECD's eight privacy principles (1980, updated 2013) underpin most privacy laws globally, including Australia's APPs: Collection limitation, Data quality, Purpose specification, Use limitation, Security safeguards, Openness, Individual participation, and Accountability. They provide a useful baseline for evaluating any processing activity regardless of which specific law applies.

### Data Lifecycle

```
  ACQUISITION           CLASSIFICATION         USE & ARCHIVAL          DESTRUCTION
  ─────────────►        ─────────────►         ─────────────►          ─────────────►
  Created, collected    Classified and         Read, modified,         Securely destroyed
  or received from      protected at rest,     shared externally,      at end of retention
  external source       in transit,            archived per            period; destruction
                        and in use             policy or law           verified and recorded
```

Retention and destruction are frequently neglected. Data held longer than necessary creates liability with no benefit. Destruction must be verified and documented, and applied to backups and archives — not just primary systems.

### Pseudonymisation vs Anonymisation

Pseudonymous data — where direct identifiers are replaced with tokens but a re-identification mapping table exists — is still personal data under GDPR and most frameworks. All obligations apply. Truly anonymous data, where re-identification is not reasonably possible by any available means, falls outside GDPR's scope. Most "anonymised" datasets in practice are pseudonymised. The ICO and EDPB have both emphasised how demanding the bar for true anonymisation is — it is not met simply by removing a name field.

### Privacy-Enhancing Technologies (PETs)

PETs minimise personal data exposure while preserving analytical or operational utility:

- **Differential privacy** — adds controlled statistical noise to data so individual records cannot be inferred from aggregate outputs; used by Apple and Google for analytics
- **Homomorphic encryption** — allows computation on encrypted data without decrypting; computationally expensive, emerging in practice
- **Federated learning** — trains machine learning models across distributed nodes without centralising raw personal data
- **Synthetic data generation** — produces artificial datasets that mirror statistical properties without containing actual personal records
- **Secure multi-party computation (SMPC)** — enables joint computation over inputs from multiple parties without revealing those inputs
- **Tokenisation** — replaces sensitive values (e.g. payment card numbers) with non-sensitive tokens; common in financial services
- **Pseudonymisation** — replaces identifiers with tokens; reduces direct identifiability while allowing re-linkage under controlled conditions

PETs support Privacy by Design and can help demonstrate "data protection by design and default" to regulators. They do not replace governance or legal obligations.

## Australian Government Cyber Security Compliance — PSPF and SOCI in Practice

### PSPF: What It Requires for Information and Privacy

The PSPF (Release 2025, published 24 July 2025) is mandatory for non-corporate Commonwealth entities and extends in practice to contractors and suppliers handling Commonwealth data. Its six security domains — governance, risk, information, technology, personnel, and physical — all carry privacy implications.

The information security domain is the most directly relevant. The PSPF requires entities to:

- Classify all information according to its sensitivity using the Australian Government's classification scheme and apply protective markings. The classification levels are OFFICIAL, OFFICIAL: Sensitive, PROTECTED, SECRET, and TOP SECRET. Personal information about individuals typically attracts the marking OFFICIAL: Sensitive with the Information Management Marker (IMM) "Personal Privacy." Health records, tax information, and legally privileged material have their own specific IMMs.
- Implement operational controls proportional to the value, importance, and sensitivity of information holdings. For OFFICIAL: Sensitive and above, need-to-know must be enforced and access restricted to those with appropriate clearances.
- Maintain a register of security-classified information holdings (an information asset register).
- Apply the Australian Government Email Protective Marking Standard to email — all emails must carry a protective marking in the subject line in the format `[SEC=OFFICIAL:Sensitive, ACCESS=Personal-Privacy]`.
- Ensure that ICT systems holding PROTECTED or above are IRAP-assessed (assessed by an ASD-accredited IRAP assessor) and hosted in environments with appropriate Hosting Certification Framework certification.
- Report security incidents to the Australian Signals Directorate and the Department of Home Affairs. PSPF 2025 increased accountability for CISOs with more frequent reporting requirements.
- Apply Zero Trust principles (embedded in PSPF 2025) to technology architecture — this directly affects how personal data in government systems is accessed and protected.

The Privacy Act and PSPF overlap significantly for Commonwealth entities. An entity subject to both must satisfy both frameworks simultaneously. A data breach involving OFFICIAL: Sensitive personal information triggers PSPF incident reporting obligations to the ASD and DoHA, as well as NDB scheme obligations to the OAIC and the affected individuals. These clocks run in parallel, not sequentially.

The PSPF's approach to classification is not a substitute for Privacy Act compliance — it is an additional layer. An entity can be PSPF-compliant (correct classification, correct markings, correct storage) while simultaneously breaching APP 11 (failure to take reasonable steps to protect personal information) if its technical controls are inadequate. Both regimes must be satisfied.

**PSPF and supply chain.** Contractors and suppliers handling Commonwealth data must comply with PSPF information security requirements as a contractual obligation. Commonwealth contracts typically require PSPF alignment, security vetting of personnel, and IRAP assessment of relevant systems. Non-government organisations supplying to government must understand where their data handling obligations under the Privacy Act end and their PSPF contractual obligations begin — these are different legal instruments with different enforcement mechanisms.

### SOCI: What It Requires and Where Privacy Intersects

The SOCI Act 2018, as substantially amended by the Security Legislation Amendment (Critical Infrastructure) Act 2021, the Security Legislation Amendment (Critical Infrastructure Protection) Act 2022, and the Security of Critical Infrastructure and Other Legislation Amendment (Enhanced Response and Prevention) Act 2024 (ERP Act, commenced December 2024), covers 11 critical infrastructure sectors and 22 asset classes:

```
  Communications       Energy                 Financial services
  Defence industry     Transport              Data processing and storage
  Water and sewerage   Health and medical     Food and grocery
  Higher education     Space technology
```

For responsible entities — those with operational responsibility for critical infrastructure assets — the three core Positive Security Obligations (PSOs) are:

1. **Asset registration:** Provide ownership and operational information to the Register of Critical Infrastructure Assets (maintained by the Cyber and Infrastructure Security Centre, CISC).

2. **Mandatory cyber incident reporting:** Report to the Australian Cyber Security Centre (ACSC) via ReportCyber:
   - Within 12 hours of becoming aware of a significant impact (serious disruption to essential services)
   - Within 72 hours of becoming aware of a relevant impact (other qualifying incidents)

3. **Critical Infrastructure Risk Management Program (CIRMP):** A written, board-approved program taking an all-hazards approach across four vectors: cyber and information security, personnel, supply chain, and physical/natural hazards. Annual reports due to the Department of Home Affairs within 90 days of financial year end (28 September each year). From August 2024, CIRMPs must reference and comply with one of the five designated cybersecurity frameworks (Essential Eight, NIST CSF, ISO 27001, AESCSF, C2M2) or an equivalent framework.

The ERP Act (December 2024) clarified that data storage and processing systems holding business-critical data that are used in connection with a primary critical infrastructure asset are themselves in scope — effective April 2025. This directly affects cloud providers, managed service providers, and data centres serving critical infrastructure clients.

**Systems of National Significance (SoNS)** carry four additional Enhanced Cyber Security Obligations (ECSOs): develop a cyber incident response plan, conduct cyber exercises, undertake vulnerability assessments, and provide system information to the ASD for near-real-time threat intelligence.

**Where SOCI and privacy intersect.** SOCI's CIRMP cyber and information security hazard requirements directly encompass the protection of personal data held within critical infrastructure systems. The confidentiality, integrity, and availability of those systems — and of the personal data they process — are both SOCI obligations and Privacy Act obligations simultaneously. A ransomware attack on a healthcare critical infrastructure asset is, in the same event: a SOCI-reportable cyber incident (12 or 72 hours to ACSC), an NDB scheme notification event if personal health data is exposed (to OAIC and affected individuals), a Cyber Security Act ransomware reporting event if a ransom is paid (72 hours to ASD), and potentially a APRA CPS 234 incident notification if the entity is also APRA-regulated (72 hours to APRA).

**Managing overlapping reporting timelines.** The practical consequence of this overlap is that critical infrastructure operators and financial services entities must build consolidated incident response processes with a unified reporting matrix. A single cyber event involving personal data can trigger four or more parallel notification obligations to different regulators, each with slightly different information requirements and timeframes:

```
  EVENT: Ransomware attack + personal data breach + ransom paid

  Obligation                      Regulator   Timeframe    Trigger
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  SOCI significant impact         ACSC        12 hours     Serious service disruption
  SOCI relevant impact            ACSC        72 hours     Other qualifying CI impact
  Privacy Act NDB scheme          OAIC +      ASAP         Likely serious harm to individuals
                                  individuals
  Cyber Security Act ransomware   ASD         72 hours     Payment made/authorised
  reporting
  APRA CPS 234 (if applicable)    APRA        72 hours     Material information security
                                                           incident
  ASX continuous disclosure       ASX         ASAP         Material information for listed
  (if listed)                                              entities
```

Incident response plans must be designed to handle all applicable obligations simultaneously. The OAIC, ACSC, ASD, and APRA do not co-ordinate notifications on your behalf.

### Cyber Security Act 2024: Key Privacy-Adjacent Obligations

The Cyber Security Act 2024 (Royal Assent 29 November 2024) is Australia's first standalone cybersecurity law. Its directly privacy-relevant provisions are:

- **Ransomware payment reporting (from 30 May 2025):** Businesses with annual turnover exceeding AU$3 million, and all critical infrastructure responsible entities, must report ransomware payments to the ASD within 72 hours. The report must include incident details, impact, extortion demand, and communications with the attacker. This reporting obligation is separate from and in addition to the NDB scheme and SOCI incident reporting. The limited-use obligation means ASD cannot use this report for civil or regulatory enforcement against the reporting entity — this was designed to encourage reporting without fear of self-incrimination.
- **Smart device security standards:** Mandatory minimum security standards for connectable devices (IoT) sold in Australia. Relevant to organisations deploying IoT in environments that process personal data — smart devices collecting personal data (health monitors, building management systems, CCTV) must meet baseline security standards.
- **Cyber Incident Review Board (CIRB):** Conducts no-fault, no-liability reviews of significant cyber incidents. Reports are published but cannot apportion blame or identify individuals. Designed to produce sector-wide lessons learned — similar to aviation accident investigation.
- **Limited-use obligation:** Information voluntarily shared with the National Cyber Security Coordinator cannot be used for civil or regulatory enforcement against the sharing entity. This encourages organisations to engage with government during incidents without fear that disclosed information will be used against them in Privacy Act enforcement proceedings.

## How It Works in Practice

### Roles

Privacy programmes require defined ownership across the organisation:

- **Data Protection Officer (DPO):** Mandatory under GDPR for public authorities, organisations whose core activities require large-scale systematic monitoring, or large-scale processing of special categories. Must be operationally independent; cannot be directed by management on how to perform DPO functions. Formal contact point for the supervisory authority.
- **Chief Security Officer / Security Executive (Government):** Under PSPF, non-corporate Commonwealth entities must designate a Chief Security Officer (CSO) accountable for the entity's protective security posture. The CSO and DPO/Privacy Officer functions may be separate roles or combined, depending on entity size.
- **Data Owner:** Typically a business unit head. Accountable for classifying data and authorising access changes; determines required controls.
- **Data Custodian:** Operationally manages data under the owner's direction — implements access controls, backup, and handling procedures.
- **Privacy Officer / Chief Privacy Officer:** Owns the privacy programme, policy framework, and regulatory relationships.
- **Security team:** Implements and operates technical controls — encryption, DLP, access control, monitoring, incident response.

### Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)

Required under GDPR Article 35 before processing likely to result in high risk to individuals. Mandatory in at minimum these situations: systematic profiling with significant effects on individuals, large-scale processing of special categories, and systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas on a large scale. For Commonwealth entities subject to PSPF, privacy impact assessments (PIAs) are required under APP 1 for any new activity involving personal information. A DPIA and a PIA serve similar purposes — for organisations subject to both GDPR and the Privacy Act, a single well-constructed assessment can satisfy both if it addresses the required elements of each.

A DPIA must contain at minimum:

- A systematic description of the processing and its purposes
- An assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing
- An assessment of risks to data subjects' rights and freedoms
- The measures envisaged to address those risks (safeguards, security measures)

The DPO must be consulted. If residual risk cannot be reduced to acceptable levels, the supervisory authority must be consulted before processing begins. A DPIA is a living document.

### Records of Processing Activities (RoPA)

Required under GDPR Article 30 for most controllers. Documents what personal data is held, for what purpose, where it is processed, retention periods, recipients, and cross-border transfer mechanisms. For PSPF entities, the information asset register serves a parallel function for classified government information — both the RoPA and the information asset register must be maintained and kept current against actual live systems. Minimum fields per processing activity:

```
  Controller / DPO contact details
  Purposes of processing
  Categories of data subjects and personal data
  Categories of recipients (including in third countries)
  Cross-border transfer mechanism (if applicable)
  Retention periods
  Description of technical and organisational security measures
```

### Cross-Border Data Transfers

Transferring personal data outside the country or region of original collection requires a legal mechanism. Under GDPR: adequacy decision, Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs), Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs), or derogations under Article 49. Australia does not hold a formal EU adequacy decision — SCCs or another approved mechanism are required for EU personal data. Under the amended Privacy Act, the government can whitelist countries for APP 8 transfers — no countries had been whitelisted as at April 2026. Under PSPF, transfer of classified Commonwealth information offshore requires specific approvals and in some cases is prohibited without encryption to the required level. PSPF does not substitute for Privacy Act compliance on personal data — both apply simultaneously.

### Practical Tooling

- **Data Loss Prevention (DLP):** Enforces data handling policies at the system level; detects and blocks transmission of personal data to unapproved destinations. In PSPF environments, DLP policies must be aligned to the Australian Government Email Protective Marking Standard and PSPF classification markings.
- **Privacy management platforms** (OneTrust, Securiti, Osano, TrustArc): Support RoPA maintenance, DPIA/PIA workflows, consent management, and subject rights request tracking.
- **Sensitivity labelling** (e.g. Microsoft Purview in M365 Government): In PSPF-aligned environments, sensitivity labels map directly to PSPF classifications and drive DLP, access, and encryption policies.
- **SIEM / SOC:** Detection capability for breaches triggering mandatory notification timelines. For SOCI-covered entities, the SIEM must be calibrated to detect events that would trigger 12- or 72-hour reporting obligations.
- **Privacy-Enhancing Technologies:** See Core Concepts section.
- **IAM systems:** Enforce need-to-know (PSPF), least privilege (Privacy Act/IAM best practice), and provide the audit trail for access reviews and subject access requests.

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Privacy treated as solely a legal function.** When privacy sits entirely with legal, security and engineering teams receive no requirements until systems are already built. Privacy obligations affect system architecture, APIs, data flows, vendor selection, and incident response. Legal cannot implement technical controls.

**Treating PSPF classification as a substitute for Privacy Act compliance.** An entity can correctly classify information as OFFICIAL: Sensitive Personal Privacy, apply proper markings, and store it in a PSPF-compliant environment — and still breach APP 11 if the technical security controls are inadequate. The PSPF and Privacy Act are separate frameworks with different legal bases and different enforcement bodies. Government entities must satisfy both simultaneously.

**Failing to map overlapping incident reporting obligations.** Organisations subject to SOCI, CPS 234, the Privacy Act NDB scheme, and the Cyber Security Act ransomware reporting obligation can face four or more parallel notification requirements from a single incident. Organisations that discover this during an active incident — rather than having rehearsed it — will miss regulatory deadlines. The consolidated reporting matrix must be built, tested, and owned before an incident occurs.

**Over-collection as the default.** Systems collect everything because storage is cheap and future use is uncertain. This directly contradicts data minimisation and creates a larger attack surface and liability footprint. The Medibank breach is instructive — data retained well beyond operational need was exposed because retention policy had not been enforced technically, only documented.

**Inadequate data mapping.** Organisations frequently cannot answer "where is the personal data?" at the granularity needed for subject access requests or breach response. A data map that is not maintained against actual live systems is useless under regulatory scrutiny. For PSPF entities, the information asset register must be accurate and current — it is not a one-time exercise.

**Conflating pseudonymisation with anonymisation.** Pseudonymous data — identifiers replaced with tokens, re-identification mapping table retained — is still personal data. True anonymisation is extremely difficult to achieve in practice. Most "anonymised" datasets can be re-identified with supplementary data. This failure is common across both government and private sector.

**Consent implemented poorly.** Consent collected through pre-ticked boxes, buried opt-outs, or vague language does not satisfy GDPR or equivalent requirements. Cookie banners that make "accept all" a single click and "manage preferences" deliberately difficult are a consistent enforcement target — French CNIL imposed substantial fines against Google and Facebook for exactly this pattern. Consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous.

**Ignoring processors and third-party vendors.** Controllers are accountable for how their processors handle data. Data Processing Agreements must be in place before data is shared. Under SOCI, responsible entities must ensure that managed service providers and data storage providers handling their critical infrastructure data meet the same security standards. Under CPS 234, third parties handling APRA-regulated entity data must meet equivalent information security controls.

**Production data in non-production environments.** Using real personal data in development, test, or staging environments, or sharing it with third-party vendors for debugging, is a common and serious compliance failure. Pipelines and development processes must enforce this technically.

**Treating privacy compliance as a one-time project.** Privacy obligations apply continuously. Systems change, data flows evolve, laws update. The PSPF has annual releases (2025 release published July 2025 added Zero Trust, AI guidance, and post-quantum cryptography requirements). SOCI has been amended multiple times since 2018 with further changes anticipated. The Privacy Act is undergoing tranche 2 reform expected in 2025–2026. A programme that does a single assessment and does not maintain living documentation will drift out of compliance.

## Connections to Other Domains

**1. Cyber Governance and Management:** Privacy obligations must be embedded in governance structures. Board-level accountability for privacy is expected by regulators — it is explicit under APRA CPS 234, required for SOCI CIRMP approval, and expected under the Privacy Act. Privacy policy requires the same approval, review cycle, and executive ownership as information security policy. Under PSPF, the Security Committee at entity level has governance responsibility over information holdings.

**2. Cyber Risk Management:** Data protection risk is a category of cyber risk. DPIAs and PIAs are risk assessment tools. SOCI's CIRMP cyber and information security hazard requirements overlap directly with privacy risk. Privacy harms must be quantified and treated within the enterprise risk framework, not managed as a purely legal matter.

**3. Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance:** Privacy law is the primary driver of most data protection requirements. Australian organisations may simultaneously face obligations under: the Privacy Act (OAIC), PSPF (Department of Home Affairs / ASD), SOCI (CISC), CPS 234 (APRA), the Cyber Security Act (ASD / Home Affairs), and GDPR (if handling EU personal data). These frameworks have different legal bases, different enforcement bodies, and different penalties. Legal counsel familiar with all applicable frameworks is essential.

**5. Vendor Management:** Every third-party processor requires a Data Processing Agreement and a security assessment before data is shared. Under SOCI, supply chain security is a mandated CIRMP hazard category — contracts with suppliers must include rights enabling the responsible entity to meet its SOCI obligations. Under CPS 234, third parties handling APRA-regulated entity data must meet equivalent information security controls.

**6. Asset and Change Management:** Data classification (Privacy Act, PSPF) drives handling and protection requirements. Change management must include a privacy screening step. Under PSPF, changes to systems holding OFFICIAL: Sensitive or above must be assessed against the information security domain requirements. Under SOCI, changes affecting critical infrastructure assets may need to be reflected in the CIRMP.

**8. Identity and Access Management:** Least privilege is the technical implementation of data minimisation. Under PSPF, need-to-know must be enforced for all OFFICIAL: Sensitive and above information. Access to personal data must be controlled, logged, and regularly reviewed. Subject access requests frequently require aggregating data across multiple identity-linked systems.

**9. Cryptography:** Encryption is the primary technical control for data at rest and in transit. Under GDPR, a breach involving encrypted data (key not compromised) may not require individual notification. Under PSPF, specific cryptographic standards apply at each classification level — PROTECTED and above requires ASD-approved encryption. PSPF 2025 introduced post-quantum cryptography guidance as an emerging requirement.

**12. Security Operations and Monitoring:** Detection of breaches triggering notification obligations requires SIEM and SOC capability. For SOCI-covered entities, the SIEM must detect events at the threshold of 12-hour and 72-hour reporting obligations. DLP tools must be calibrated to PSPF classification markings in government environments.

**13. Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning:** Data breach response is a privacy-specific incident type with mandatory regulatory notification timelines. For organisations subject to multiple frameworks (SOCI + Privacy Act + CPS 234 + Cyber Security Act), a single ransomware incident can trigger four or more parallel notification obligations with different timeframes and different regulators. Incident response plans must be built and tested to handle all of these simultaneously, not sequentially.

**14. Cloud Management:** Cloud providers are typically processors. Shared responsibility models must account for which privacy obligations the customer retains. Under PSPF, cloud systems holding PROTECTED or above must be IRAP-assessed and hosted in appropriately certified environments. Under SOCI, data storage and processing systems connected to critical infrastructure assets are in scope from April 2025 — cloud providers may themselves be captured as part of a critical infrastructure asset.

**15. Software Development Security:** Privacy by Design requires privacy requirements in the SDLC. DPIAs and PIAs should be triggered at the design phase. Under PSPF, system design for government systems must incorporate classification requirements from the outset. IoT and connectable devices must meet the Cyber Security Act's mandatory security standards from November 2025 (or earlier if proclaimed).

**16. DevOps and DevSecOps:** Production personal data must not flow into non-production environments. Pipeline controls must enforce this. PSPF classification markings must be preserved in DevOps tooling — development environments accessing classified information must themselves meet appropriate PSPF controls.

**19. LLM Security:** Training LLMs on personal data without a lawful basis, using personal data in RAG contexts, and generating outputs that reveal personal information are all emerging privacy risk vectors. For government entities, using LLMs with OFFICIAL: Sensitive or above data requires assessment against PSPF information security requirements and IRAP assessment of the hosting environment. The EU AI Act (in force August 2024) intersects with GDPR for AI systems making decisions with legal or significant effects on individuals.

**20. Physical Security:** Physical access controls protect data at rest on physical media and systems. Under PSPF, physical security requirements are defined by classification level — PROTECTED and above material requires SCEC-approved security containers and rooms. Clean desk policies, secure disposal of printed classified documents, and visitor management are privacy controls as well as physical security controls.
