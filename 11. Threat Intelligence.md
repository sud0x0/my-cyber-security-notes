# Threat Intelligence

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Threat intelligence is the discipline of collecting, processing, analysing, and applying information about adversaries — their capabilities, infrastructure, intentions, and methods — to enable better security decisions. It sits at the intersection of security operations, risk management, and strategic planning. The key word is _intelligence_, not information. Raw data (an IP address, a file hash) is not intelligence until it has been contextualised, analysed, and assessed for relevance to a specific organisation. Most organisations drown in threat data while remaining blind to actionable intelligence.

In Australia, threat intelligence has moved from a best-practice posture to a legislated obligation. Commonwealth entities must participate in government intelligence sharing programmes and SOCI-regulated critical infrastructure operators must demonstrate threat-informed risk management as part of their mandatory Critical Infrastructure Risk Management Programs (CIRMPs). Threat intelligence is no longer optional for any materially sized Australian organisation.

---

## Why It Matters

When threat intelligence is absent or done poorly:

- **SOC analysts chase noise.** Without context, every alert is treated with equal urgency. Teams burn out on false positives while missing the signals that matter.
- **Defenders are always reactive.** Organisations learn about a threat campaign after being compromised, rather than after a peer organisation was hit.
- **Risk decisions lack grounding.** Risk registers are populated with generic threats ("ransomware") rather than specific, sector-relevant adversaries with known TTPs and realistic likelihood.
- **Incident response is slower.** Analysts who know the adversary group active in their sector, their tooling, and their typical dwell time contain incidents faster than those starting from zero.
- **Vulnerability prioritisation fails.** Without intelligence on which vulnerabilities are being actively exploited against organisations like yours, patching is driven by CVSS scores rather than actual exploitation risk.
- **Compliance obligations cannot be met.** Under SOCI, the CIRMP must address cyber hazards based on the organisation's actual threat environment. Without a functioning intelligence capability, this assessment cannot be performed credibly and the organisation cannot demonstrate CIRMP effectiveness to its board or to the Cyber and Infrastructure Security Centre (CISC).

Mandiant M-Trends 2025 (covering 2024 data) reported a global median dwell time of 11 days overall — but 26 days when a compromise was identified by an external entity, versus 10 days when identified internally. The difference between self-detection and external notification is largely attributable to monitoring maturity and the use of intelligence-driven detections. The same report noted that 57% of organisations still first learned of a compromise from an external source.

## Core Concepts

**The intelligence cycle.** Raw data does not become intelligence on its own. The traditional cycle runs:

```
Direction ──► Collection ──► Processing ──► Analysis ──► Dissemination ──► Feedback
   ▲                                                                           │
   └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Direction sets the questions that need answering (Priority Intelligence Requirements). Collection gathers relevant data. Processing normalises and organises it. Analysis contextualises and assesses it. Dissemination delivers finished intelligence to the right audience in a usable form. Feedback closes the loop. In practice this is not linear — requirements shift as collection surfaces new information.

**Intelligence tiers.** Intelligence is described across three levels, each with a different audience and shelf life:

```
STRATEGIC  ─ Why and who
  e.g. "Nation-state actors are targeting Australian critical infrastructure
        operators ahead of geopolitical events"
  Audience: board, executives, risk management
  Shelf life: months to years

OPERATIONAL ─ What campaigns are currently active
  e.g. "APT41 is running a spear-phishing campaign targeting defence contractors
        using lure documents referencing procurement tenders"
  Audience: security managers, incident response leads
  Shelf life: weeks to months

TACTICAL ─ How (TTPs) and specific indicators
  e.g. MITRE ATT&CK T1566.001, specific C2 domains, YARA rules, file hashes
  Audience: SOC analysts, detection engineers, threat hunters
  Shelf life: hours to days (IOCs) / months to years (TTPs)
```

**Indicators of Compromise (IOCs) vs TTPs.** IOCs (IPs, domains, hashes) are cheap for defenders to share but equally cheap for adversaries to rotate. TTPs — the tactics, techniques, and procedures that describe _how_ an adversary operates — are expensive for the adversary to change and far more durable as detection targets. Intelligence-mature programmes prioritise TTP-based detection over IOC blocking.

**Pyramid of Pain.** David Bianco's model captures the asymmetry between what is easy to share and what is actually painful for the adversary to change:

```
             /  TTPs  \              <- hardest for adversary to change
            /──────────\
           /   Tools    \
          /──────────────\
         / Network & Host \
        /   Artefacts      \
       /────────────────────\
      /    Domain Names      \
     /────────────────────────\
    /      IP Addresses        \
   /────────────────────────────\
  /        Hash Values           \  <- easiest for adversary to replace
 /────────────────────────────────\
```

Operationalising TTPs in detection rules is the goal. Blocking a file hash is a speed bump; detecting the lateral movement technique an adversary consistently uses is a sustained advantage.

**Threat actor classification.** Adversaries are generally categorised by motivation:

```
Nation-state / state-nexus    ─ Espionage, disruption, sabotage; well-resourced,
                                patient, technically advanced
Cybercriminal                 ─ Financially motivated; ransomware, fraud,
                                Initial Access Brokers (IABs), MaaS ecosystems
Hacktivist                    ─ Ideologically motivated; DDoS, defacement, data
                                leaks; increasing overlap with state-nexus actors
Insider                       ─ Current or former employee/contractor; motivated
                                by grievance, coercion, or financial gain
Private Sector Offensive      ─ Commercial surveillance and intrusion vendors
  Actors (PSOAs)                (e.g. spyware firms targeting individuals)
```

**Attribution.** Attributing a cyber intrusion to a specific threat actor is analytically difficult and rarely certain. Intelligence analysts express confidence levels (high/medium/low) against attribution judgements, which should always be explicitly caveated. Sophisticated adversaries deliberately plant false flags. Public attribution carries legal and diplomatic weight and should never rest on technical indicators alone.

**Collection sources.** Intelligence is gathered from multiple source categories:

```
OSINT (Open Source Intelligence)
  ─ Vendor threat reports, government advisories, academic research, news,
    social media, paste sites, code repositories, certificate transparency logs

HUMINT (Human Intelligence)
  ─ Analyst networks, ISAC peer relationships, vendor briefings,
    law enforcement liaisons

TECHINT (Technical Intelligence)
  ─ Internal telemetry (SIEM, EDR, NDR), honeypots, malware analysis,
    network traffic analysis, deception technologies

DARKINT (Dark Web / Underground Forum Monitoring)
  ─ Tracking threat actor forums, IAB marketplaces, ransomware leak sites,
    credential dumps; provides early warning of targeted campaigns
    and data exposure

Commercial feeds
  ─ Aggregated IOC and TTP feeds from vendors (Recorded Future, Flashpoint,
    Mandiant, etc.)

Government and sector feeds
  ─ ASD ACSC and CTIS (Australia — mandatory for NCEs), CISA AIS (US),
    FS-ISAC, H-ISAC, sector-specific sharing communities
```

**Confidence scoring.** Intelligence products should carry explicit confidence ratings covering source reliability, information credibility, and analytical confidence. Without confidence indicators, consumers cannot calibrate how much weight to place on a product or indicator.

**Priority Intelligence Requirements (PIRs).** PIRs are the questions an organisation needs intelligence to answer in order to make security decisions. They drive collection focus and ensure the programme produces relevant outputs rather than generic reports. PIRs should be set by senior stakeholders, reviewed at least quarterly, and tied directly to business risk. Under SOCI, PIRs should explicitly address the four CIRMP hazard categories and the specific adversary landscape for the organisation's sector.

## Australian Compliance Context

_Full regulatory detail is in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md). Volatile figures are in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md)._


This section covers obligations that are specific to Australian organisations operating under the PSPF, SOCI Act, or Cyber Security Act 2024. These are verified mandatory requirements, not recommendations.

### Protective Security Policy Framework (PSPF)

The PSPF applies mandatorily to non-corporate Commonwealth entities (NCEs — most departments and agencies). It extends in practice to contractors, cloud providers, and suppliers handling Commonwealth data through contract terms, panel agreements, and IRAP requirements. PSPF is updated annually. PSPF Release 2026 took effect 1 July 2026 and is now the current release; Release 2025 was published 24 July 2025.

**Direct threat intelligence obligations under PSPF:**

Three binding Directions issued July 2024 created mandatory threat intelligence obligations:

```
PSPF Direction 003-2024: Supporting Visibility of the Cyber Threat
  ─ Mandatory participation in ASD's Cyber Security Partnership Program
  ─ Mandatory connection to ASD's Cyber Threat Intelligence Sharing (CTIS) platform
  ─ Mandatory sharing of cyber threat information with ASD for entities using
    threat intelligence sharing platforms
  ─ Mandatory disclosure to ASD of any cyber threat hunting capability
    employed by the entity
  ─ All NCEs must have joined the ASD Partnership Program (99% compliance
    as at June 2025 per ASD Commonwealth Cyber Security Posture 2025)

PSPF Direction 002-2024: Vulnerable Technology Management
  ─ Technology asset stocktake of all internet-facing systems and services
  ─ Active management of vulnerable technology risks
  ─ Directly informs threat intelligence collection requirements and PIRs

PSPF Direction 001-2024: Foreign Ownership, Control or Influence (FOCI)
  ─ FOCI risk identification and management in technology procurement
  ─ Intelligence about foreign state-linked vendors is a direct input to this
    assessment process
```

PSPF Release 2024 and 2025 also formally embedded ASD Cyber Security Partnership Program and CTIS participation into the PSPF requirements structure itself (not only as Directions). PSPF 2025 added requirements around Zero Trust, AI security, and post-quantum cryptography — each creating new intelligence collection requirements.

**ASD Cyber Threat Intelligence Sharing (CTIS) platform:**

CTIS is ASD's operational threat intelligence sharing platform. It operates on TAXII2 APIs and enables bi-directional, machine-speed exchange of IOCs, TTPs, malware signatures, and threat data between ASD and government and industry partners. Integration plugins exist for Microsoft Sentinel and Splunk Enterprise Security. CTIS participation requires ASD Network Partnership status.

Key operational facts:

- Over 1.37 million pieces of threat intelligence were shared through CTIS in the 12 months to June 2024
- 35 government entities connected as at June 2025, with 18 more in pipeline
- Average of 129,000 unique IOCs shared monthly through the platform

**For private sector organisations**, CTIS participation is voluntary but strongly encouraged. The government has signalled that extending mandatory sharing obligations to the private sector is under consideration.

### Security of Critical Infrastructure (SOCI) Act 2018 (as amended to 2025)

The SOCI Act covers 11 critical infrastructure sectors: energy, communications, data storage or processing, financial services and markets, water and sewerage, health care and medical, higher education and research, food and grocery, transport, space technology, and the defence industry. The ERP Act 2024 (in force 20 December 2024) expanded the definition of "critical infrastructure asset" to expressly include data storage systems that store or process business critical data.

**Threat intelligence obligations under SOCI:**

The Critical Infrastructure Risk Management Program (CIRMP) is the centrepiece of SOCI compliance for responsible entities. It creates direct threat intelligence requirements:

```
CIRMP Requirement: Four mandatory hazard domains
  1. Cyber and information security hazards
  2. Personnel security hazards
  3. Supply chain hazards
  4. Physical security hazards

For the cyber hazard domain, entities must demonstrate:
  ─ Processes to minimise or eliminate material risk of cyber hazards
  ─ Processes to mitigate the impact of cyber hazards if they occur
  ─ Alignment to one of five recognised cybersecurity frameworks
    (Essential Eight, NIST CSF, C2M2, ISO 27001, or AESCSF — mandatory
    from August 2024)
  ─ Annual board-approved report submitted within 90 days of financial year end
```

A CIRMP cyber hazard assessment that is not grounded in current, sector-relevant threat intelligence will not withstand scrutiny. The CISC has authority to direct entities to vary a seriously deficient CIRMP. Board members who sign off on a CIRMP without understanding its threat basis face governance exposure.

**Mandatory cyber incident reporting timelines:**

```
Significant impact (material disruption to the asset): report within 12 hours
Other notifiable incidents (potential disruption, confidentiality loss,
or loss of control): report within 72 hours

Reports go to ASD. This data feeds directly into ASD's national threat
intelligence picture and CTIS.
```

**Systems of National Significance (SoNS):** Entities designated as SoNS face enhanced cyber security obligations including mandatory proactive threat monitoring, cyber security exercises, incident response planning, and provision of system information to government. These obligations map directly to intelligence programme requirements.

**Supply chain hazards:** SOCI explicitly requires CIRMP to address supply chain risks. Threat intelligence about adversaries who target vendors and managed service providers is a mandatory input to this assessment.

### Cyber Security Act 2024 (Royal Assent 29 November 2024)

**Mandatory ransomware payment reporting (in force 30 May 2025):**

```
Who must report:
  ─ Businesses carrying on business in Australia with annual turnover > AUD$3M
  ─ Responsible entities for critical infrastructure assets under SOCI

Reporting obligation:
  ─ Report within 72 hours of making a ransomware or cyber extortion payment
  ─ Report to ASD via ReportCyber portal
  ─ Must include: ransom demanded, amount paid, malware variants used,
    vulnerabilities exploited, communications with threat actor

Limited use protections:
  ─ Report cannot be used for civil or regulatory enforcement against the entity
  ─ Information can be used by ASD for intelligence purposes
  ─ Penalty for non-reporting: 60 penalty units per contravention
    (a penalty unit is $330 from 7 Nov 2024; the body corporate
    multiplier is 5x, so up to $99,000 for a corporation)
```

This reporting obligation feeds directly into Australia's national threat intelligence picture. Each ransomware report provides ASD with intelligence on active threat actor TTPs, initial access vectors, and affected vulnerability classes — this data is used to produce advisories and update CTIS.

**Voluntary information sharing with NCSC:** The Act also establishes a limited use regime for voluntary sharing of incident information with the National Cyber Security Coordinator (NCSC). Information shared voluntarily cannot be used for enforcement. This is designed to lower barriers to threat intelligence sharing during significant incidents.

## How It Works in Practice

**The Threat Intelligence Platform (TIP).** Most organisations of meaningful size centralise intelligence in a TIP. Commercial examples include Recorded Future, ThreatConnect, and Anomali. Open-source options include MISP and OpenCTI. The TIP aggregates feeds from paid providers, open sources (VirusTotal, AlienVault OTX, Shodan), government feeds including ASD CTIS, and community ISACs. It normalises, deduplicates, scores, enriches, and routes indicators to downstream controls.

**The detection and response flow.** The diagram below illustrates how external threat intelligence feeds into the SOC detection and response pipeline:

```
DISCOVERY
  External Threat Intel (incl. ASD CTIS) ─────────────────────────────────────┐
  Internal Telemetry (Logs / Endpoint / Network)                              │
    ├── Correlation (SIEM)                                                    │
    ├── Behavioural Analytics / UEBA                   PRELIMINARY            │
    ├── Anomaly Analytics                              INVESTIGATION          │
    └── Deception Technologies                        ┌──────────────────────┘
                                                      │
                                                      ▼
                                         Linking / Scoring / Merging /
                                         Preliminary Scoping
                                                      │
                                                      ▼
                                         TRIAGE ── Ranking
                                                      │
                                          ┌───────────┴──────────────┐
                                          ▼                          ▼
                                   EXTENDED INVESTIGATION      CONTAIN / RESPOND
                                   Full Scoping                Quarantine
                                   Visualisation               Remediate
                                   Enrichment                  Update
                                                               Share (incl. ASD)
```

**SOC integration.** Within the SOC, Threat Intelligence is typically a Level 3 / SME function. The Threat Intel SME produces actor profiles, curated IOC feeds, detection hypotheses, and threat hunting leads. Detection engineers translate TTP intelligence into SIEM correlation rules, EDR signatures, and hunting queries. Tier 1 and Tier 2 analysts consume tactical intelligence during triage and investigation.

**Roles in a mature programme:**

```
Intelligence Manager    ─ Sets PIRs, owns stakeholder relationships, governs
                          sharing agreements, manages CTIS/ASD relationship,
                          ensures TLP compliance
Intelligence Analyst    ─ Performs analysis, produces finished intelligence
                          products, maintains actor and campaign profiles
Collection Manager      ─ Manages feed sources including CTIS, ISACs, and
                          commercial providers; evaluates quality and coverage
Detection Engineer      ─ Translates TTP intelligence into detection rules and
                          SIEM/EDR content
Threat Hunter           ─ Applies intelligence-derived hypotheses to internal
                          telemetry through proactive hunting operations
SOC Analyst (consumer)  ─ Applies tactical intelligence during alert triage
                          and investigation
```

**Feed lifecycle management.** Intelligence feeds require active governance, not just ingestion:

```
Ingest ──► Score (confidence, source reliability, relevance to organisation)
       ──► Age   (apply time decay — most IOCs lose value within 30 days)
       ──► Validate (measure against internal telemetry for false positive rate)
       ──► Route    (push to appropriate controls: firewall, DNS, SIEM, EDR)
       ──► Retire   (automatically decommission stale or low-value indicators)
```

Failure to age and retire indicators is a primary driver of alert fatigue and analyst desensitisation to IOC-sourced alerts.

**Integration with vulnerability management.** Intelligence should directly drive patch prioritisation. A CVE with CVSS 8.0 that is not actively exploited in the wild may warrant lower priority than a CVE with CVSS 6.5 that an active threat actor is weaponising against your sector. CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalogue provides a practical, intelligence-backed prioritisation mechanism. Under SOCI, intelligence-informed vulnerability prioritisation is part of demonstrating effective CIRMP cyber hazard management.

**Producing finished intelligence.** Raw indicator feeds are not finished intelligence products. Finished products — adversary profiles, campaign assessments, sector threat reports, defensive recommendations with specific control actions — require analytical work. Organisations that subscribe to feeds without this analytical layer are collecting data, not producing intelligence. For SOCI entities, a CIRMP cyber hazard section supported only by commercial feed subscriptions, without analysis, is unlikely to demonstrate the required level of risk understanding.

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Treating intelligence as a feed subscription.** Pushing a commercial IOC feed directly to a SIEM blocklist is not a threat intelligence programme. Without analysis there is no assessment of relevance, confidence, or applicability. The value of intelligence is in what it enables, not what it collects.

**No defined PIRs.** Without requirements, teams collect everything and answer nothing. The TIP fills with millions of indicators, none validated against the organisation's actual threat profile. PIRs are the most important governance mechanism in any intelligence programme.

**Consuming without contributing.** Organisations join ASD's Partnership Program or sector ISACs for incoming feeds but share nothing back. This degrades collective national intelligence. Under PSPF Direction 003-2024, government entities are not only required to connect to CTIS but to share threat information with ASD — passive receipt is not sufficient.

**Alert fatigue from unaged, low-quality IOCs.** Bulk ingestion from unvalidated sources floods detection pipelines with stale or irrelevant indicators. Analysts learn to ignore IOC-sourced alerts. Confidence-weighted, time-decayed indicators with relevance scoring are essential — quality over quantity.

**Strategic intelligence that never reaches decision-makers.** Analysts produce technically dense reports that executives cannot act on. Under SOCI, board members must sign off on the annual CIRMP — if they do not understand the threat basis for the programme, the organisation has a governance failure, not just a communications problem.

**CIRMP threat assessment not grounded in intelligence.** A CIRMP that lists generic cyber hazards without connecting them to the organisation's actual sector threat landscape will not demonstrate effective hazard management. The CISC expects meaningful compliance, not paperwork compliance.

**Ignoring internal telemetry as a source.** External commercial feeds dominate most programmes. Internal incident history, red team outputs, threat hunting findings, and vulnerability data are frequently the richest source of organisation-specific intelligence and are underutilised.

**Overconfident attribution.** Asserting attribution based on shared tooling or infrastructure without adequate confidence caveats. Attribution should always carry an explicit confidence statement and never rest on technical indicators alone.

**TLP violations.** Resharing TLP:RED or TLP:AMBER intelligence — including from ASD CTIS — beyond its designated audience has legal and community trust consequences. ASD CTIS intelligence carries handling requirements that must be understood before the platform is connected.

**Neglecting the dark web.** Threat actor forums, IAB marketplaces, and ransomware leak sites provide early warning of targeted campaigns, credential exposure, and planned attacks. Most organisations do not monitor these sources at all.

**No feedback loop from operations.** Intelligence programmes produce content but rarely receive structured feedback on whether it helped. Without feedback, the programme cannot improve and CIRMP reviews cannot demonstrate programme effectiveness.

## Connections to Other Domains

- **[Cyber Governance and Management (1)](01.%20Cyber%20Governance%20and%20Management.md):** Senior leadership must be consumers of strategic intelligence. Under PSPF and SOCI, governance frameworks must mandate PIR setting, intelligence programme resourcing, and escalation triggers. SOCI requires board-approved CIRMP annual reports — the board must understand the threat basis for the programme.
- **[Cyber Risk Management (2)](02.%20Cyber%20Risk%20Management.md):** Intelligence grounds risk assessments in real, active threats. Under SOCI CIRMP, threat-informed risk assessment across all four hazard domains is mandatory. Threat actor capability and intent directly informs likelihood scoring.
- **[Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance (3)](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md):** PSPF Direction 003-2024, SOCI CIRMP requirements, and the Cyber Security Act 2024 ransomware reporting obligation all create direct legal requirements around threat intelligence sharing and reporting. Legal review of sharing agreements and TLP handling obligations is essential. The Cyber Security Act's limited use protections exist specifically to facilitate sharing without enforcement risk.
- **[Privacy and Data Protection (4)](04.%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Protection.md):** IOCs frequently contain PII. STIX/TAXII sharing through CTIS must account for the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth). The Cyber Security Act's limited use provisions restrict how incident data shared with ASD can be used, providing some protection — but this does not remove Privacy Act obligations on the reporting entity.
- **[Vendor Management (5)](05.%20Vendor%20Management.md):** SOCI CIRMP explicitly mandates supply chain hazard assessment. Threat intelligence about adversaries who target vendors, MSPs, and software suppliers is a required input. Foreign interference intelligence feeds directly into PSPF Direction 001-2024 FOCI assessments.
- **[Asset and Change Management (6)](06.%20Asset%20and%20Change%20Management.md):** PSPF Direction 002-2024 requires a technology asset stocktake of all internet-facing systems. Threat intelligence about active exploitation of specific technology products should trigger urgent change management. Intelligence about which assets adversaries prioritise informs asset criticality ratings.
- **[Network and Communication Security (7)](07.%20Network%20and%20Communication%20Security.md):** Tactical IOCs from ASD CTIS — C2 domains, malicious IPs, DNS patterns — are actioned directly as network-layer controls. The ASD's Australian Protective Domain Name Service (AUPDNS) is a free service that uses threat intelligence to block malicious domains; 55 government entities subscribed as at June 2025.
- **[Identity and Access Management (8)](08.%20Identity%20and%20Access%20Management.md):** Credential theft TTPs, active phishing kit campaigns, and adversary-in-the-middle tooling should drive MFA enforcement priorities, suspicious login detection tuning, and session management controls. Under SOCI SoNS enhanced obligations, identity-related threat monitoring is a specific requirement.
- **[Cyber Testing (10)](10.%20Cyber%20Testing.md):** Intelligence-driven adversary emulation using real actor TTPs produces far more operationally realistic results than generic penetration testing. Purple team exercises built around current ASD advisories and CTIS intelligence are directly relevant to SoNS cyber exercise obligations.
- **[Security Operations and Monitoring (12)](12.%20Security%20Operations%20and%20Monitoring.md):** The primary tactical intelligence consumer. Intelligence drives detection content creation, alert tuning, and threat hunting hypotheses. For SOCI SoNS entities, proactive threat monitoring is a mandatory enhanced obligation — this requires an intelligence-driven detection programme, not reactive alerting alone.
- **[Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning (13)](13.%20Incident%20Management%20and%20Business%20Continuity%20Planning.md):** Intelligence accelerates incident response. SOCI 12-hour and 72-hour incident reporting deadlines require detection capability that is intelligence-informed. Post-incident data submitted to ASD feeds back into CTIS and the national threat picture. BCP design should account for the threat actor categories most likely to cause extended disruption in the specific sector.
- **[Cloud Management (14)](14.%20Cloud%20Management.md):** Cloud-specific TTPs — identity-based attacks on control planes, abuse of cloud-native services for lateral movement, misconfiguration exploitation — require dedicated collection sources. PSPF 2025 introduced cloud-based gateway security requirements that need intelligence to configure effectively.
- **[Software Development Security (15)](15.%20Software%20Development%20Security.md):** Intelligence about active exploitation of specific libraries, frameworks, or supply chain components should directly inform development team patch prioritisation and dependency audits. Under SOCI, software supply chain risk is an explicit CIRMP hazard domain.
- **[DevOps and DevSecOps (16)](16.%20DevOps%20and%20DevSecOps.md):** Intelligence about CI/CD pipeline attacks and code repository compromises should be integrated into DevSecOps threat modelling and control decisions.
- **[Cyber Architecture (17)](17.%20Cyber%20Architecture.md):** Threat-informed design — using intelligence about adversary TTPs and objectives to shape architectural decisions — is the basis of zero trust architecture. PSPF 2025 explicitly introduced Zero Trust requirements for NCEs; intelligence about how adversaries move through environments shapes segmentation, detection placement, and deception strategies.
- **[Cyber Culture and Education (18)](18.%20Cyber%20Culture%20and%20Education.md):** Security awareness programmes are more effective when grounded in current intelligence about social engineering campaigns targeting the organisation or sector. ASD CTIS advisories provide specific phishing lure content and pretexting themes that make training concrete and timely.
- **[LLM Security (19)](19.%20LLM%20Security.md):** An emerging intelligence domain. Collection requirements now include tracking AI-generated spear phishing at scale, prompt injection campaigns, and adversarial use of LLMs for malware development and reconnaissance automation. PSPF 2025 introduced new AI security requirements for NCEs.
- **[Physical Security (20)](20.%20Physical%20Security.md):** Nation-state and insider threat intelligence may carry physical security implications — physical surveillance preceding a cyber intrusion, or insider threats who operate across both physical and digital domains. SOCI CIRMP covers physical security hazards as one of its four mandatory domains; intelligence programmes should feed physical security risk assessments as well as cyber ones.
