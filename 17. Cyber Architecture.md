# Cyber Architecture

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Cyber architecture is the discipline of deliberately designing information systems so that security properties are built in from the start, not added after deployment. It sits at the intersection of enterprise IT architecture and security engineering, translating business requirements and risk appetite into concrete structural decisions: what components exist, how they connect, where trust boundaries lie, how data flows across those boundaries, and what controls govern each crossing. Architecture, framework, and model are related but distinct terms. An architecture is the documented thinking — the abstract design of how parts relate and why those choices were made. A framework (SABSA, TOGAF, NIST CSF) is a pre-built, reusable structure that guides and constrains architecture development. A model is a representation used to reason about something — a threat model, the OSI model, a trust model. An organisation's security architecture will draw on multiple frameworks simultaneously and at multiple layers.

An enterprise security architecture does not itself detect or prevent threats. It establishes the blueprint — the trust zones, the policy enforcement points, the control placement — within which every other security function operates. Identity management, incident detection, network monitoring, and data protection all depend on whether the underlying architecture was designed coherently. Security governance must be in place before a sound security architecture can be developed. The architecture implements policy and strategy; it does not invent them.

---

## Why It Matters

Poor architectural decisions are extraordinarily expensive to fix. Unlike a misconfigured firewall rule, a fundamental design flaw — implicit internal trust, a flat network spanning all business units, no separation between production and development — can persist for years and silently expand the blast radius of every incident that occurs.

Real-world consequences:

- **Lateral movement at scale.** The 2017 NotPetya incident demonstrated that flat internal networks with no meaningful segmentation allowed ransomware to propagate across entire enterprises in minutes. Maersk lost an estimated USD 300 million, partly because the architecture provided no internal barriers. Lateral movement remains a driver of many successful breaches (unverified — confirm any specific percentage against the IBM Cost of a Data Breach Report), yet only a small minority of organisations have deployed meaningful microsegmentation (unverified — confirm against the cited Black Hat 2025 source).
- **Perimeter collapse.** Architectures that assumed a trusted internal network became untenable as remote work, BYOD, and cloud adoption dissolved the perimeter. The 2020 SolarWinds supply chain compromise penetrated organisations relying entirely on perimeter-based trust, with attackers operating undetected inside trusted networks for months.
- **Uncontrolled privilege.** Over-provisioned service accounts and standing privileged access are architectural decisions, not misconfigurations. Unit 42 incident response data shows compromised credentials were the initial access vector in over 20% of investigated cases. Attacker breakout time from initial access to lateral movement has dropped to as little as 51 seconds in documented cases (CrowdStrike 2024 data).
- **Shadow IT and ungoverned connectivity.** When architecture teams are not involved in procurement or cloud adoption, business units deploy systems outside any security design, creating blind spots that no monitoring tool can reach.
- **Architecture scope gaps.** Excluding OT, IoT, or supply chain connectivity from architectural scope creates ungoverned corridors. The 2024 trend of perimeter weaponisation — attackers exploiting VPN gateways, firewalls, and edge appliances as primary entry points — reflects a failure to architect those boundaries with the same rigour as internal systems.

The cost of retrofitting architecture — adding segmentation, re-architecting identity, implementing Zero Trust controls — is orders of magnitude higher than designing correctly from the start.

## Core Concepts

**Architecture vs. framework vs. model.** These are not interchangeable. Architecture is the documented design rationale. A framework is a reusable structure that guides how to build the architecture. A model is a representation for reasoning (threat model, data flow diagram). Confusing these causes organisations to adopt a framework and call it an architecture, producing controls lists with no traceability to actual risk or structural design.

**Enterprise security architecture.** A blueprint mapping security capabilities and controls to business requirements and risk. It spans people, process, and technology, and must be traceable back to business objectives. An architecture element that cannot be linked to a business requirement or a risk scenario should be questioned.

**Defence in depth.** Multiple independent layers of controls so that the failure of any single control does not lead to full compromise. This is a design principle, not a product category. Layers cover physical, network, host, application, and data. The goal is to make an attacker's progression progressively harder and to create detection opportunities between layers.

**Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA).** Defined in NIST SP 800-207 (2020), Zero Trust means no subject — user, device, or service — receives implicit trust based solely on network location or ownership. Every access request is evaluated at the time of the request using identity, device posture, and context. The three core logical components are:

- **Policy Engine (PE):** Makes the access decision, evaluating identity assurance, device posture, behavioural signals, threat intelligence, and policy rules.
- **Policy Administrator (PA):** Communicates the PE's decision to the enforcement point and manages session credentials or tokens.
- **Policy Enforcement Point (PEP):** Applies the decision — grants, denies, or terminates the connection between a subject and a resource.

ZTA does not replace defence in depth. It removes the assumption that the internal network is safe and shifts enforcement from the network boundary to the resource boundary.

```
Zero Trust Policy Decision Flow
=================================================================
  Subject (User / Device / Service / Workload)
         |
         v
  Policy Enforcement Point (PEP)
  [Blocks all access until a decision is received]
         |
         v
  Policy Administrator (PA)
  [Translates PE decision into action; manages session tokens]
         |
         v
  Policy Engine (PE)
  [Evaluates: identity assurance level
              + device health/posture
              + behavioural signals
              + threat intelligence feeds
              + policy rules]
         |
    Decision: Allow / Deny / Allow with conditions
         |
         v
  Resource (Application / Data / Service)
=================================================================
Continuous telemetry feeds back to the PE throughout the
session — trust is not permanently granted once established
=================================================================
```

**Zones of trust and trust boundaries.** Architecture divides systems into zones based on their security posture. Data moving between zones crosses a trust boundary and must be subject to controls — validation, inspection, transformation — appropriate to the risk. The NCSC defines a zone of trust as a group of components sharing a similar security posture within a defined boundary. Even apparently one-way data flows often involve hidden bidirectional exchanges; controls must account for both directions simultaneously.

**Secure by design.** Security properties are specified as requirements before design begins. Core engineering principles include least privilege, fail secure (default to deny on failure), economy of mechanism (simplicity reduces attack surface), complete mediation (every access is checked every time), separation of duties, and open design (security does not depend on secrecy of the mechanism). CISA and international partners — including the ASD's ACSC, NCSC UK, and others — published joint Secure by Design guidance in 2023 urging technology manufacturers to ship products secure out of the box.

**Threat modelling.** A structured process for identifying what can go wrong in a design before it is built. Performed at design time using data flow diagrams to map components, data stores, processes, external entities, and trust boundary crossings. Common approaches:

- **STRIDE** (Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclosure, Denial of Service, Elevation of Privilege): Developed by Microsoft. Threat categories map directly to security controls. Best suited to structured, component-level analysis during design.
- **PASTA** (Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis): A seven-stage, risk-centric, attacker-focused methodology incorporating business context, operational impact, and simulated attack paths. Better suited to complex enterprise-level systems requiring business risk alignment.

Threat modelling is not a one-time activity. It should be repeated when architecture changes materially, when significant new components are added, or when the threat landscape shifts.

**Architecture layers.** TOGAF models enterprise architecture at four levels: Business, Application (Information Systems), Data, and Technology. Security architecture must address all four. Security requirements originating at the business layer must be traceable through design to specific technical controls.

**The Zachman Framework.** A two-dimensional classification schema for describing an enterprise — a 6x6 matrix of six stakeholder perspectives against six interrogatives (What, How, Where, Who, When, Why). SABSA closely follows the Zachman structure, adapting it to a security focus. The Zachman Framework prevents scope gaps; TOGAF and SABSA provide the methodology.

**The SABSA model.** SABSA (Sherwood Applied Business Security Architecture) is a business-driven, risk-focused security architecture methodology. It applies across six layers — Contextual (Business), Conceptual (Architecture), Logical (Design), Physical (Build), Component (Tools), and Service Management — using Zachman's six interrogatives at each layer to ensure complete traceability from business context to operational security controls.

**TOGAF ADM (Architecture Development Method).** An iterative process for developing enterprise architecture. Security requirements must be injected at every phase, not added as an afterthought at phase D (Technology Architecture).

**Cybersecurity Mesh Architecture (CSMA).** A Gartner strategic concept that moves beyond siloed, product-centric security toward a modular, composable security ecosystem. CSMA organises around four layers: security analytics and intelligence; distributed identity fabric; consolidated policy management; and integrated dashboards. It is an architectural philosophy aligned with ZTA and microsegmentation approaches in multi-cloud and hybrid environments.

## Australian Compliance Context

_Full regulatory detail is in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md). Volatile figures are in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md)._


This section covers the Australian legislative and regulatory requirements that directly constrain or shape security architecture decisions. These apply in addition to international standards and are not optional for the entities they cover.

### Protective Security Policy Framework (PSPF)

The PSPF, maintained by the Department of Home Affairs, sets mandatory protective security requirements for non-corporate Commonwealth entities (NCEs) — most federal departments and agencies. It is reviewed annually. PSPF Release 2026 took effect 1 July 2026 and is now the current release; Release 2025 (published 24 July 2025) represented the most substantial technology-domain update to date. Verify current requirements against the annual release page.

**Who it applies to:** Non-corporate Commonwealth entities are legally bound. In practice, requirements extend to corporate Commonwealth entities, contractors, and service providers handling Commonwealth information or assets through contractual deeds. Non-compliance increasingly affects tender eligibility and service continuity.

**Architecture-relevant PSPF requirements:**

The PSPF is organised into six security domains: Governance, Risk, Information, Technology, Personnel, and Physical. The Technology domain carries the most direct architecture obligations.

- **Zero Trust Culture (PSPF 2025, Requirement 0098 and a new Zero Trust Culture requirement).** PSPF 2025 formally mandates that entities develop, implement, and maintain a cybersecurity strategy and uplift plan aligned to the ISM and the _Guiding Principles to Embed a Zero Trust Culture_. This is an organisation-wide obligation — not purely a technical one. The standalone Zero Trust Culture requirement covers governance, identity, verification, and access. <!-- TODO: verify requirement number against current PSPF list of requirements --> Security architecture must be designed with Zero Trust principles from the outset, not as a retrofit.

- **Gateway security.** PSPF 2025 introduced a new Gateway Security Standard (released in parallel by the Department of Home Affairs in July 2025). Gateways are the set of capabilities controlling data flow between security domains — into and out of an entity's ICT environment. Architecture must specify gateway controls proportionate to classification levels. For SECRET and TOP SECRET, Cross Domain Solutions (CDS) with ASD involvement are required. For PROTECTED and below, IRAP-assessed solutions hosted in Hosting Certification Framework (HCF) certified facilities are the benchmark. ASD's ACSC has updated Gateway Guidance to account for cloud-based Security Service Edge (SSE) solutions and a risk-based authorisation model.

- **Information security classification and security domains.** The PSPF information classification scheme — OFFICIAL, OFFICIAL: Sensitive, PROTECTED, SECRET, TOP SECRET — is a direct architectural input. Systems must be designed to handle the classifications they will process, and the architecture must enforce handling requirements: access controls, encryption, audit logging, physical security of infrastructure, and transmission protections. The architecture determines which systems can exchange information across classification boundaries.

- **Systems of Government Significance (SoGS).** PSPF 2025 introduced mandatory cybersecurity obligations for declared Systems of Government Significance — critical digital services and their supporting systems. Security architects working on these systems face additional obligations including formal risk management, assessment, and authorisation requirements.

- **Technology Asset Management.** PSPF 2025 uplifted requirements for technology asset management, including a Technology Asset Stocktake obligation. Security architecture must produce and maintain an accurate asset inventory that reflects what is actually deployed, not just what was planned. Architecture drift — where the documented design diverges from reality — is now a compliance risk, not just a quality concern.

- **AI, post-quantum cryptography, and connected peripheral technologies.** PSPF 2025 introduced new sections on AI security, post-quantum cryptography planning, and connected peripheral device risks. Security architects in government must now address these as architectural requirements, not future considerations. The ISM (December 2024) has approved post-quantum cryptographic algorithms including Module-Lattice-Based Key Encapsulation Mechanism (ML-KEM), and architecture decisions about cryptographic agility must account for the quantum transition timeline.

- **Annual reporting.** Entities must report compliance against PSPF requirements annually. Architectural assessments and security posture reviews feed directly into this reporting cycle.

**IRAP (Infosec Registered Assessors Program).** For systems processing OFFICIAL: Sensitive, PROTECTED, SECRET, or TOP SECRET information, security assessment by an IRAP assessor is the standard mechanism for demonstrating ISM compliance. Architecture documentation — system security plans, security risk management plans, architecture diagrams — is a primary input to IRAP assessments. Architecture that has not been documented to the ISM standard cannot be effectively assessed, and assessing officers cannot grant authorisation to operate (ATO) without adequate documentation.

**The ASD Information Security Manual (ISM).** The ISM provides the technical security controls that implement PSPF obligations. Relevant to architecture:

- The ISM's risk management framework draws from NIST SP 800-37 Rev. 2 and requires classification-driven control selection. System classification (based on the sensitivity of data the system will store, process, or communicate) determines which ISM controls apply.
- Gateway controls in the ISM specify requirements for filtering, content inspection, logging, and assessment of gateways. ISM requires gateways to undergo IRAP assessment at least every 24 months.
- The ISM's cybersecurity principles are structured across four activities that map directly to architecture concerns: Govern (identify and manage risks), Protect (implement controls), Detect (detect security events), and Respond (respond to and recover from incidents).
- Approved cryptographic algorithms are specified in the ISM. Architecture must use ASD-Approved Cryptographic Algorithms; use of non-approved algorithms in government systems is a control deficiency.

**ASD's Essential Eight.** The Essential Eight are ASD's prioritised mitigation strategies — application control, patch applications, configure Microsoft Office macro settings, user application hardening, restrict administrative privileges, patch operating systems, MFA, and regular backups. They are structured across four maturity levels (ML0, ML1, ML2, ML3). For non-corporate Commonwealth entities, ML2 is the baseline mandatory requirement. ML3 is recommended for critical infrastructure and high-threat environments. The Essential Eight are not primarily an architecture framework, but they have direct architectural implications:

- Application control and user application hardening require endpoint architecture decisions about what can execute and where.
- Restriction of administrative privileges requires an identity and access architecture that eliminates standing admin access.
- MFA requirements must be built into authentication architecture across all user-facing systems.
- The Essential Eight applies to internet-connected IT networks. For OT or enterprise mobility, ASD acknowledges alternative strategies may be needed; architecture must address these separately.

### Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 (SOCI Act)

The SOCI Act, as amended by the Security Legislation Amendment (Critical Infrastructure) Act 2021 and the Security Legislation Amendment (Critical Infrastructure Protection) Act 2022, establishes the national framework for managing security risks to Australia's critical infrastructure. It covers 11 sectors including communications, data storage and processing, defence industry, energy, financial services and markets, food and grocery, health care and medical, higher education and research, space technology, transport, and water and sewerage.

**Who it applies to:** Responsible entities for critical infrastructure assets in the 11 covered sectors. The 2024 SOCI Amendment Act (Security of Critical Infrastructure and Other Legislation Amendment (Enhanced Response and Prevention) Act 2024) expanded the scope and strengthened obligations.

**Architecture-relevant SOCI obligations:**

- **Critical Infrastructure Risk Management Program (CIRMP).** Responsible entities must adopt, maintain, and comply with a written CIRMP. The CIRMP Rules require the program to take an all-hazards approach across four vectors: physical security and natural hazards; personnel hazards; supply chain hazards; and cyber security and information security hazards. Security architecture is the mechanism through which the cyber and information security hazard requirements are structurally addressed. The CIRMP is not a paper exercise — it must describe the actual processes and systems in place. As of August 2024, entities must also comply with a recognised cybersecurity framework as part of the CIRMP. The five designated frameworks are the Australian Energy Sector Cyber Security Framework (AESCSF), NIST CSF, ISO 27001/27002, the Essential Eight, and C2M2, plus an equivalent-framework pathway.

- **Mandatory cyber incident reporting.** Cyber incidents with significant impact on the availability, integrity, reliability, or confidentiality of a critical infrastructure asset must be reported to the ACSC within 12 hours (for incidents with a serious impact) and within 72 hours for incidents with a relevant impact. Architecture must support the detection and rapid classification of incidents — which requires logging architecture, monitoring coverage, and well-defined security zones so that analysts can determine scope quickly.

- **Systems of National Significance (SoNS).** Assets declared by the Minister as SoNS face enhanced cyber security obligations (ECSOs): incident response planning, cyber security exercises, vulnerability assessment, and system information reporting. Architecture for SoNS assets must be designed to support all four ECSOs, including the technical capability to provide near-real-time system information to ASD upon direction.

- **Supply chain security.** The CIRMP Rules require responsible entities to identify major suppliers and assess supply chain risks. Security architecture must address how third-party components, services, and access paths are scoped, isolated, and assessed. Every supplier connection is an architectural trust boundary.

- **Government assistance powers.** The SOCI Act grants government powers to direct or intervene in the event of a major cyber incident affecting a SoNS. Architecture that supports rapid isolation, network segmentation, and handover of system access to government responders is therefore a compliance consideration, not purely an operational one.

**Architecture obligations summary for SOCI-regulated entities:**

An entity subject to SOCI cannot treat security architecture as an internal matter. The CIRMP, incident reporting, and SoNS obligations all require that architecture decisions are documented, risks are formally assessed, and the system can be demonstrated to a regulator (the Cyber and Infrastructure Security Centre — CISC) as compliant with the adopted cybersecurity framework. Annual CIRMP reports, due within 90 days of 30 June each year, must reflect the actual security posture of the architecture, not a theoretical target state.

### 2023–2030 Australian Cyber Security Strategy

The Strategy, released in November 2023, sets the overarching policy direction for making Australia one of the most cyber secure nations in the world by 2030. The PSPF is cited as a source of security best practices within the Strategy. Architecture practitioners in both government and critical infrastructure should be aware of the Strategy's six cyber shields framework — strong businesses and citizens, safe technology, world-class threat sharing and blocking, protected critical infrastructure, sovereign capabilities, and resilient region and global leadership. The PSPF and SOCI reform programmes are the primary legislative and regulatory instruments implementing the Strategy.

## How It Works in Practice

**The security architect role.** The ISC2 ISSAP (Information Systems Security Architecture Professional) defines the security architect role across six domains: Architect for Governance, Compliance and Risk Management (17%); Security Architecture Modelling (15%); Infrastructure Security Architecture (21%); Identity and Access Management Architecture (16%); Architect for Application Security (13%); and Security Operations Architecture (18%). The architect sits between the C-suite and implementation, advising on design without owning business systems or compliance obligations. Their primary outputs are design decisions, documented rationale, control specifications, and architecture artefacts.

**Architecture review in the change process.** In a mature organisation, no significant technology change — new system procurement, cloud migration, network reconfiguration, major software deployment — proceeds without an architecture review. The architect assesses whether the change introduces new trust boundaries, expands the attack surface, creates resilience risks, or violates established design principles. In government contexts, significant changes also trigger obligations under the PSPF change management and authorisation framework — the ATO may need to be updated.

**Typical architecture artefacts produced:**

- Architecture principles — what the organisation will and will not do by design
- Reference architectures — approved standard patterns for cloud connectivity, remote access, third-party integration, DMZ design, and classification zone boundaries
- Security domain models — which systems sit in which zone (classification-aligned in government), with what controls at each boundary
- Data flow diagrams with trust boundary markings — primary input to threat modelling and privacy compliance
- Threat models — STRIDE or PASTA applied to specific systems, changes, or new capabilities
- System Security Plan (SSP) — the primary documentation artefact for ISM/IRAP assessment; includes system description, classification, controls implementation, and risk treatment
- Security Risk Management Plan (SRMP) — documents residual risks accepted by the authorising officer
- Architecture decision records (ADRs) — why a design choice was made, what alternatives were rejected, what residual risk was accepted
- Target state architecture and migration roadmap

**Threat modelling in practice.** Four steps: (1) decompose the system — produce a data flow diagram identifying components, data stores, processes, external entities, data flows, and trust boundary crossings; (2) identify threats — apply STRIDE to each element and each boundary crossing; (3) rate threats — assess likelihood and impact; (4) specify mitigations — define design-time controls addressing each identified threat. Outputs feed directly into architecture decisions and security requirements passed to development or procurement teams.

**Zero Trust as a programme, not a product.** A typical ZTA implementation programme covers: identity consolidation (strong MFA, federated identity, consistent directory services); device posture assessment; privileged access management (just-in-time access, no standing privilege); microsegmentation (workload-level boundaries); and continuous monitoring feeding the Policy Engine. In Australian government, this must also align to the PSPF's Zero Trust Culture requirements and the ISM's access control and MFA controls.

**Architecture governance.** Governance determines how design decisions are made, documented, challenged, and enforced. A mature approach includes an Architecture Review Board reviewing significant changes, a documented exceptions process, regular reviews of the target state architecture against what is actually deployed, and defined domain owners. In Australian government, the authorising officer (AO) is the senior officer who accepts the residual security risks of operating a system — they depend on architecture documentation to make an informed risk decision.

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Security as an afterthought.** The architecture team is engaged after a procurement decision has been made or a system is deployed. Controls are then mapped to an existing structure not designed to support them. In government this creates IRAP findings that cannot be remediated without redesign, often delaying ATOs.

**Flat networks with implicit internal trust.** The dominant failure mode across organisations of all sizes. Meaningful microsegmentation adoption remains low, despite the problem being well understood for over a decade.

**Architecture that exists only on paper.** Documented architectures that diverge significantly from what is actually deployed. In Australian government, this creates direct PSPF compliance risk — the Technology Asset Stocktake requirement means that undocumented assets and ungoverned deviations from the architecture are now a reportable gap.

**Treating a framework as an architecture.** Adopting ISO 27001, CIS Controls, Essential Eight, or NIST CSF 2.0 as an architecture methodology. These frameworks specify what outcomes or controls should exist; they do not tell you how to design a system structurally. Under the SOCI CIRMP, entities must adopt one of these frameworks — but adopting a framework and having a coherent security architecture are different things.

**Over-scoping ZTA as a product.** A single ZTNA gateway or an identity provider does not constitute a Zero Trust architecture. Under PSPF 2025, this distinction matters — the Zero Trust Culture requirement covers governance, identity verification, access decisions, and continuous improvement, not just technical controls.

**Ignoring the human and process layers.** Technically correct architecture that is operationally unworkable. A privileged access workflow with too many steps will be bypassed. A segmentation design that breaks undocumented application dependencies will be rolled back under business pressure.

**Scope limited to IT.** Failing to include OT, IoT, physical access systems, or cloud-native workloads in the architectural scope. For SOCI-regulated entities, the CIRMP requires an all-hazards approach — cyber and information security hazards apply across all technology environments the entity operates, not just the corporate IT network.

**Skipping threat modelling.** Without structured threat modelling during design, security requirements are guessed rather than derived from analysis. For IRAP assessments, an absence of threat modelling artefacts is a gap against ISM risk management controls.

**No architecture decision records.** Design decisions made without documentation of rationale create institutional amnesia. For IRAP and PSPF annual reporting purposes, the inability to demonstrate why a design choice was made — and what risk was accepted — is an assurance deficiency.

**Architecture drift.** No ongoing process for maintaining architecture as the environment evolves. Under PSPF 2025's Technology Asset Stocktake requirement, architecture drift is no longer just a quality concern — it is a mandatory compliance gap.

## Connections to Other Domains

- **[Cyber Governance and Management (1)](01.%20Cyber%20Governance%20and%20Management.md):** Governance must precede architecture. The PSPF requires that security governance — including the role of the Chief Security Officer and Chief Information Security Officer — is in place before architectural decisions are formalised. The authorising officer who grants the ATO is a governance role that depends entirely on architecture documentation.

- **[Cyber Risk Management (2)](02.%20Cyber%20Risk%20Management.md):** Architecture decisions are risk decisions. Threat modelling, attack surface assessment, control placement, and trust zone definition are risk management activities at design time. The SOCI CIRMP and PSPF risk management domain both require that risks are formally assessed and documented — architecture is the mechanism for doing so structurally.

- **[Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance (3)](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md):** Architecture must enable compliance with the PSPF, SOCI Act, Privacy Act 1988, and sector-specific regulation. Data residency, classification handling, audit logging, and access control requirements set by legislation are architectural inputs that must be specified before design begins.

- **[Privacy and Data Protection (4)](04.%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Protection.md):** The Privacy Act 1988 and the Australian Privacy Principles apply to many entities alongside the PSPF. Data classification drives architectural decisions about storage, processing, and transmission. Privacy by design is an architectural responsibility — the data flow diagrams produced for threat modelling are the same artefacts used to demonstrate privacy compliance.

- **[Vendor Management (5)](05.%20Vendor%20Management.md):** Every third-party connection is a trust boundary. Under SOCI CIRMP Rules, supply chain hazards must be formally managed. Architecture must specify how third-party access is isolated and controlled. PSPF requires contractual arrangements with service providers to address handling requirements before classified information is shared.

- **[Asset and Change Management (6)](06.%20Asset%20and%20Change%20Management.md):** The PSPF Technology Asset Stocktake requires accurate asset visibility. Architecture defines what assets exist; asset management must keep this picture current. Change management must route significant changes through architectural review — and in government, through the authorising officer where the change affects the system's security risk profile.

- **[Network and Communication Security (7)](07.%20Network%20and%20Communication%20Security.md):** Gateway security is a specific PSPF obligation. Network segmentation, traffic inspection, and boundary controls are direct outputs of network security architecture. The PSPF Gateway Security Standard and ASD Gateway Guidance specify architecture requirements for government gateways.

- **[Identity and Access Management (8)](08.%20Identity%20and%20Access%20Management.md):** Identity is the primary control plane in modern security architecture. PSPF 2025 Zero Trust Culture requirements, ISM MFA controls, and Essential Eight restriction of administrative privileges all drive specific identity architecture requirements. Privileged access management and federated identity design are joint architecture and IAM concerns.

- **[Cryptography (9)](09.%20Cryptography.md):** ISM-approved cryptographic algorithms must be used in government systems. Architecture must specify which ASD-Approved Cryptographic Algorithms are used, key management approach, and certificate lifecycle management. Post-quantum cryptography planning is now a PSPF technology domain requirement, making cryptographic agility an explicit architectural concern.

- **[Cyber Testing (10)](10.%20Cyber%20Testing.md):** Architecture must be validated through structured threat modelling, architecture review, and adversarial testing. IRAP assessment is the formal mechanism for government systems. For SoNS under SOCI, vulnerability assessments and cyber exercises are mandatory enhanced cyber security obligations.

- **[Threat Intelligence (11)](11.%20Threat%20Intelligence.md):** ASD's ACSC provides threat intelligence to government and critical infrastructure through the Cyber Security Partnership Program and Cyber Threat Intelligence Sharing Platform (both introduced in PSPF 2025). Architecture must be designed to consume and act on threat intelligence — including the ability to modify access decisions and update policy based on ASD advisories.

- **[Security Operations and Monitoring (12)](12.%20Security%20Operations%20and%20Monitoring.md):** The architecture must ensure every zone produces observable telemetry. ISM Govern and Detect principles require logging and monitoring. SOCI incident reporting timelines (12 and 72 hours) require that the architecture supports rapid detection and scope determination — which is only possible if monitoring coverage is designed in, not added after.

- **[Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning (13)](13.%20Incident%20Management%20and%20Business%20Continuity%20Planning.md):** PSPF 2024 introduced explicit operational resilience requirements including business continuity planning and incident notification obligations. SOCI mandatory incident reporting requires rapid notification to the ACSC. Architecture provides the isolation, containment, and recovery capabilities that enable these obligations to be met.

- **[Cloud Management (14)](14.%20Cloud%20Management.md):** For government systems, cloud services must be IRAP-assessed or assessed by the entity against the ISM. The PSPF Hosting Certification Framework (HCF) specifies which facilities and services are certified for hosting government data at different classification levels. Cloud architecture in Australian government must account for data sovereignty, classification-appropriate hosting, and IRAP assessment requirements.

- **[Software Development Security (15)](15.%20Software%20Development%20Security.md):** Security architects specify application security requirements that development teams must implement. For systems subject to ISM, secure development requirements include threat modelling, code review, and security testing before systems are assessed by IRAP.

- **[DevOps and DevSecOps (16)](16.%20DevOps%20and%20DevSecOps.md):** Architecture must be expressible as code and enforceable through automated pipelines. Infrastructure-as-code and policy-as-code reduce drift between documented design and deployed reality — which matters directly for PSPF Technology Asset Stocktake compliance.

- **[Cyber Culture and Education (18)](18.%20Cyber%20Culture%20and%20Education.md):** PSPF 2025's Zero Trust Culture requirement is explicitly cultural as well as technical. Architecture decisions must be communicated and understood by the people who build and maintain systems. Undocumented architectural intent cannot be maintained.

- **[LLM Security (19)](19.%20LLM%20Security.md):** PSPF 2025 introduced new sections on AI security. Security architects in government must address LLM and AI deployments as architectural concerns — data flows to model providers, handling of OFFICIAL and PROTECTED information through AI systems, and supply chain risks from third-party AI services are not yet covered by existing reference architectures and require explicit treatment.

- **[Physical Security (20)](20.%20Physical%20Security.md):** The PSPF Physical domain requires security zone design for facilities handling classified information. The classification of a system determines the physical security requirements of the facility in which it is hosted. Physical access controls, SCEC-approved containers and rooms, and cable management are part of the physical architecture that security architecture must address. Physical trust boundaries must be consistent with logical trust boundaries.
