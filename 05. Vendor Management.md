# Vendor Management

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Vendor management — often called Third-Party Risk Management (TPRM) or Supply Chain Risk Management (SCRM) — is the discipline of governing the security, performance, and compliance of external parties who deliver products or services to your organisation. It covers the full relationship lifecycle: selection and due diligence before contracting, controls and obligations embedded in agreements, ongoing monitoring during the relationship, and controlled exit when it ends.

Modern organisations rely heavily on third parties — cloud providers, managed security service providers, software vendors, contractors, subprocessors — meaning a significant portion of an organisation's risk exposure sits outside its direct control. Vendor management is where that exposure is identified and governed.

It sits at the intersection of risk management, legal and contractual governance, asset management, and security operations. In most frameworks it is treated as a distinct risk domain rather than an IT procurement function.

---

## Why It Matters

Outsourcing transfers operational tasks, but it does not transfer accountability. The enterprise remains accountable for information it stores with a third-party host, regardless of where that information resides.

When vendor management fails, the consequences cascade:

**SolarWinds (2020):** Attackers compromised SolarWinds' software build environment and inserted the SUNBURST backdoor into legitimate Orion updates distributed between March and June 2020. Approximately 18,000 customers installed the compromised software. Of those, roughly 100 high-value targets — including U.S. federal agencies and Fortune 500 companies — were actively exploited. The attack operated undetected for approximately 9 to 14 months. Most victims had no contractual or monitoring mechanisms to detect a compromise originating from a trusted software vendor's build pipeline.

**Target (2013):** Attackers gained entry to Target's network via a compromised HVAC contractor with access to Target's vendor portal. The contractor's credentials were used to pivot into payment systems, resulting in the theft of approximately 40 million payment card records.

**Change Healthcare (February 2024):** A ransomware attack on Change Healthcare — which processes an estimated one-third of all U.S. medical transactions — caused widespread disruption to medical billing, insurance eligibility verification, and claims processing across the healthcare sector. Many practices could not submit claims or access patient records for weeks. The incident illustrated concentration risk: the healthcare industry had consolidated around a single clearinghouse to the point where its failure became a sector-wide crisis, regardless of any individual organisation's own security posture.

**CrowdStrike (July 2024):** A faulty content configuration update to the Falcon sensor caused approximately 8.5 million Windows devices to crash globally. This was not a cyberattack — it was an operational failure in a vendor's release process. Delta Air Lines alone estimated losses of around $500 million from over 7,000 cancelled flights. The incident demonstrated that vendor-caused disruption does not require malicious intent, and that contractual requirements around change management, staged rollouts, and liability need to be explicit.

SolarWinds, Target, Change Healthcare, and CrowdStrike share a common thread: the affected organisations had insufficient visibility into their vendors' operational and security practices, and insufficient contractual leverage to enforce requirements or recover costs when things went wrong. The SolarWinds attack cost affected organisations an estimated 11% of annual revenue on average in the U.S. By 2024, 30% of all data breaches involved a third-party vendor, with supply chain compromise ranking as the second-most-expensive breach vector globally at an average of USD 4.91 million per incident.

## Core Concepts

**Risk is not transferred by contract.** A contract can shift financial liability, but it cannot transfer the reputational damage, regulatory exposure, or operational impact of a vendor-caused incident. Organisations that discover this mid-incident are often surprised; practitioners should establish this expectation with leadership before a contract is signed.

**The supply chain has multiple tiers.** Your direct (Tier 1) vendors have their own vendors (Tier 2, Tier 3 and beyond). A compromise anywhere in this chain can propagate to you. Most TPRM programmes have reasonable Tier 1 visibility and almost no Tier 2 or Tier 3 visibility. This is a known and persistent gap.

**Vendor risk is inherently dynamic.** A vendor that passes an assessment today may be acquired, experience a financial downturn, suffer a breach, or lose key security personnel tomorrow. Point-in-time assessments — annual questionnaires, one-off audits — are inadequate for critical vendors. The SolarWinds attackers operated for over a year between periodic review cycles.

**Criticality drives treatment.** Not every vendor warrants the same scrutiny. A vendor with access to production systems and personal data is materially different from a catering supplier. Risk-tiering vendors by their data access, system access, service criticality, and concentration risk allows proportionate controls and avoids programme fatigue.

**Concentration risk is a distinct and often underweighted factor.** Concentration risk arises when a critical function depends on a single vendor with no viable alternative, or when an entire industry relies on the same small set of providers. The Change Healthcare incident showed that concentration risk can be sector-wide, not just organisational. The CrowdStrike outage showed that even a non-malicious vendor failure can cascade across thousands of organisations simultaneously. A vendor may appear low-risk in isolation but create unacceptable exposure when viewed across the organisation's full dependency map. This needs to be assessed explicitly and reported to leadership — it is not just a procurement decision.

**Contractual controls are the primary lever.** Once a vendor is engaged, the contract is the most powerful tool you have. Provisions covering right-to-audit, security requirements, incident notification timeframes, data handling, subprocessor restrictions, and exit/transition obligations must be negotiated before signing — not after an incident.

**Vendor lifecycle:**

```
  Pre-engagement          Active engagement          Termination
       |                        |                        |
  Due diligence         Ongoing monitoring          Controlled exit
  Risk assessment       SLA/KPI management          Data return/deletion
  Contract negotiation  Periodic reassessment       Access revocation
  Security requirements Incident notification        Escrow/continuity
  |                        |                        |
  Onboarding              Review gates             Offboarding
```

## How It Works in Practice

**Programme structure**

A functional TPRM programme has four components working together: an inventory, a risk-tiering methodology, a lifecycle process, and ongoing monitoring. Without all four, the programme has gaps that will eventually matter.

**Roles**

The vendor/procurement team manages commercial relationships. Security owns the risk assessment methodology, security requirements, and escalation of findings. Legal owns contract language. The business unit that relies on the vendor is the risk owner and must sign off on accepted residual risk. Internal audit or assurance reviews programme effectiveness.

**Vendor inventory**

You cannot manage what you have not catalogued. The inventory should record: vendor name, service description, data types processed, system access level, criticality tier, contract expiry, last assessment date, and risk rating. This feeds into asset management and is a prerequisite for meaningful monitoring.

**Risk tiering (example)**

```
  Tier 1 – Critical
    Access to production systems or sensitive data
    Single-source dependency
    Regulatory visibility (e.g., APRA material outsourcing)
    Controls: Full security assessment, contractual audit rights,
              continuous monitoring, annual reassessment

  Tier 2 – High
    Access to internal systems, non-production environments
    Significant data processing
    Controls: Security questionnaire, contractual requirements,
              periodic monitoring, biennial reassessment

  Tier 3 – Standard
    No system access, limited or no data handling
    Controls: Self-attestation, standard contract terms,
              reassessment on contract renewal
```

**Due diligence**

Before engaging a new vendor, due diligence should include: a completed security questionnaire (or review of certifications such as ISO 27001, SOC 2 Type II), review of the vendor's incident history, assessment of financial stability for critical vendors, and confirmation that the vendor's subprocessors are identified and acceptable.

**Contractual provisions**

Material security provisions in vendor contracts:

- Security requirements (aligned to your policy baseline)
- Right to audit or inspect (or right to require third-party attestation)
- Incident notification obligations (timeframe must be defined — 24 or 48 hours is common)
- Data handling, retention, and deletion requirements
- Subprocessor/subcontractor restrictions
- Exit and transition obligations (data return, access revocation, documentation)
- Liability and indemnity clauses
- Business continuity requirements

**Contractual documents in a typical vendor engagement:**

```
  MSA (Master Service Agreement)
   |-- SLA (Service Level Agreement) -- defines performance standards
   |-- SOW (Statement of Work) -- defines scope and deliverables
   |-- NDA -- covers confidentiality
   |-- DPA (Data Processing Agreement) -- required where personal data is involved
        |-- Subprocessor schedule
```

**Ongoing monitoring**

Point-in-time assessments are necessary but not sufficient. Ongoing monitoring for critical vendors should include: continuous external attack surface monitoring, review of the vendor's published security advisories and CVE disclosures, tracking of public breach notifications, SLA/KPI performance reviews, and periodic reassessments triggered by material changes (acquisition, significant personnel changes, new services).

**Exit management**

Exit planning should start at contract initiation, not when the relationship ends. Provisions should ensure: return or certified destruction of your data, revocation of all access, transfer of documentation and credentials, and a defined transition period where the vendor supports continuity.

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Treating vendor questionnaires as assurance.** A questionnaire is a starting point for conversation, not evidence of control effectiveness. Vendors frequently self-attest positively on controls they do not consistently implement. For critical vendors, questionnaire responses should be validated against certifications, audit reports, or direct testing.

**One-size-fits-all assessments.** Applying a 200-question security questionnaire to every vendor regardless of criticality creates noise, delays, and vendor fatigue without improving security outcomes. Tiering is not optional.

**No sub-tier visibility.** Most organisations have some Tier 1 coverage and almost none beyond that. The SolarWinds attack exploited this gap. Contractual requirements to identify and notify of material Tier 2 subprocessors or technology dependencies are a minimum starting point.

**Weak contract provisions negotiated under time pressure.** Procurement timelines often compress security review and contract negotiation. Right-to-audit clauses are removed, notification timeframes are left undefined, and exit provisions are omitted. These gaps only surface when they are needed most.

**No process for continuous monitoring.** Annual assessments miss incidents that develop over months. External attack surface monitoring tools and breach intelligence feeds provide between-cycle signal for critical vendors.

**Failure to manage concentration risk.** Organisations that depend on a single vendor for a critical service have a different risk profile from those with alternatives. Concentration risk — relying on one cloud provider, one payment processor, one managed SOC — is a business risk that vendor management programmes should be surfacing to leadership.

**No defined exit process.** Data left with a departed vendor, access credentials not revoked, and undocumented dependencies discovered after termination are all common. Exit provisions must be negotiated upfront.

**Treating TPRM as a procurement function.** When security does not own or co-own the assessment methodology and sign-off process, vendor risk assessments become checkbox exercises. Security must have meaningful input — and veto rights for critical vendors — in the procurement decision.

## Connections to Other Domains

- **[Cyber Governance and Management (1)](01.%20Cyber%20Governance%20and%20Management.md):** Board and executive accountability for third-party risk is a governance matter. Material outsourcing decisions and concentration risk should be reported at the governance level.

- **[Cyber Risk Management (2)](02.%20Cyber%20Risk%20Management.md):** Vendor risk is a specific category of enterprise risk. Risk tiering, risk appetite for third-party exposure, and residual risk sign-off are risk management functions. Outsourcing transfers tasks but not risk ownership.

- **[Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance (3)](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md):** APRA CPS 230, GDPR, the Australian Privacy Act, and sector-specific regulations impose specific obligations on vendor management — due diligence, contract requirements, and notification duties. Compliance obligations must flow down into vendor contracts.

- **[Privacy and Data Protection (4)](04.%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Protection.md):** Vendors who process personal information are data processors. Data Processing Agreements (DPAs), subprocessor restrictions, and cross-border transfer controls are vendor management responsibilities.

- **[Asset and Change Management (6)](06.%20Asset%20and%20Change%20Management.md):** The vendor inventory is a subset of the broader asset register. Changes to vendor services, system access, or subprocessors must flow through change management.

- **[Identity and Access Management (8)](08.%20Identity%20and%20Access%20Management.md):** Third-party access provisioning — contractor accounts, service accounts, API keys — must follow least-privilege principles, be tied to a specific vendor and duration, and be revoked as part of exit management.

- **[Cyber Testing (10)](10.%20Cyber%20Testing.md):** Right-to-audit and penetration testing provisions in vendor contracts depend on what the organisation can contractually require. For cloud and SaaS vendors, shared responsibility models and customer-facing audit reports (SOC 2, ISO 27001) substitute for direct testing.

- **[Threat Intelligence (11)](11.%20Threat%20Intelligence.md):** External attack surface monitoring, breach intelligence, and dark web monitoring for vendor credentials are operational inputs into the vendor monitoring programme.

- **[Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning (13)](13.%20Incident%20Management%20and%20Business%20Continuity%20Planning.md):** Vendor incident notification clauses, vendor-caused incidents that trigger your own IR plan, and single-vendor dependencies that affect your BCP/DR posture all connect here. Vendor exit must also be incorporated into continuity planning.

- **[Cloud Management (14)](14.%20Cloud%20Management.md):** Cloud providers are vendors. The shared responsibility model defines what the provider controls and what the customer controls. Cloud vendor management requires understanding these boundaries and ensuring customer-side controls are implemented.

- **[Software Development Security (15)](15.%20Software%20Development%20Security.md):** Software bill of materials (SBOM), secure development requirements in vendor contracts, and supply chain integrity for open-source dependencies all connect vendor management to software security.

- **[Cyber Architecture (17)](17.%20Cyber%20Architecture.md):** Architecture decisions around third-party integrations — API design, network segmentation for vendor access, zero-trust principles for contractor access — are informed by the vendor risk landscape.

## Australian Compliance Context

_Full regulatory detail is in [note 03](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md). Volatile figures are in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md)._


Australian organisations face a layered and sector-specific set of obligations on vendor management. Understanding which framework applies — and what it actually requires — is essential for practitioners operating in this jurisdiction.

**APRA CPS 230 (effective 1 July 2025)**

CPS 230 is now the primary vendor management obligation for APRA-regulated entities (banks, insurers, superannuation funds). It replaces both CPS 231 (Outsourcing) and CPS 232 (Business Continuity Management). The key vendor-specific requirements are:

- Maintain a register of all material service providers and submit it to APRA (register due to APRA by 1 October 2025 for ADIs, insurers, and superannuation trustees)
- Establish a documented service provider management policy
- Ensure contracts with material service providers contain appropriate safeguards, particularly for services supporting critical operations
- Define disruption tolerance thresholds for critical operations and test them
- For the first time, formally document and manage fourth-party risk — the sub-outsourcing dependencies of your material service providers (e.g. a material vendor's reliance on a specific cloud provider)
- Pre-existing contracts must comply by the earlier of the next contract renewal or 1 July 2026

The shift from CPS 231 to CPS 230 is significant. CPS 231 framed outsourcing as a compliance matter. CPS 230 frames it as an operational resilience matter. The question is no longer just "do we have a contract?" but "can we continue critical operations if this vendor fails?"

**PSPF (Australian Government entities and their vendors)**

The Protective Security Policy Framework applies to Commonwealth entities and flows down to contractors and service providers handling government information or assets. PSPF Release 2024 was the first to explicitly address third-party risk and supply chain security as mandatory requirements. PSPF 2025 formalised a dedicated Risk domain.

For vendors engaging with government:

- Vendors handling OFFICIAL: Sensitive or above must undergo an IRAP (Infosec Registered Assessors Program) assessment, renewed every 24 months
- PSPF Direction 001-2024 requires government entities to assess FOCI risk at procurement — evaluating whether a vendor is subject to foreign ownership, control, or influence that could compromise government data or systems. Indicators include substantial foreign ownership, foreign executive roles, dependency on foreign suppliers, obligations to foreign laws, and foreign access to sensitive data
- Government entities must have formal agreements in place before disclosing classified information or resources to any third party
- The Accountable Authority (typically the agency head) is responsible for residual risk sign-off where vendor exceptions exceed the entity's risk appetite
- Vendors that change ownership trigger a re-assessment obligation

**SOCI Act 2018 (critical infrastructure operators and their supply chains)**

The SOCI Act covers responsible entities in 11 critical infrastructure sectors: energy, water, transport, communications, health, finance, education, food, space, defence industry, and data storage. The 2024 amendments extended scope to secondary data storage systems holding business-critical data of primary CI assets, meaning cloud and data centre providers serving CI operators face increased obligations.

For vendor management specifically, SOCI requires:

- The CIRMP (Critical Infrastructure Risk Management Program) must address supply chain hazards explicitly alongside cyber, personnel, and physical hazards. The CIRMP must be reviewed annually and reported to the Department of Home Affairs
- Responsible entities must identify major suppliers and evaluate risks that could affect their critical infrastructure assets
- Personnel with access to critical components — including contractors and subcontractors — must be assessed for suitability; background screening (aligned to AS 4811) is recommended by the Cyber and Infrastructure Security Centre (CISC) though not strictly mandated
- Where business-critical data is processed or stored by a third party on a commercial basis, the responsible entity must take reasonable steps to inform the third party that they are doing so
- Managed service providers serving CI operators also face obligations under the Act and should not assume SOCI does not apply to them
- Significant cyber security incidents must be reported to ASD within 12 hours; other notifiable incidents within 72 hours

```
  Australian Compliance Applicability

  Organisation type                   Primary obligation(s)
  ---------------------------------------------------------------
  APRA-regulated entity               CPS 230 (mandatory, eff. 1 Jul 2025)
  Commonwealth Government entity      PSPF (mandatory, inc. FOCI, IRAP)
  CI responsible entity               SOCI CIRMP (mandatory, annual report)
  Vendor to government                PSPF flows down via contract
  MSP serving CI operator             SOCI obligations apply
  Any org handling personal data      Privacy Act 1988 / APPs
  All of the above                    Australian Privacy Act 1988
```

Practitioners should map their organisation against this table and identify which obligations are active, not simply which are aspirational.
