# Cyber Testing

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Cyber testing is the deliberate, structured examination of an organisation's people, processes, and technology to find security weaknesses before adversaries do. It sits at the intersection of assurance and offence — borrowing the mindset of an attacker to inform the decisions of a defender. Testing is not a single activity. It spans a spectrum from automated vulnerability scanning through manual penetration testing to full-scale, intelligence-led red team exercises that simulate advanced persistent threats against live production systems. Where risk management tells you what might go wrong in theory, cyber testing tells you what actually goes wrong in practice.

It feeds directly into the risk register, the security programme, and board-level assurance. Without it, controls are assumed to work rather than known to work.

---

## Why It Matters

When testing is absent or poorly executed, organisations discover their weaknesses through breaches rather than exercises. The consequences are consistent and well-documented:

**Undetected misconfigurations persist.** The 2021 Colonial Pipeline attack originated from an unmonitored legacy VPN credential. The exposure was discoverable — it just was not discovered first by defenders.

**Assumed controls fail silently.** Organisations implement firewalls, EDR tools, and access controls but rarely verify that they behave as expected under realistic attack conditions. A control that was never tested is a control that is trusted without evidence.

**Compliance masquerades as security.** Passing an audit or ticking a PCI DSS checkbox does not mean a system is actually secure. Testing at depth reveals the gap between compliance posture and real-world resilience.

**Third-party exposure goes unverified.** Supply chain attacks — such as the SolarWinds compromise — succeed partly because organisations test their own environments but not their dependencies. Testing scope that stops at the organisation's boundary leaves a meaningful attack surface unexamined.

**Detection capability is never validated.** Organisations build SOCs and deploy SIEM tools but rarely verify whether those tools would actually alert on a real intrusion. Red team exercises answer this directly.

## Core Concepts

**The testing spectrum is not a hierarchy.** Vulnerability scanning, penetration testing, and red team exercises are not progressively better versions of the same thing — they answer different questions. Scanning identifies known weaknesses at scale. Penetration testing determines whether weaknesses can be exploited and to what consequence. Red teaming tests whether the organisation can detect and respond to a realistic, multi-stage attack. Breach and attack simulation answers whether controls are working continuously, between engagements. Each has a legitimate place and none replaces the others.

**Knowledge state shapes the test.** The terms black box, grey box, and white box describe how much information the tester is given before starting:

```
Black box  -->  No prior knowledge (simulates an external attacker)
Grey box   -->  Partial knowledge (simulates a compromised user or insider)
White box  -->  Full access to architecture, source code, credentials
```

Black box tests are more realistic simulations; white box tests find more vulnerabilities per hour. Neither is universally superior — the right choice depends on the objective.

**Rules of engagement define the boundary.** Every test must have a written, signed scope agreement that specifies what systems are in scope, what actions are permitted, who is notified if something breaks, and what the emergency stop procedure is. Testing without authorisation is a criminal act in most jurisdictions, regardless of intent.

**Findings are not the output — decisions are.** A penetration test report that sits unread is worthless. The value of testing is in what the organisation changes as a result. This requires findings to be risk-rated, triaged, assigned to owners, and tracked to remediation.

**Threat-led testing anchors exercise to reality.** Intelligence-led red team frameworks (TIBER-EU, CBEST) use actual threat intelligence about actors who target the organisation's sector to design attack scenarios. This is more valuable than generic exploitation because it tests against plausible, not theoretical, threats.

**Purple teaming improves both sides.** Purple team exercises involve red and blue teams working together — the red team explains what they did and why, the blue team explains what they saw (or missed). The goal is knowledge transfer and detection improvement, not competitive scoring.

**Social engineering and phishing simulation test the human layer.** Simulated phishing campaigns, vishing (voice phishing), and physical pretexting exercises test whether employees recognise and report attacks. These are distinct test disciplines requiring their own scoping and authorisation — and they frequently reveal gaps that no technical test will find. An organisation with hardened infrastructure that falls to a well-crafted phishing email has an incomplete testing programme. Results feed directly into security awareness training effectiveness measurement.

**Breach and Attack Simulation (BAS) and Adversarial Exposure Validation (AEV)** are automated platforms that continuously emulate adversary techniques — mapped to MITRE ATT&CK — against production controls (firewalls, EDR, email gateways, SIEM) to validate whether prevention and detection work as expected. Gartner's 2024 Hype Cycle for Security Operations consolidated BAS and automated penetration testing into the Adversarial Exposure Validation (AEV) category. These tools answer a different question from a penetration test: not "what can be exploited?" but "are my controls actually stopping and detecting known attack techniques right now?" They complement manual testing — they do not replace it.

**Continuous Threat Exposure Management (CTEM)** is a Gartner-defined programme framework (introduced 2022) for continuously and consistently evaluating the accessibility and exploitability of an organisation's assets. It operates through five phases: scoping, discovery, prioritisation, validation, and mobilisation. Testing activities — vulnerability scanning, penetration testing, BAS/AEV, red team — sit primarily in the validation phase, providing evidence of exploitability rather than theoretical risk scores. CTEM represents the shift from periodic point-in-time testing to continuous exposure management as an organisational capability.

**Vulnerability prioritisation requires more than a CVSS score.** CVSSv4.0 (released November 2023 by FIRST) measures severity — not likelihood of exploitation. Using CVSS alone to drive remediation queues is inefficient: the majority of critical-rated CVEs are never exploited in the wild.

EPSS (Exploit Prediction Scoring System), managed by FIRST, estimates the daily probability that a given CVE will be exploited within the next 30 days. EPSS v4 was released March 2025. Research shows fewer than 2% of CVEs have a high EPSS score, meaning a CVSS-only queue wastes significant remediation effort. The CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalogue lists CVEs confirmed as actively exploited — anything in KEV is highest priority regardless of CVSS score.

```
Practical triage logic:

In CISA KEV                         -->  Emergency: patch within 24-72 hours
CVSS high + EPSS high               -->  High: fix within SLA
CVSS high + EPSS low                -->  Planned: schedule or risk-accept with justification
CVSS medium + EPSS high             -->  Investigate: may be chained into something worse
CVSS low + EPSS low                 -->  Monitor: no immediate action
```

**OT and ICS environments require a fundamentally different approach.** Active exploitation of operational technology systems can cause physical consequences — safety incidents, equipment damage, or process disruption. Passive techniques (network traffic analysis, architecture review, configuration inspection) are typically used in preference to active exploitation. Any active OT testing requires explicit engineering sign-off, not just security authorisation, and must be coordinated with operational staff who can respond if a system behaves unexpectedly. Specialist OT security knowledge is non-negotiable; applying IT penetration testing methodology directly to OT is dangerous.

**PTaaS (Penetration Testing as a Service)** is an emerging delivery model where penetration testing is provided on a continuous or on-demand basis through a platform, rather than as a periodic point-in-time engagement. PTaaS reduces the gap between tests as environments change and can provide ongoing coverage across a development pipeline. It does not replicate the depth of a full manual engagement for complex targets, but it is a meaningful improvement over annual-only testing for organisations with fast-changing environments.

## How It Works in Practice

**Programme governance comes first.** Testing should be governed by a written programme that defines frequency, scope categories, testing types, ownership, and how findings are tracked. Ad hoc testing is better than nothing but produces inconsistent coverage and uneven remediation.

**Typical annual testing cycle for a mid-size organisation:**

```
Q1  Internal vulnerability scan (all in-scope systems)
    External penetration test (internet-facing perimeter)

Q2  Web application penetration test (priority applications)
    Phishing simulation (all staff or targeted groups)
    Review and remediation tracking from Q1

Q3  Internal penetration test (assume breach scenario)
    Cloud configuration review
    OT/ICS architecture and configuration review (if applicable)

Q4  Red team exercise (threat-led, if capability and budget allow)
    Annual programme review and scope update

Continuous  BAS / AEV platform running against key controls
            Vulnerability scanner on new assets as deployed
            PTaaS coverage for fast-changing environments
```

**Roles involved:**

- _Security team / CISO_ — commissions and governs the programme; tracks remediation
- _Internal red team_ — conducts ongoing testing, simulated phishing, assumed breach exercises
- _External penetration testers_ — engaged for independence; CREST-accredited or equivalent
- _System / application owners_ — authorise scope, receive findings, own remediation
- _Risk team_ — receives test outputs for risk register update
- _Audit_ — may independently commission or review testing as part of IS audit activity

**Vulnerability management is the operational engine.** Scanning tools (Tenable Nessus, Qualys, Rapid7 InsightVM) generate large volumes of findings. Without a defined triage process — criticality scoring, CVSS weighting adjusted for environment context, ownership assignment, and SLA — findings accumulate without driving action. CVSS alone is a poor prioritisation tool; a CVSS 9.8 vulnerability on a non-internet-facing, isolated system may be lower priority than a CVSS 6.5 vulnerability on a publicly accessible authentication endpoint.

**Penetration test lifecycle:**

```
Scoping and rules of engagement
        |
Intelligence gathering (passive OSINT, active recon)
        |
Vulnerability identification
        |
Exploitation (controlled, within agreed scope)
        |
Post-exploitation (lateral movement, privilege escalation)
        |
Evidence collection
        |
Report (executive summary + technical findings + risk ratings)
        |
Remediation validation (retest)
```

**Tooling:** Common tools include Nmap (host/port discovery), Burp Suite (web application testing), Metasploit (exploitation framework), Cobalt Strike or Brute Ratel (red team command-and-control), BloodHound (Active Directory attack path mapping), and Nuclei (template-based vulnerability scanning). Tool selection should be driven by scope — the tool list is not the methodology.

**Vulnerability prioritisation in practice.** Scanning tools produce large volumes of findings. Without a structured triage process, teams waste effort on findings that will never be exploited while genuinely dangerous exposures wait. A practical approach layers three data sources: CVSSv4.0 (severity ceiling), EPSS (exploitation probability), and CISA KEV (confirmed exploitation). Anything in CISA KEV gets patched immediately. High CVSS combined with high EPSS drives the next tier. High CVSS with low EPSS can be scheduled or risk-accepted with documented justification. Environmental context — is the system internet-facing, does it hold sensitive data, are there compensating controls — adjusts the final priority. This approach dramatically reduces the number of findings that require urgent action compared to a CVSS-only queue.

**Australian compliance obligations in practice.** The Australian regulatory landscape imposes specific testing obligations depending on an organisation's sector and relationship with government. Practitioners need to know which apply to their organisation and what the requirements actually demand:

_Commonwealth entities (PSPF / ISM):_ ISM control ISM-1163 requires a continuous monitoring plan that includes fortnightly vulnerability scans and annual penetration testing — including prior to deployment and prior to deployment of significant changes. The PSPF mandates Essential Eight at Maturity Level 2 for all Non-Corporate Commonwealth Entities. Essential Eight assessments must follow the ASD Essential Eight Assessment Process Guide and must be conducted by suitably skilled personnel independent of the system being assessed. IRAP assessors are required for cloud and outsourced systems — an entity's own assessors are not sufficient. Vendors supplying to government must also meet ISM requirements under their contracts.

_Critical infrastructure operators (SOCI Act / ERP Act 2024):_ Responsible entities must maintain a CIRMP that addresses cyber and information security hazards. The CIRMP must nominate and achieve a specified maturity level under one of five designated cyber security frameworks (Essential Eight, NIST CSF, ISO 27001, AESCSF, C2M2) or an equivalent framework. Systems of National Significance face additional Enhanced Cyber Security Obligations, which include mandatory vulnerability assessments and mandatory cyber security exercises testing the entity's preparedness to respond to and mitigate cyber incidents. These exercises must be reported to the Cyber and Infrastructure Security Centre (CISC), and in some circumstances require external audit. Mandatory cyber incident reporting applies: 12 hours for significant incidents, 72 hours for relevant incidents, reported to ASD/ACSC.

_APRA-regulated entities (CPS 234):_ A systematic testing programme is required that considers the rate of threat change, criticality and sensitivity of assets, and exposure to environments where security policies cannot be enforced. Controls must be tested at least annually and following significant changes. Penetration testing of critical and sensitive systems is expected. Testing must be conducted by an independent body, and results must be communicated to the board. Control weaknesses that cannot be remediated in a timely manner must be notified to APRA. Material information security incidents must be notified to APRA within 72 hours.

_All entities handling personal information (Privacy Act 1988):_ Penetration testing and vulnerability assessment are considered best-practice demonstrations of "reasonable steps" to protect personal information. After a breach, testing is recommended to identify and address underlying vulnerabilities. Notifiable Data Breach obligations apply where a breach is likely to result in serious harm to individuals.

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Treating an Essential Eight assessment as a penetration test.** An Essential Eight maturity assessment evaluates whether controls are implemented and operating as the ASD model intends — it is a control assessment, not an adversarial test. It will not discover novel attack paths, chained vulnerabilities, or whether an attacker could bypass your controls in practice. Both are needed; neither substitutes for the other.

**Assuming IRAP assessment is optional for cloud services.** Under PSPF Release 2024, cloud and outsourced services must use an IRAP Assessor — an entity's own assessors are explicitly insufficient for these systems. Organisations that use entity assessors for cloud deployments are non-compliant regardless of the quality of the assessment work.

**Treating the annual penetration test as a compliance event.** Organisations schedule a pentest to satisfy an audit requirement, file the report, and move on. If findings from the previous year recur, that is a systemic remediation failure — not a testing finding.

**Using CVSS score alone to prioritise remediation.** CVSS measures severity, not exploitation likelihood. A CVSS 9.8 vulnerability on an isolated internal system with no public exploit may be lower priority than a CVSS 6.5 vulnerability in a perimeter-facing service that appears in the CISA KEV catalogue. Prioritisation that ignores EPSS and KEV wastes remediation effort and leaves genuinely high-risk exposures unaddressed.

**Treating BAS as a substitute for penetration testing.** BAS platforms validate whether known techniques are blocked or detected — they do not discover novel attack paths, exploit logic flaws, or chain misconfigurations the way a skilled human tester does. Both serve distinct purposes and should coexist in a mature programme.

**Applying IT penetration testing methodology to OT environments.** Active exploitation of operational technology can cause physical harm, equipment damage, or process disruption. OT testing requires specialist knowledge, passive-first approaches, and explicit engineering approval alongside security authorisation. Treating OT targets the same as a corporate network is a safety risk, not just a security programme gap.

**Scope that protects the most sensitive systems.** Testers are often instructed to avoid production systems, critical databases, or recently deployed applications. These are frequently the highest-risk targets. Overly narrow scope produces a clean report that does not reflect real exposure.

**Testing isolated from remediation.** Testing generates a report; remediation requires a tracked process. Organisations that lack a formal vulnerability management workflow often cannot demonstrate whether findings from 12 months ago were fixed, partially fixed, or ignored.

**Conflating vulnerability scanning with penetration testing.** A scanner identifies known CVEs against a signature database. A penetration tester chains vulnerabilities together, exploits misconfigurations, abuses trust relationships, and demonstrates actual impact. They are complementary, not interchangeable.

**No retesting after remediation.** Developers close a finding, but the fix is never independently validated. Regressions, partial patches, and misapplied remediations are common. Retesting is the only way to confirm closure.

**Purple team exercises treated as red team report reviews.** The value of purple teaming is live, collaborative detection tuning — not a post-exercise briefing. Without defenders actively observing red team actions in real time and adjusting detection logic, the exercise produces conversation but not capability uplift.

**Social engineering and phishing simulation are excluded from scope.** Many testing programmes are scoped purely to technical infrastructure. Omitting the human layer means the programme does not measure one of the most consistently exploited attack vectors — and produces no data to drive awareness training improvement.

**Findings reported in technical language to non-technical stakeholders.** Executives need to understand the business consequence of a finding, not its CVE number. Reports that lead with CVSS scores rather than business impact fail to drive appropriate prioritisation and funding decisions.

## Connections to Other Domains

- **[Cyber Risk Management (2)](02.%20Cyber%20Risk%20Management.md):** Test findings are a primary input to the risk register. Penetration test results quantify the exploitability of identified risks and provide evidence for inherent and residual risk calculations. Risk appetite decisions drive testing frequency and depth.

- **[Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance (3)](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md):** PCI DSS, DORA, ISO 27001, and sector-specific regulations mandate specific testing activities globally. In Australia, PSPF, ISM, SOCI Act CIRMP obligations, APRA CPS 234, and the Privacy Act each impose specific and distinct testing requirements depending on sector. Rules of engagement must be legally sound — written authorisation is required in all cases. Testing that crosses into third-party systems without authorisation creates criminal and civil liability under the Criminal Code Act 1995 (Cth) and equivalent state legislation.

- **[Vendor Management (5)](05.%20Vendor%20Management.md):** Third-party and supply chain testing is an underexercised area. Vendor penetration test results, right-to-audit clauses, and third-party security assessments are the primary tools for managing this exposure.

- **[Asset and Change Management (6)](06.%20Asset%20and%20Change%20Management.md):** Testing scope depends on an accurate, up-to-date asset inventory. Changes to infrastructure (new systems, configuration changes, major deployments) should trigger targeted testing. Without change management integration, testing lags behind the actual attack surface.

- **[Network and Communication Security (7)](07.%20Network%20and%20Communication%20Security.md):** Network penetration tests directly assess segmentation, firewall rules, exposed services, and lateral movement paths. Findings inform network architecture decisions.

- **[Identity and Access Management (8)](08.%20Identity%20and%20Access%20Management.md):** Privilege escalation, credential theft, and lateral movement via Active Directory misconfigurations are among the most common penetration test findings. Testing validates whether IAM controls are effective in practice.

- **[Threat Intelligence (11)](11.%20Threat%20Intelligence.md):** Intelligence-led testing (TIBER-EU, CBEST, red team exercises) uses threat intelligence to design realistic attack scenarios. Without this, testing is generic rather than targeted to the organisation's actual threat landscape.

- **[Security Operations and Monitoring (12)](12.%20Security%20Operations%20and%20Monitoring.md):** Red team exercises validate detection and response capability. Purple team exercises directly improve SIEM rules, alert thresholds, and SOC playbooks. Testing without a detection validation component misses a critical question: would the organisation know if this happened for real?

- **[Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning (13)](13.%20Incident%20Management%20and%20Business%20Continuity%20Planning.md):** Tabletop exercises and simulation testing (referenced in the BCP/DR notes) overlap with cyber testing. Crisis simulation exercises test the human and process response layer that technical testing does not reach.

- **[Cloud Management (14)](14.%20Cloud%20Management.md):** Cloud environments require specific testing approaches — configuration review, IAM misconfiguration analysis, container security testing, and serverless function assessment. Standard network penetration testing methodologies do not cover cloud-native attack surfaces without adaptation.

- **[Software Development Security (15)](15.%20Software%20Development%20Security.md):** Manual application penetration testing finds logic flaws, business logic abuse, and chained vulnerabilities that automated tooling misses. Web and API penetration tests are the manual assurance layer that sits on top of automated SDLC security tools covered in the DevSecOps note.

- **[DevOps and DevSecOps (16)](16.%20DevOps%20and%20DevSecOps.md):** Testing integrated into CI/CD pipelines shifts security left. This does not replace periodic manual penetration testing — it reduces the volume of basic findings that reach it. BAS and AEV platforms can also be integrated into deployment pipelines to validate that new releases do not degrade control effectiveness.

- **[Cyber Governance and Management (1)](01.%20Cyber%20Governance%20and%20Management.md):** The testing programme requires board and executive sponsorship. Governance structures determine who commissions testing, who receives results, and whether findings translate into funded remediation.

- **[Physical Security (20)](20.%20Physical%20Security.md):** Social engineering and physical pretexting tests deliberately cross the boundary between digital and physical — testing whether an attacker can gain physical access to facilities, plant devices, or tailgate into secure areas. These exercises must be coordinated with physical security and facilities teams under the same rules-of-engagement discipline as technical tests.

- **[LLM Security (19)](19.%20LLM%20Security.md):** AI and LLM deployments introduce attack surfaces — prompt injection, data exfiltration through model outputs, insecure integrations — that require dedicated testing approaches. Standard penetration testing methodologies do not yet have mature, standardised coverage for LLM-specific attack vectors; this is an actively developing area that testing programmes should explicitly scope and resource.
