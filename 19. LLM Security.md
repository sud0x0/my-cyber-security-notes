# LLM Security

---

## Overview

_Volatile facts (framework versions, requirement numbers, reporting clocks) are maintained in [note 00](00.%20Current%20Versions%20and%20Facts.md). Check it before relying on figures here._

Large language model (LLM) security sits at the intersection of application security, data protection, AI governance, and supply chain risk. It covers the threats, vulnerabilities, and controls specific to systems built on or powered by generative AI models — whether those are public APIs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google Gemini), self-hosted open-source models, or fine-tuned proprietary variants.

LLMs are not traditional software. They do not follow deterministic logic. Their behaviour emerges from training data and probabilistic generation, which means attack surfaces include the model itself, the data it was trained on, the context it is given at runtime, and every system it is connected to. Shadow AI — employees using unsanctioned LLM tools for work — adds a further layer that sits entirely outside the typical application security programme.

In Australia, LLM security is now a compliance matter as well as a technical one. The PSPF 2025 release (24 July 2025) introduced explicit AI policy requirements. PSPF Policy Advisory 001-2025 (October 2025) defines mandatory conditions for using OFFICIAL information with generative AI. The ASD's Information Security Manual (ISM) has included LLM-specific controls since the June 2024 update. SOCI-regulated entities must account for AI systems in their Critical Infrastructure Risk Management Programmes (CIRMPs). The Cyber Security Act 2024 introduced mandatory ransomware payment reporting that applies to AI-assisted attacks as much as any other attack vector.

Internationally, the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (v2025), NIST AI RMF 1.0 with its Generative AI Profile (July 2024), ISO/IEC 42001:2023, and MITRE ATLAS are the primary technical frameworks practitioners anchor to.

## Why It Matters

LLM security failures are already producing real data loss, regulatory action, and operational harm, and most of them come from ordinary use of the technology rather than exotic attacks. The cases below show why the risk is immediate rather than theoretical.

**Data exfiltration through shadow AI** is the most common real-world failure. In April 2023, Samsung engineers uploaded proprietary semiconductor source code and confidential meeting notes to ChatGPT on three separate occasions within weeks of the company enabling access. That data entered OpenAI's training pipeline. Samsung banned all external AI tools shortly after, but the exposure was already done. Similar incidents were recorded at Amazon, Walmart, and JPMorgan Chase within the same period. A 2025 LayerX study found 77% of enterprise employees who use AI tools had pasted company data into a chatbot, with 22% of those instances involving confidential personal or financial information.

**Prompt injection** allows attackers to override an LLM's instructions. In August 2024, researchers demonstrated a prompt injection attack against Slack AI where a payload embedded in a public Slack channel caused the model to surface content from private channels. No credentials were stolen — the model was simply told to retrieve what it should not have.

**Legal and regulatory consequences are real — including in Australia.** Italy's Garante fined OpenAI EUR 15 million in December 2024 for processing European user data without adequate legal basis. In Australia, the PSPF's OFFICIAL: Sensitive classification ceiling for public generative AI tools means government employees who put the wrong data into a public chatbot breach mandatory policy. PSPF Direction 001-2025 (February 2025) prohibited Australian Government entities from accessing, using, or installing DeepSeek products entirely due to Foreign Ownership, Control, or Influence (FOCI) concerns — the first AI-specific direction under the PSPF, and a clear signal that the government treats AI tool selection as a national security question.

**SOCI-regulated entities** face the additional obligation that any AI system affecting critical infrastructure assets must be included in the CIRMP. An LLM agent with access to operational data, supervisory control systems, or business-critical data stores is not a peripheral productivity tool — it is an asset with mandatory security obligations attached. Data storage systems forming part of primary CI assets came into full CIRMP scope on 4 April 2025 under the Enhanced Response and Prevention Act 2024.

**Operational consequences** include AI-generated misinformation, fabricated legal citations (Mata v. Avianca, 2023), and customer-facing chatbots that can be manipulated into committing the business to unintended outcomes.

## Core Concepts

**The attack surface is unlike traditional applications.** Input (the prompt), output (the generated text), the model weights, the training data, the retrieval layer (RAG), tool integrations (agents), and the system prompt are all distinct attack vectors. Compromising any of them can compromise the application.

**Prompt injection** is the LLM equivalent of SQL injection, but harder to fully prevent because the model cannot reliably distinguish between legitimate instructions and malicious ones embedded in user input or retrieved documents. Direct injection comes from the user directly. Indirect injection arrives via content the model retrieves — a document, a web page, a database row — that contains hidden instructions. There is no complete mitigation; defence in depth is the only viable approach.

**Model memorisation** means LLMs can regurgitate fragments of their training data, including PII, credentials, and proprietary text. Research has shown that under adversarial prompting, even fine-tuned models with safety training revert to exposing memorised content. Safety alignment creates what researchers have called an "illusion of privacy" rather than a genuine guarantee.

**Data and model poisoning** occurs when malicious or manipulated content enters the training or fine-tuning pipeline, causing the model to behave differently — producing biased outputs, inserting backdoors, or undermining safety constraints.

**Excessive agency** is the risk that arises when an LLM is given too much authority — access to APIs, databases, email, code execution — without sufficient human oversight or least-privilege constraints. Agentic architectures amplify this risk substantially because the model can take multi-step autonomous actions, each of which may interact with sensitive systems.

**RAG and vector database risk** is an emerging concern. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems connect LLMs to organisational knowledge bases via embedding vectors. Poisoning the vector store, injecting malicious documents into the retrieval corpus, or gaining unauthorised access to the vector database can all manipulate what the model retrieves and therefore what it outputs.

**Supply chain risk** extends to model weights, training datasets, and third-party plugins. In the Australian government context, this is directly addressed by the FOCI assessment requirement — certain named providers may be exempt from a full FOCI assessment while other AI providers must undergo one before government entities may give them access to OFFICIAL information (unverified — confirm the current exemption list against PSPF Policy Advisory 001-2025). Open-source model weights from high-risk jurisdictions carry their own FOCI-equivalent considerations.

```
LLM ATTACK SURFACE MAP

  Attacker
     |
     v
[System Prompt]   <-- indirect injection via instructions
[User Prompt]     <-- direct prompt injection, jailbreaking
     |
     v
 [LLM Model]  <-- weights poisoning, model theft, evasion, FOCI/supply chain risk
     |
     +---> [RAG / Vector DB]  <-- embedding poisoning, data injection
     |
     +---> [Plugins / Tools]  <-- tool abuse, excessive agency
     |
     v
 [Output]  <-- improper output handling, misinformation, data leakage
     |
     v
 Downstream systems / users
```

## How It Works in Practice

**Australian Government entities** must follow a defined sequence before enabling any generative AI tool for government use:

```
PSPF AI TOOL APPROVAL SEQUENCE (Government Entities)

1. Is the provider on the current FOCI-exemption list?
   (unverified — confirm the list against PSPF Policy Advisory 001-2025)
   YES --> No additional FOCI assessment required (proceed to step 2)
   NO  --> Conduct FOCI assessment per PSPF Direction 001-2024

2. Is the tool banned by a PSPF Direction? (e.g. DeepSeek per Direction 001-2025)
   YES --> Stop. Tool is prohibited.
   NO  --> Proceed to step 3

3. Complete technology authorisation per PSPF Requirements 0086, 0087, 0088
   (ISM risk-based approach; authorise to highest classification to be processed)

4. Restrict access: OFFICIAL information only in public AI tools
   OFFICIAL: Sensitive and above --> prohibited from public AI tools

5. Deliver staff training per DTA guidance on responsible AI use
```

**SOCI-regulated entities** must include AI systems that process, store, or communicate information related to critical infrastructure assets within their CIRMP. Data storage and processing systems forming part of primary CI assets came into full scope on 4 April 2025. An LLM with access to operational data, network configuration, encryption keys, or SCADA-adjacent data is in scope. The CIRMP must document the hazard, controls, and residual risk.

**Policy before tools (all sectors).** The first intervention is an AI acceptable use policy that defines which tools employees may use, what data classifications may be input into each, and what the consequences of violation are. For government entities, this must align with the PSPF and DTA guidance. For private sector, this is the minimum baseline to avoid Samsung-style incidents.

**Approved tool registry.** Security teams maintain a list of sanctioned AI tools, each with a data processing agreement, a clear statement on whether query data is used for model training, and confirmed SOC 2 or equivalent assurance. For government entities, this list must only contain FOCI-cleared providers.

**Data loss prevention (DLP) integration.** DLP classifiers must be updated to recognise common LLM API endpoints and flag high-risk content types: source code, financial data, PII, classified data, and confidential documents. For government, classification labels are the primary trigger — OFFICIAL: Sensitive and above must be blocked from egressing to a public AI tool.

**Input and output validation.** Applications built on LLMs implement guardrails at the prompt level and output level. The ISM controls for LLM applications, added in the June 2024 update and referencing OWASP LLM Top 10, apply to systems developed or operated by government and government-adjacent organisations. Adversarial prompt mitigation is not optional for in-scope systems.

**Least privilege for agents.** Any LLM with tool access is given only the permissions necessary to perform its defined function. Tool access is reviewed with the same rigour as privileged access.

**Vector database security.** RAG vector stores are treated with the same access controls as primary databases. For SOCI entities, vector stores holding CI-relevant data must be reflected in the CIRMP.

**Red teaming.** Adversarial testing before deployment is required under the ISM for government systems and is best practice for all LLM applications. MITRE ATLAS provides the threat taxonomy. Garak, Promptfoo, and Mindgard support automated red teaming.

**Incident response and mandatory reporting.** Under the Cyber Security Act 2024, any entity in scope that makes a ransomware or cyber extortion payment — including payments arising from attacks on AI systems or through AI-enabled attacks — must report to ASD within 72 hours. There is no grace period and no minimum payment threshold. SOCI entities must separately report hazards affecting CI assets. Both reporting obligations apply concurrently. Playbooks must be updated and the clock must be drilled.

**Roles involved:** CISO or CSO (technology authorisation under PSPF, increased reporting obligations under PSPF 2025, accountability for AI use cases under the Responsible AI Policy), security architects (threat modelling), application security engineers (input/output controls), procurement (FOCI assessment, DPA review), legal and compliance (SOCI, Cyber Security Act, Privacy Act obligations), and the SOC (monitoring LLM API usage, mandatory reporting timelines).

## Common Mistakes and Weaknesses

**Failing to apply PSPF classification controls to AI tools.** Government employees and contractors routinely underestimate what counts as OFFICIAL: Sensitive. Meeting notes, contract details, personnel matters, draft policy, and budget figures are typically OFFICIAL: Sensitive or above. The DTA guidance is explicit: assume anything entered into a public generative AI tool could be made public.

**Treating FOCI as a one-time procurement check.** The FOCI risk does not disappear after initial assessment. Provider ownership structures change, data routing changes, and employees continuously adopt new AI tools outside formal procurement. Shadow AI is a FOCI risk as much as it is a data leakage risk.

**Not including AI systems in the CIRMP.** SOCI-regulated entities that deploy AI tools with access to operational data often fail to recognise these as data storage or processing systems relevant to their CI asset. The 2024 amendments made this explicit. Regulators moved to compliance audit activity from the 2024–25 period.

**Relying on the model's safety training as a security control.** Safety fine-tuning reduces harmful outputs under normal conditions but is not a security boundary. It can be bypassed through adversarial prompting. Security controls must sit in the application layer, not inside the model.

**Shadow AI with no visibility.** Organisations that ban AI tools without providing approved alternatives drive usage underground. In government, this creates PSPF compliance exposure on top of the data leakage risk. The NCA's 2024 report found only 48% of employees had received any AI-related security training.

**No DLP coverage for LLM endpoints.** Most DLP programmes were built for email, web uploads, and USB. Traffic to a cloud AI provider looks like HTTPS to a cloud service and passes most firewall rules without inspection.

**Vector databases left unsecured.** RAG deployments treat the vector store as a configuration detail rather than a data store. Unsecured vector databases are a common finding in AI security assessments, and for SOCI entities they represent an unmanaged hazard.

**Over-privileged agents.** LLM agents are given broad permissions to avoid friction during development. Those permissions are rarely reviewed before production deployment.

**No system prompt governance.** System prompts often contain business logic, API keys, access control rules, and proprietary instructions. System prompt leakage is a distinct category in the OWASP LLM Top 10 2025.

**Not preparing for the 72-hour ransomware reporting clock.** Many incident response plans predate the Cyber Security Act 2024 and do not account for the mandatory reporting obligation. The clock applies to any ransomware payment, including those arising from AI-enabled attacks. Entities that have not drilled this will be caught short.

## Connections to Other Domains

- **[Cyber Governance and Management (1)](01.%20Cyber%20Governance%20and%20Management.md):** AI acceptable use policy, governance of AI tool adoption, and board-level AI risk oversight all require a governance foundation. In Australia, the Policy for the Responsible Use of AI in Government v2.0 (December 2025) imposes mandatory AI governance obligations on Commonwealth entities. ISO/IEC 42001 is the AI-specific management system standard applicable to all sectors.

- **[Cyber Risk Management (2)](02.%20Cyber%20Risk%20Management.md):** LLM-specific risks — prompt injection, data leakage, model poisoning, FOCI exposure — must be entered into the enterprise risk register. For SOCI entities, AI risk must be reflected in the CIRMP. NIST AI RMF's MAP and MEASURE functions operationalise this for any organisation.

- **[Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance (3)](03.%20Legal,%20Regulatory,%20and%20Compliance.md):** Australian compliance obligations are now substantial: PSPF mandatory requirements for Commonwealth entities, SOCI CIRMP obligations, Cyber Security Act 2024 ransomware reporting, the Privacy Act 1988 Notifiable Data Breach scheme (applies to AI-caused breaches), and APRA CPS 234 for regulated financial entities. The EU AI Act's GPAI obligations (from August 2025) apply to Australian organisations deploying AI in the EU.

- **[Privacy and Data Protection (4)](04.%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Protection.md):** LLMs are major vectors for accidental PII disclosure. Australia's Privacy Act 1988 Notifiable Data Breach scheme applies where an AI tool causes an eligible data breach. The OAIC has published guidance on AI and privacy. Data minimisation and purpose limitation are directly relevant to any LLM deployment.

- **[Vendor Management (5)](05.%20Vendor%20Management.md):** For Australian Government entities, the FOCI assessment process is mandatory for non-approved AI providers. All AI providers require enhanced due diligence: DPAs, training data usage terms, breach notification obligations, and model provenance. The supply chain risk extends to open-source model weights and third-party plugins.

- **[Asset and Change Management (6)](06.%20Asset%20and%20Change%20Management.md):** LLM models, fine-tuned variants, system prompts, and vector databases are assets that must be inventoried. PSPF 2025 introduced a mandatory technology asset stocktake for internet-facing systems. Changes to model versions or retrieval corpora need change management because they can silently alter application behaviour.

- **[Identity and Access Management (8)](08.%20Identity%20and%20Access%20Management.md):** Agent access to enterprise systems must follow least-privilege principles. API keys for LLM providers need rotation and vaulting. Service account identities for LLM integrations need the same lifecycle management as human identities.

- **[Cyber Testing (10)](10.%20Cyber%20Testing.md):** Red teaming LLM applications is a distinct discipline. MITRE ATLAS provides the threat taxonomy. The ISM requires evaluation and mitigation of adversarial user prompts for government systems. Tools such as Garak, Promptfoo, and Mindgard support structured test execution.

- **[Threat Intelligence (11)](11.%20Threat%20Intelligence.md):** MITRE ATLAS documents real-world case studies of attacks against ML systems. PSPF Directions — such as the DeepSeek ban — are government threat intelligence being operationalised into mandatory compliance action. Tracking jailbreak techniques and adversarial ML research is part of maintaining current LLM defences.

- **[Security Operations and Monitoring (12)](12.%20Security%20Operations%20and%20Monitoring.md):** LLM API usage must be logged and monitored. Anomalies in query volume, unusual input patterns, unexpected output content, and unanticipated access to vector databases are all indicators worth investigating. SIEM rules need extending for LLM-specific events.

- **[Incident Management and Business Continuity Planning (13)](13.%20Incident%20Management%20and%20Business%20Continuity%20Planning.md):** Incident response playbooks need LLM-specific scenarios. The Cyber Security Act 2024 mandates a 72-hour reporting window for ransomware payments — the clock is the same regardless of attack vector. SOCI entities have separate hazard reporting obligations. Both must be in the playbook and drilled.

- **[Cloud Management (14)](14.%20Cloud%20Management.md):** Most LLM integrations are cloud-delivered. For government entities, all cloud services must be hosted on HCF-certified providers or go through the relevant assurance process. PSPF 2025 introduced an upgraded Australian Government Gateway Security Standard (released 24 July 2025).

- **[Software Development Security (15)](15.%20Software%20Development%20Security.md):** The ISM's AI application development controls (added June 2024) reference OWASP LLM Top 10 directly. Improper output handling — passing raw LLM output to interpreters or command shells — is a critical vulnerability class. Any application calling an LLM API needs secure coding practices around prompt construction and output handling.

- **[DevOps and DevSecOps (16)](16.%20DevOps%20and%20DevSecOps.md):** LLM security testing must be embedded in CI/CD pipelines. Model cards, AIBOMs, and red team results should be deployment gates. For government systems, the ISM's system authorisation process, including IRAP assessment, applies to LLM-integrated systems.

- **[Cyber Architecture (17)](17.%20Cyber%20Architecture.md):** Threat modelling LLM integrations requires defining trust boundaries between the model, retrieval layer, tools, and users. PSPF 2025's Zero Trust principles apply to AI system architecture as much as traditional system architecture. Blast radius reduction for agentic architectures and network egress controls for AI traffic are architectural decisions, not operational ones.

- **[Cyber Culture and Education (18)](18.%20Cyber%20Culture%20and%20Education.md):** The Samsung incident was not a technical failure. The DTA's Staff Guidance on Public Generative AI states: assume anything you enter into a public generative AI tool could be made public. For Australian Government staff, the AI in Government Fundamentals course is now an expected baseline. For all sectors, AI-specific security awareness is as important as phishing awareness.
